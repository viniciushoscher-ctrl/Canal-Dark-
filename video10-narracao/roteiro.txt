### 00_hook
Nineteen thirty-five. A frail, white-haired old man in a dark suit walks into a federal courtroom in Washington. The government, led by President Franklin Roosevelt, is accusing him of cheating on his taxes. Reporters crowd the benches.

He is eighty years old, and for a decade he was the most powerful finance minister in the world. Presidents asked his advice. Congress passed his tax cuts. A famous saying claimed that three presidents served under him.

His name is Andrew Mellon. And the strangest part of his story is what he did with his fortune, because it ended up in a building that millions of people walk through every year without knowing who paid for it.

### 01_cap1
Andrew William Mellon was born on March twenty-fourth, eighteen fifty-five, in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. His father, Thomas Mellon, was a judge and a banker who had grown up poor in Ireland and America, and who believed in thrift and hard work.

Andrew was shy, small, and quiet. He hated speeches and preferred numbers. At seventeen, he was already working in his father's bank, and his father soon noticed that the boy had a rare eye for risk.

When Andrew was twenty-seven, Thomas made a surprising decision. He handed over the bank to his son. The old judge wrote that the young man was better at the business than he had ever been.

It was the start of a fortune, and it came from one idea: in Pittsburgh, the best investments were not in banks. They were in the factories that the banks financed.

### 02_cap2
Pittsburgh was a smoky, booming city of steel and coal. Mellon had a simple method. He loaned money to promising young inventors and businessmen, and in exchange, he took a share of their companies.

His first big bet was a young chemist named Charles Martin Hall, who had found a cheap way to make aluminum. Mellon financed the company, which became Alcoa. For years, it had almost no competition, and it made the Mellons staggeringly rich.

Then came oil. In nineteen-oh-one, a gusher in Texas called Spindletop changed the industry. Mellon backed the drillers, and their company grew into Gulf Oil.

He also funded Carborundum, a company that made abrasives, and Koppers, a coke producer. At the same time, he was close friends with the steel baron Henry Clay Frick. Together, the two men built much of industrial Pittsburgh.

By the nineteen-tens, Mellon was one of the three richest men in the country, behind only Rockefeller and Ford.

Mellon's rule was always the same. He did not run the companies he financed. He picked the best managers he could find, gave them freedom, and watched the accounts. He preferred to be a silent partner, so that no one could say the factory belonged to a banker.

Visitors found him cold. He rarely smiled, spoke in a low, hesitant voice, and wore the same plain dark suit every day. But in his office, men who had met kings said that his silence made them nervous.

### 03_cap3
But Mellon's private life was cold. In eighteen ninety-nine, at the age of forty-four, he married a young Englishwoman named Nora McMullen. They had two children, Ailsa and Paul.

The marriage fell apart. Nora felt ignored. Andrew spent his days at the bank and his evenings alone with his art collection. In nineteen twelve, she asked for a divorce, and the scandal was enormous. Newspapers printed every detail, and Mellon, who valued privacy above everything, was humiliated.

He won custody of his children, but at a price. He never remarried. He retreated into work, into silence, and into the paintings he was beginning to buy.

It was the one passion that he allowed himself. And it would eventually shape his place in history.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen twenty-one, newly elected President Warren Harding asked Mellon to become Secretary of the Treasury. Mellon was sixty-six, and he did not want the job. But he accepted, with one condition: he would remain a businessman at heart.

America was in debt after the First World War, and taxes on the rich were enormous. Mellon proposed a radical plan. He argued that lower tax rates would encourage people to invest, and in the end the government would collect more money, not less.

Congress listened. Over the nineteen twenties, the top income tax rate fell from seventy-three percent to twenty-five percent. At the same time, Mellon used the budget surplus to pay down the national debt, reducing it by about a third.

The economy boomed. The nineteen twenties roared. Mellon became the most admired Treasury Secretary in memory. Magazines put him on their covers, and people said that three presidents served under him: Harding, Coolidge, and Hoover.

His tax plan was never as simple as people remember. Critics said the cuts helped the rich more than anyone else. Defenders said that by closing loopholes and lowering rates, he made the rich pay more in total. Economists still argue about it.

What nobody doubted was his talent as an administrator. He cut the federal budget, simplified the Treasury, and kept the books balanced. He once joked that the best government was the one that stayed out of sight.

### 05_cap5
Then the crash came. In October nineteen twenty-nine, the stock market collapsed, and the Great Depression began. Mellon, the man who had presided over the boom, was now blamed for the bust.

Herbert Hoover later claimed that Mellon had advised him to let the crisis run its course, to liquidate labor, liquidate stocks, and liquidate farmers. Mellon denied saying it, but the phrase stuck, and his reputation collapsed.

By nineteen thirty-two, Congress was investigating him, and Hoover quietly moved him out of the Treasury, sending him to London as ambassador. Mellon accepted. He was seventy-seven, tired, and increasingly isolated.

Yet he still had one project left, the one he cared about most. While governments were struggling and banks were failing, Andrew Mellon was quietly buying the greatest art collection in America.

In London, he found a different kind of life. He walked in the parks, visited galleries, and attended dinners with kings and prime ministers. For the first time, the shy banker seemed almost at ease. He even told friends that he wished he had spent more of his life among paintings.

### 06_cap6
In nineteen thirty and thirty-one, Mellon secretly bought some of the finest paintings in the world from the Soviet government, which needed cash. They came from the Hermitage Museum in Leningrad, and they included works by Raphael, Titian, and Rembrandt. The price was about seven million dollars.

Because he was still Treasury Secretary, the purchase caused a scandal. But Mellon had a plan. He wanted to give the whole collection, and the money to house it, to the American people. He offered to build a national gallery in Washington, with no strings attached.

He also bought paintings by Vermeer, by Van Dyck, and by Jan van Eyck. His friends said that when he stood in front of a masterpiece, the cold banker disappeared. He would stare at the same painting for an hour, silent, as if he were reading a ledger of beauty.

Then Roosevelt's government struck back. In nineteen thirty-four, the Justice Department charged him with tax evasion. It was a political trial, and a federal tribunal heard the case.

In nineteen thirty-seven, Mellon was cleared, but he never learned the verdict. He died on August twenty-sixth, nineteen thirty-seven, at the age of eighty-two.

### 07_lesson
Four years later, in nineteen forty-one, the National Gallery of Art opened in Washington. It held the paintings he had bought, a new marble building he had paid for, and an endowment to keep it running. Admission was free, and it still is.

Mellon's story has two lessons. The first is that the best investor is the one who funds others. He built Alcoa and Gulf Oil by betting on people who had ideas but no money.

The second is that a fortune takes on the meaning you give it. Mellon was not a warm man, and he was not loved. But for the last decade of his life, he gave away his collection so that anyone, rich or poor, could walk in and see a Rembrandt for free.
