### 00_hook
October, eighteen seventy-one. A great fire sweeps through Chicago for three days, and burns almost the entire city. A department store owner stands in the street and watches his building burn. Millions of dollars of merchandise are gone. Nearly everything is lost, except one thing: his reputation.

Within days, he is back in business. He rents an old streetcar barn on the edge of town, hangs a sign, and starts selling again. Customers line up. They remember how he treated them. The store they trusted, the store that took back any item without question, is open once more.

His name is Marshall Field. He was a farm boy from Massachusetts who arrived in Chicago with almost nothing. He turned a dry goods store into the most famous department store in America. And he did it with one simple idea: give the lady what she wants. This is the story of the merchant prince of Chicago.

### 01_cap1
Marshall Field was born on August eighteenth, eighteen thirty-four, in Conway, Massachusetts. His family were farmers, who had come to New England generations before. The farm was poor and rocky, and young Marshall disliked the work. He wanted to be a merchant.

His mother, Fidelia, was a woman of strong character, and she encouraged him to learn. At seventeen, he left home and found a job as a clerk in a dry goods store in Pittsfield, Massachusetts. He worked from early morning to late at night, six days a week, for a small salary. He was quiet, disciplined, and observant. He studied how customers behaved, and what made them buy. He lived on almost nothing, and he saved.

The lesson of those years stayed with him. Honesty is the best way to keep a customer, and a clerk who tells the truth about the goods will always have buyers.

At twenty-one, hearing that the Midwest offered opportunity, he took a train west. He arrived in Chicago in eighteen fifty-six with a few dollars in his pocket and a letter of introduction.

### 02_cap2
Chicago was a booming, muddy frontier city. Railroads were bringing immigrants and farmers from all directions. Field got a job at a wholesale dry goods firm called Cooley, Wadsworth and Company. He started at the bottom, selling goods to country merchants. He was so successful that within four years he became a partner.

He was also careful with a dollar. As a young clerk, he wore the same suit for years. In his first year in Chicago, he lived in a boardinghouse, and sent money home to his family. He later said that thrift was the foundation of every business he ever saw.

Field worked harder than anyone. He was at his desk before the doors opened, and he stayed after they closed. He never drank. A coworker later said that he had no hobbies except business. By the end of the eighteen sixties, he had become a wealthy partner in the firm.

In eighteen sixty-five, the firm became Field, Palmer and Leiter, when the merchant Potter Palmer, who owned the famous store on Lake Street, joined with Field and a young colleague, Levi Leiter. Palmer wanted to focus on real estate. In eighteen sixty-seven, he sold his share to the others, and the company became Field, Leiter and Company.

### 03_cap3
The store, on State Street, became known for its values. Field wanted to build the best retail store in America. He insisted on a few principles that were radical at the time.

He set a fixed price for every item, so customers did not have to bargain. He allowed exchanges and refunds. He demanded honesty, and he told his clerks never to push a sale. His rule, repeated for generations, was, give the lady what she wants. And he never wavered from it.

At the time, most stores treated customers with suspicion. Field treated them as guests. He built a large, bright, clean store, with fine goods, and good lighting. He trained his staff to be courteous and helpful. Women, who made up most shoppers, loved it.

Field and Leiter grew rapidly. By eighteen seventy-one, the firm had sales of millions of dollars a year, and the building on State Street was one of the finest in the city, a marble palace. Then came the fire.

### 04_cap4
On the night of October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, a fire started in a barn on the southwest side of Chicago, and spread through the dry wooden city. The Great Chicago Fire raged for three days. It killed about three hundred people, and left a hundred thousand homeless.

Field and Leiter saved some of their stock by hauling it by wagon to a nearby street. But the store, a fine marble building, burned to the ground. Most of the inventory was lost, though the firm had insurance.

Within days, they reopened in a rented building, a streetcar barn on Twentieth Street. Customers came by the hundreds, because they wanted to support the firm. When the owners rebuilt on State Street in eighteen seventy-three, a second fire in eighteen seventy-seven burned them out once more. Each time, Field rebuilt.

He did not complain, and he did not delay. His ability to recover became a part of the legend. Chicago itself rose from the ashes, and Field was one of the men who rebuilt it.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen eighty-one, Field bought out Leiter, who was a difficult partner and a gambler. The company was renamed Marshall Field and Company. Field was now the sole owner, at forty-seven.

He expanded the business into a true department store. It had floors devoted to clothing, furniture, books, china, and toys. It had a tea room, a bargain basement, a bridal registry, and a personal shopper service. Customers could buy almost anything. In eighteen ninety-three, during the World's Columbian Exposition, millions of visitors came to Chicago, and many shopped at Field's.

The Great Clock on the corner of State and Washington became a landmark, and people said, meet me under the clock. Visitors from across the Midwest took trains to Chicago just to shop at Field's. A shopper could spend a whole day there, eat lunch, and buy a wedding dress.

The store made a fortune. By the end of the century, Field was one of the richest men in America. His wealth was estimated at over a hundred million dollars. He was also a major investor in railroads, and the founder of the Field Museum of Natural History, which he supported with a large gift.

### 06_cap6
But Field's private life was lonely. He worked constantly, and his wife, Nannie, withdrew from society. Nannie died in eighteen ninety-six, and in nineteen oh five he married again. That same year, his only son, Marshall Junior, died from an accidental gunshot.

In the last years of his life, he tried to give back. He donated land for Fort Sheridan, a military post near Chicago, and ten acres of land to the University of Chicago, for its campus. He liked to say that he made his money in Chicago, and that he owed the city.

Field was a stern man. He was strict with his employees, but he also paid fair wages, and he supported his workers' education. During the labor unrest of the eighteen eighties, he took the side of the business owners, and the public was divided. His critics called him cold and unyielding.

He died on January sixteenth, nineteen oh six, at the age of seventy-one, after catching a cold at a golf game. He was the richest man in Chicago, and left more than one hundred million dollars. Most of it went to his grandsons. The Field Museum received eight million dollars.

### 07_cap7
The store lived on long after him. Marshall Field and Company moved into a huge building on State Street, where its clocks, its Christmas windows, and its Walnut Room restaurant became part of the life of the city. Generations of Chicago families made it a holiday tradition to see the windows and eat lunch under the Tiffany mosaic ceiling.

The company invented the modern department store experience, with the personal shopper, the bridal registry, and the sales rule that the customer can return anything. Its ideas were copied by stores around the world.

In two thousand six, the Marshall Field name disappeared, and the stores were renamed Macy's. Many Chicagoans protested. But the idea survives. Every time a store accepts a return without a question, or a clerk bends over backward to help a stranger, it follows the old rule of a farm boy from Massachusetts.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Marshall Field?

The first lesson is that trust is the strongest asset in business. When his store burned, customers still came, because they knew he would keep his word. Reputation survived the flames.

The second lesson is that service is a strategy. He didn't sell the cheapest goods. He sold the best experience, and treated every customer as an honored guest. In a world of competitors, kindness is a very hard thing to copy.

The third lesson is discipline. He lived thrifty, worked more than anyone, and reinvested for decades. Before he was rich, he behaved like a man who would become rich.

And the last is a warning. He gave all his hours to the store and left little for his family. A great business can be built at a heavy personal cost.
