### 00_hook
October, eighteen seventy-one. A great fire sweeps through Chicago for three days, and burns almost the entire city. A department store owner stands in the street and watches his building collapse. Three million dollars of merchandise are gone. Nearly everything is lost, except one thing: his reputation.

Within weeks, he is back in business. He rents a barn and some old horse stables, hangs a sign, and starts selling again. Customers line up. They remember how he treated them. The store they trusted, the store that took back any item without question, is open once more.

His name is Marshall Field. He was a farm boy from Massachusetts who arrived in Chicago with almost nothing. He turned a dry goods store into the most famous department store in America. And he did it with one simple idea: the customer is always right. This is the story of the merchant prince of Chicago.

### 01_cap1
Marshall Field was born on August eighteenth, eighteen thirty-four, in Conway, Massachusetts. His family were farmers, who had come to New England generations before. The farm was poor, and young Marshall disliked it. He wanted to be a merchant.

At seventeen, he left home and found a job as a clerk in a dry goods store in Pittsfield, Massachusetts. He worked twelve hours a day, six days a week, for four hundred dollars a year. He was quiet, disciplined, and observant. He studied how customers behaved, and what made them buy. He saved money, living on a tiny budget.

Field once wrote that he was determined to save five hundred dollars. At twenty-one, hearing that the Midwest offered opportunity, he took a train west. He arrived in Chicago in eighteen fifty-six with a few dollars in his pocket.

### 02_cap2
Chicago was a booming, muddy frontier city. Railroads were bringing immigrants and farmers from all directions. Field got a job at a wholesale dry goods firm called Cooley, Wadsworth and Company. He started at the bottom, selling goods to country merchants. He was so successful that within four years he became a partner.

He was also careful with a dollar. As a young clerk, he wore the same suit for years. In his first year in Chicago, he lived in a boardinghouse, and sent money home to his family. He later said that thrift was the foundation of every business he ever saw.

Field worked harder than anyone. He was at his desk before the doors opened, and he stayed after they closed. He never drank. He was strict about money. A coworker later said that he had no hobbies except business.

In eighteen sixty-five, he met a rising young merchant named Potter Palmer, who owned a famous dry goods store on Lake Street. Palmer wanted to focus on real estate. He offered to sell the business to Field and a colleague, Levi Leiter. In eighteen sixty-five, Field, Leiter and Company was born.

### 03_cap3
The store, on State Street, became known for its values. Field wanted to build the best retail store in America. He insisted on a few principles that were radical at the time.

He set a fixed price for every item, so customers did not have to bargain. He allowed exchanges and refunds. He demanded honesty, and he told his clerks never to push a sale. His rule, repeated for generations, was, give the lady what she wants. And he never wavered from it.

At the time, most stores treated customers with suspicion. Field treated them as guests. He built a large, bright, clean store, with fine goods, and good lighting. He trained his staff to be courteous and helpful. Women, who made up most shoppers, loved it.

Field and Leiter grew rapidly. By eighteen seventy-one, the firm had sales of millions of dollars a year, and the building on State Street was one of the finest in the city. Then came the fire.

### 04_cap4
On the night of October eighth, eighteen seventy-one, a fire started in a barn on the southwest side of Chicago, and spread through the dry wooden city. The Great Chicago Fire raged for three days. It killed about three hundred people, and left a hundred thousand homeless.

Field and Leiter saved some stock by hauling it by wagon to a nearby street. But the store, a fine marble building, burned. Most of the inventory was lost, though the firm had insurance.

Within days, they reopened in a rented building, a streetcar barn on Twentieth Street. Customers came by the hundreds, because they wanted to support the firm. When the owners rebuilt on State Street in eighteen seventy-three, a second fire in eighteen seventy-seven burned them out once more. Each time, Field rebuilt.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen eighty-one, Field bought out Leiter, who was a difficult partner and a gambler. The company was renamed Marshall Field and Company. Field was now the sole owner, at forty-seven.

He expanded the business into a true department store. It had floors devoted to clothing, furniture, books, china, and toys. It had a tea room, a bargain basement, a bridal registry, and a personal shopper service. Customers could buy almost anything. In eighteen ninety-three, during the World's Columbian Exposition, millions of visitors came to Chicago, and many shopped at Field's.

The Walnut Room restaurant, the Great Clock on the corner, and the Tiffany glass mosaic ceiling became landmarks. Visitors from across the Midwest took trains to Chicago just to shop at Field's. A shopper could spend a whole day there, eat lunch, have her hair done, and buy a wedding dress.

The store made a fortune. In the late eighteen nineties, Field was one of the richest men in America. His wealth was estimated at over a hundred and twenty million dollars. He was also a major investor in railroads, and the founder of Chicago's Field Museum of Natural History.

### 06_cap6
But Field's private life was lonely. He worked constantly, and his wife, Nannie, withdrew from society. His wife Nannie died in eighteen ninety-six, and in nineteen-oh-five he married again. That same year, his only son, Marshall Junior, died from an accidental gunshot.

In the last years of his life, he tried to give back. He donated land for Fort Sheridan, a military post, and money for the University of Chicago, where he gave the land for its campus. He liked to say that he made his money in Chicago, and that he owed the city.

Field was a stern man. He was strict with his employees. But he also paid fair wages, and he supported his workers' education. During the Haymarket affair in eighteen eighty-six, he donated land to build a military post near Chicago, and the public was divided.

He died on January sixteenth, nineteen-oh-six, at age seventy-one, after catching a cold at a golf game. He was the richest man in Chicago, and left about a hundred and forty million dollars. Most of it went to his grandsons. The museum received eight million dollars.

The store continued for more than a century, famous for its clocks, its Christmas windows, and its Frango mints. In two thousand six, it was renamed Macy's.

### 07_lesson
Marshall Field's story holds two lessons. The first is that trust is the strongest asset in business. When his store burned, customers still came, because they knew he would keep his word.

The second is that service is a strategy. He didn't sell the cheapest goods. He sold the best experience, and treated every customer as an honored guest. In a world of competitors, kindness is a very hard thing to copy.
