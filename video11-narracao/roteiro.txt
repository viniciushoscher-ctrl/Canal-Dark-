### 00_hook
July sixth, eighteen ninety-two. Along the Monongahela River near Pittsburgh, three hundred armed men sail up the water on two barges, in the middle of the night. Waiting for them on the riverbank are thousands of steelworkers, women, and children. Somebody fires a shot. By the end of the day, at least nine people are dead.

The man who ordered this was not a general. He was a quiet, bearded businessman who was watching from an office miles away, and who would later be nearly killed by an assassin and still refuse to leave his desk.

His name is Henry Clay Frick. He built the biggest coke empire on earth, helped create U.S. Steel, and used his fortune to build the most beautiful mansion on Fifth Avenue. And this is the story of how one man became both the most feared and the most admired industrialist in America.

### 01_cap1
Henry Clay Frick was born on December nineteenth, eighteen forty-nine, in a stone farmhouse in West Overton, Pennsylvania. His grandfather owned a famous whiskey distillery, and the family was comfortable, but never rich.

Henry was a serious boy. He read constantly, and at sixteen he got a job as a clerk in a store. By nineteen, he was working as a bookkeeper at his grandfather's distillery. He noticed something that most people missed. Just outside the farm lay the richest coal fields in America.

Coal, in the eighteen seventies, was just another rock. But Frick realized that when coal is baked, it turns into coke, a fuel with the heat needed to make steel. And the new steel mills of Pittsburgh needed coke in huge amounts.

At twenty-one, he borrowed money from his family and from Judge Thomas Mellon, the banker you may know from another file. He bought a handful of coke ovens, and started to buy more.

### 02_cap2
Frick's method was simple and ruthless. When the market crashed in the panic of eighteen seventy-three, other coke producers went bankrupt. Frick borrowed more money and bought their ovens at a fraction of the price.

By thirty, he was a millionaire. By thirty-five, his company, H. C. Frick Coke Company, controlled about eighty percent of the coke in the Connellsville region. Almost every steel mill in Pittsburgh bought fuel from him.

That brought him to the attention of the biggest steel maker in the world, Andrew Carnegie. Carnegie bought a share of Frick's company, and then made him the chairman of Carnegie Steel. The two men looked like a perfect team. Carnegie had the vision, and Frick had the discipline.

Under Frick, Carnegie's mills became more efficient, and more profitable than any steel company in history. He cut costs everywhere. He pushed the workers harder, and he forced the managers to account for every ton. Profits rose from about three million dollars a year to more than forty million.

But all of this had a human price. The steelworkers worked twelve-hour shifts, seven days a week, in heat that could kill. And in the summer of eighteen ninety-two, Frick decided to break the union.

### 03_cap3
The union was called the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, and it was the strongest labor union in the country. Its contract at the Homestead mill was about to expire, and Frick wanted to cut wages and end the union for good.

He built a three-mile fence around the plant, topped with barbed wire, and he hired three hundred armed guards from the Pinkerton Detective Agency. When the workers refused his terms, he locked them out.

On the morning of July sixth, the Pinkertons floated up the river on barges. Thousands of workers and their families met them on the bank. A shot rang out, and a gunfight began that lasted all day. Seven workers and three guards died. In the end, the Pinkertons surrendered, and the workers marched them through town in disgrace.

Frick did not back down. He asked the governor to send eight thousand state militia soldiers, and the soldiers took control of the plant. The strike dragged on for months. In the end, the union was crushed, and wages were cut. It would take decades before steelworkers would organize again.

### 04_cap4
Then came the strangest event of Frick's life. On July twenty-third, a young anarchist named Alexander Berkman walked into Frick's office in Pittsburgh. He had no connection to the strike, but he thought that killing Frick would start a revolution.

Berkman shot Frick twice, and then stabbed him with a dagger. Frick was bleeding on the floor, but he fought back, and with the help of his staff, they pinned the attacker down.

When doctors arrived, Frick refused to be carried away. He insisted on finishing his work. He signed the letters on his desk first. Only after the paperwork was done did he allow the doctors to treat him. Within a week, he was back in the office.

The public reaction was remarkable. Frick had been hated as a tyrant, but after the attack, newspapers praised his courage. A man who had sent soldiers against his own workers suddenly looked like a hero.

Meanwhile, his friendship with Andrew Carnegie was falling apart. They argued over coal prices, then over the future of the company, and then over the Homestead strike itself. Carnegie even wrote letters blaming Frick for the violence, which Frick never forgave.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen ninety-nine, the two men went to court. Carnegie tried to force Frick out of the company. The fight became so bitter that Frick reportedly told a mutual friend that he would meet Carnegie in hell, and that the devil would have to separate them.

Frick left Carnegie Steel, but he did not leave the steel business. He had already become one of the largest investors in the industry. When the banker J. P. Morgan began to build a giant steel trust in nineteen-oh-one, Frick was one of the key men behind the scenes. He helped to negotiate the sale of Carnegie Steel, a deal worth more than four hundred million dollars, and the birth of United States Steel.

It was the largest company in the world at the time, worth more than a billion dollars. Frick joined its board. He also invested heavily in railroads, in banks, in coal, and in Pittsburgh real estate. By now, he was worth about two hundred million dollars, which is billions today.

But as his wealth grew, so did his isolation. His daughter Helen was his comfort. His first child, Martha, died young, and his son Henry Junior died in infancy. Frick rarely talked about it, but those who knew him said that he never really recovered.

### 06_cap6
In nineteen-oh-five, Frick moved to New York City. He rented a mansion for a few years from the Vanderbilt family, and in nineteen thirteen he built his own on Fifth Avenue, at Seventieth Street. It was designed as a palace for art.

Frick had started collecting paintings in the eighteen eighties, and by now he owned masterpieces by Vermeer, Rembrandt, Titian, Velázquez, Goya, and Holbein. His close friend Andrew Mellon was collecting too, and the two men visited galleries together.

He died on December second, nineteen nineteen, at the age of sixty-nine, of a heart attack. His will was a surprise. He left his mansion, his entire collection, and fifteen million dollars to the public, so that it could become a museum after his daughter's death.

Helen lived for many more years. In nineteen thirty-five, after her death, the house opened to the public as The Frick Collection. It is still open today, with the same paintings hanging in the same rooms.

### 07_lesson
So what does the Frick story teach us? The first lesson is that the greatest opportunities are often sitting right next to you. Frick did not discover a new invention. He simply understood the value of a rock that everybody else ignored.

The second is that wealth can never settle a conscience. Frick built a fortune on the work of men he refused to listen to, and then spent his last years surrounded by silence and paintings. At the end, he chose to give it all away.
