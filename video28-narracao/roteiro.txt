### 00_hook
January, eighteen ninety-three. A one-eyed man in his fifties sits in a railroad car on the frozen plains of North Dakota, watching a blizzard rage outside the window. Behind him stretches a single track. The company that built it has just finished a line across the northern United States, from the Great Lakes to the Pacific Ocean. And it did it without a single acre of free land, and without a single dollar from the government.

That very summer, while railroad after railroad collapses in the Panic of eighteen ninety-three, his company will be the only major transcontinental line that never goes bankrupt. Competitors beg him for secrets. Bankers study his books. Newspapers call him the Empire Builder.

His name is James J. Hill. He arrived in America as a poor Canadian, lost an eye as a boy, and spent forty years building the Great Northern Railway. And this is the story of the man who built a railroad across a wilderness, and made it profitable.

### 01_cap1
James Jerome Hill was born on September sixteenth, eighteen thirty-eight, near Rockwood, Ontario, in Canada. His parents were Scottish and Irish farmers. When he was nine years old, he was playing with a bow and arrow with his brother. An arrow struck him in the eye, and he lost the sight in his right eye forever.

His father died when James was fourteen. The boy had to leave school to support his family, and he worked as a clerk in a village store. He read every book he could find. He had one dream, to see the world. At seventeen, he left Canada, and boarded a steamboat down the Mississippi, to the small, rough frontier town of St. Paul, Minnesota.

He arrived with almost nothing. He found work as a shipping clerk on the riverfront, loading steamboats. Within a few years, he understood the economy of the Northwest better than anyone around him. He saw that transport would be the key.

### 02_cap2
He was also fierce about work. He rose before dawn, he read reports at the dinner table, and he rarely took vacations. People said that Hill had a memory so sharp that he could recite the freight rate of every station on his line.

Hill worked hard. He studied the river trade, the fur trade, and the new coal business. He started a small company that supplied coal to the town's homes, and made money from fuel. He also began to buy shares in steamboat lines on the Red River, a waterway that ran north into Canada.

In the eighteen seventies, he saw an opportunity. A bankrupt railroad, the St. Paul and Pacific, was in trouble. It had been started to connect St. Paul to the Canadian border, but its builders had run out of money, and the tracks were unfinished. With three partners, including a Canadian banker named George Stephen, Hill bought it in eighteen seventy-eight for a few million dollars, mostly borrowed.

It was a huge risk. The railroad was in ruins. But Hill understood something that others did not. A railroad is worth only what its customers produce. So he started to fill the empty plains with farmers.

### 03_cap3
Hill brought thousands of immigrants from Scandinavia and Germany to Minnesota and Dakota, offering them cheap land and low shipping rates. He sent agents to Europe to recruit farmers. He distributed free seed, he sponsored experimental farms, and he imported purebred cattle. He wanted the farmers to prosper, because his trains would carry their wheat.

He wrote that a railroad should build the country around it. The land grants of other railroads were often sold to speculators. Hill sold his land to farmers, and he wanted them to succeed.

The railroad grew quickly. By the eighteen eighties, it had become the St. Paul, Minneapolis and Manitoba Railway. And Hill was a harsh manager, who rode over the lines in a private car, inspecting every bridge, every water tank, and every ledger. He kept costs low, ran efficient trains, and hated waste. He lived to ride the rails.

### 04_cap4
Hill also surprised bankers with his rule of thumb. A railroad, he said, should be built for the traffic that will come in twenty years, not the traffic of today. He built the line wide, strong, and straight, so that it could carry more than its first customers needed.

In the eighteen eighties, Hill saw an even larger prize. The Pacific coast was growing, and the Northwest had huge supplies of timber, wheat, and iron ore. He wanted a railroad that went all the way to the Pacific. Other transcontinental railroads had received enormous land grants and government loans. Hill decided to build without a cent.

In eighteen ninety, he renamed his company the Great Northern Railway. Between eighteen ninety and eighteen ninety-three, his workers pushed the line across the plains of Montana, over the Rocky Mountains, and through the Cascades. His chief engineer, John Stevens, found a pass in the mountains, which became known as Stevens Pass.

He kept grades low, because he knew that a locomotive hauling a long freight train would save fuel. He used the best steel, and the best ties. He built strong bridges. He insisted that the Great Northern be the most efficient railroad in America, and one that could survive a crisis.

### 05_cap5
The Great Northern reached Seattle on January sixth, eighteen ninety-three. The Panic of eighteen ninety-three followed that spring, and four major railroads went bankrupt, including Northern Pacific, the Union Pacific, and the Santa Fe. The Great Northern survived. Its low costs and loyal farmers kept it profitable.

Hill expanded abroad. He opened steamship lines across the Pacific, to carry American flour and cotton to Asia, and to bring tea and silk back. He built huge ships, the largest in the world at the time, but they were not a success.

Then came the big fight. In nineteen-oh-one, Hill, with J. P. Morgan, purchased the Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad, to secure a route into Chicago. But a rival, E. H. Harriman, tried to seize control of Northern Pacific, a crucial partner. The struggle triggered the Panic of nineteen-oh-one, when the stock of Northern Pacific rose to a thousand dollars a share, and Wall Street crashed. In the end, Hill and Morgan created the Northern Securities Company, to combine their lines.

### 06_cap6
But the government objected. President Theodore Roosevelt ordered an antitrust suit against Northern Securities. In nineteen-oh-four, the Supreme Court ruled that the company was illegal and had to be broken up. It was a landmark in antitrust law, and a blow to Hill.

Hill himself was rarely bitter. He believed that his railroad had benefited the country. He told reporters that the Northwest would have remained a wilderness without it, and that the farmers who settled along his line were his greatest asset.

Hill, then sixty-six, stepped back. He retired as president of the Great Northern in nineteen-oh-seven, becoming chairman. He spent his last years in his mansion in St. Paul, which had thirty-six rooms. He collected art, and he gave away money to charity, including a library in St. Paul and a Catholic seminary.

He died on May twenty-ninth, nineteen sixteen, at age seventy-seven, with a fortune of around sixty-three million dollars. At the hour of his funeral, every train on the Great Northern stopped for five minutes.

### 07_lesson
James J. Hill's story holds two lessons. The first is that building something real beats chasing subsidies. While rivals borrowed from Washington, Hill built a railroad from the ground up, and it was the only one that survived.

The second is that the success of your customer is your success. Hill helped farmers grow, because his trains depended on their crops. When you invest in the people who feed your business, your business endures.
