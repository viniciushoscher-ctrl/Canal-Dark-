### 00_hook
Nineteen twenty-six. A tire maker from Ohio signs a lease for one million acres of West Africa. The rent is six cents an acre, and the lease runs for ninety-nine years. On that land, he plans to plant rubber trees, as far as the eye can see.

Why would a businessman want an African jungle? Because a few years earlier, the British had controlled most of the world's rubber, and they had raised the price more than threefold. Every American car needed tires. Every tire needed rubber. And the man who owned the rubber could name his price.

This tire maker refused to be at anyone's mercy. He was a farm boy with little schooling, who started his company with a few thousand dollars, in a town full of bigger rivals. He became friends with Henry Ford and Thomas Edison. And his name is still on the tires of millions of cars today.

This is the story of Harvey Firestone, the man who put America on rubber.

### 01_cap1
Harvey Samuel Firestone was born on December twentieth, eighteen sixty-eight, on a farm near Columbiana, in northeastern Ohio. His father, Benjamin Firestone, was a farmer, a careful, hard working man with a strong sense of duty. His mother, Catherine, kept a warm and disciplined home. Harvey was the son of a family that believed in work, in honesty, and in paying your debts.

He grew up doing the chores of a farm boy. He fed the animals, worked the fields, and learned that nothing came without effort. But he was curious, and he noticed that farmers were not the people who made the most money. The people who made the most money were the ones who bought and sold.

After finishing school, he left the farm to learn business. He took a job as a bookkeeper, and then worked for a buggy maker, selling carriages to customers. In those years, the buggy was the most important vehicle in America, and the man who sold buggies had to understand horses, roads, and people.

Harvey turned out to be a natural salesman. He was tall, steady, and sincere. He listened to customers, and he remembered what they said. Most importantly, he noticed something small. The carriages of his time rode on hard wheels, wrapped in iron bands. They rattled, they shook, and they wore out quickly. A few makers had started to offer carriage wheels with solid rubber tires, which were smoother and quieter. Customers loved them.

Harvey understood the lesson. People would pay for comfort. And if rubber made the ride better, then rubber had a future.

### 02_cap2
In the eighteen nineties, Firestone moved to Chicago, and went into the rubber tire business for himself. He did not make tires at first. He sold them, and he sold them well. He found the best products, he visited the buggy makers one by one, and he made sure the tires fitted properly and lasted. His reputation grew.

But then the world changed. In the middle of the decade, the bicycle craze swept across America, and bicycles needed air-filled tires. A few years later, something even bigger arrived. The automobile. In those days, cars were rare, noisy, expensive toys. But Firestone looked at them and saw the future. A horseless carriage would need tires, and tires would be needed in huge numbers.

He did the math in his head. A buggy had four wheels, and a buggy lasted for years. But a car was heavier and faster, and it would wear its tires out far sooner. Every new car sold meant four tires, and then four more.

He wanted to make them himself. And he knew where. A town in Ohio, where the rubber industry was already growing. Its name was Akron.

In eighteen seventy, a company called B.F. Goodrich had moved to Akron to make rubber products. In eighteen ninety-eight, another young company, Goodyear, started up in the same town. Firestone arrived a little later. By then, the giants were already on the field.

In nineteen hundred, at the age of thirty-one, he founded the Firestone Tire and Rubber Company, with about twenty thousand dollars. He rented a small factory, and he hired a handful of workers. Compared with Goodrich, it was a tiny start.

### 03_cap3
The first years were hard. Firestone's early products were carriage tires, and the buggy market was slowly dying. He had to move into automobile tires, and fast. But the early auto tires were poor. They punctured, they came apart, and they blew out. Drivers expected to fix a flat tire every few miles.

Firestone studied the problem. He hired engineers, and he tested new designs. He built a tire that was stronger, and he built a tire with a better grip on muddy roads. He also did something that was just as important. He traveled and sold. He knocked on the doors of car makers, one at a time.

The breakthrough came in nineteen oh six. Henry Ford, a Detroit carmaker who was building cheap, reliable cars, needed tires. Firestone went to see him, and the two men liked each other at once. Both had grown up on farms. Both disliked waste, and both believed in making something ordinary people could afford.

Ford placed an order for two thousand sets of tires. It was a huge order for a young company. It also meant that Firestone's tires would be on the cars of a man who was about to change the world. When Ford introduced the Model T in nineteen oh eight, and the car began to sell by the thousands, the demand for Firestone tires rose with it.

Firestone also learned the power of publicity. In nineteen eleven, the first Indianapolis five hundred race was held. The winner drove on Firestone tires. In the following years, Firestone tires won again and again, and the company printed those victories in advertisements across the country. A tire that could survive five hundred miles at top speed was a tire that could survive a trip to the grocery store.

### 04_cap4
By the end of the nineteen tens, Firestone's company had grown from a handful of workers to many thousands. He built a huge factory in Akron, and he built a neighborhood for his workers, called Firestone Park, with houses, gardens, and schools. He believed that a man who owned a home worked better, and stayed longer.

He was also a personal friend of the powerful. Henry Ford and Thomas Edison were his companions. In the summers, the three of them, with the naturalist John Burroughs, took long camping trips in the mountains and the countryside. The newspapers called them the Vagabonds. They traveled in cars, cooked over campfires, and argued about everything from politics to the nature of work.

Firestone's friendship with Ford was also a business advantage. The Ford Motor Company became one of his largest customers, and in the nineteen twenties, the two families grew close. Years later, they would be united by marriage, when Henry Ford's grandson William Clay Ford married Firestone's granddaughter Martha.

Edison, meanwhile, was working on a problem that mattered to all three of them. Rubber. Edison, Ford and Firestone were worried about where it came from. The world's rubber came from trees growing in Southeast Asia, and the plantations there were controlled by the British and the Dutch. If a war or a price scheme cut off the supply, American industry could be strangled.

It was a worry that, in the next few years, would turn out to be very real.

### 05_cap5
After the First World War, there was a collapse in the price of rubber. Planters in the British colonies lost money, and the British government decided to act. In nineteen twenty-two, it introduced a plan that limited how much rubber the plantations could export. The goal was to push prices back up.

It worked too well. Between nineteen twenty-two and nineteen twenty-five, the price of rubber rose from about thirty-six cents a pound to more than a dollar. The tire makers of America were furious. In nineteen twenty-five alone, Americans paid hundreds of millions of dollars more for crude rubber than they would have a few years earlier. And Americans bought around three quarters of the world's rubber.

Harvey Firestone was one of the loudest voices against the British scheme. He gave speeches, he lobbied Congress, and he told the public that America must grow its own rubber.

But speeches were not enough. He sent men to search the world for land where rubber trees could grow, in the Philippines, in Central America, and in Africa. Then he found the place that fitted. Liberia. It was a small West African country, founded in the nineteenth century by freed American slaves. It was poor, deeply in debt, and eager for investment.

In nineteen twenty-six, Firestone signed the famous lease. One million acres, at six cents an acre, for ninety-nine years. The company also arranged a large loan to the Liberian government. In the green hills near the coast, workers began to clear the jungle, and to plant rubber trees.

### 06_cap6
The Liberian plantation, in a place later called Harbel, grew into the largest rubber plantation on earth. By the nineteen thirties, it was producing a large part of the rubber that Firestone needed. It gave the company a supply that no foreign government could control.

But there was a darker side to the story. The workers who cleared the land and tapped the trees were Liberians, and many of them worked for low wages in harsh conditions. In nineteen thirty, an international commission, set up by the League of Nations, investigated reports of forced labor in Liberia. It found that the government's own officials had been rounding up men and shipping them to work on plantations, including some that supplied Firestone. The scandal forced the Liberian president to resign.

Firestone's company said it had not known about the abuses, and it cut ties to the practice. But for decades afterward, critics said that the company had profited from a system that exploited Liberian workers. It is part of his legacy, and an honest account has to include it.

At the same time, back in the United States, Firestone kept building. In nineteen twenty-eight, he started a weekly radio program called the Voice of Firestone, which brought classical music into American homes. It became one of the longest running programs on American radio. He pushed his engineers to invent. The company developed new ways to make tires stronger, and in the early nineteen thirties, it introduced low pressure rubber tires for farm tractors, which transformed farming.

He had gone from selling buggy tires to leading one of the biggest industrial companies in the world.

### 07_cap7
But success was not easy at home. The Great Depression struck in nineteen twenty-nine, and car sales collapsed. Firestone's company fought through it by cutting costs, by developing new products, and by selling hard. He remained a tireless, restless worker, even in his sixties.

He was also a family man. He and his wife, Idabelle, had six children, and the sons joined the business. His son Harvey Junior would eventually lead the company. Harvey Senior treated his sons the way he had been treated, with high expectations. He wanted them to start at the bottom and earn their way.

In his later years, he wrote a book about his career, called Men and Rubber, which told the story of how he built his company, and what he believed about business. He argued that the secret of success was honesty, hard work, and the willingness to see the future before others did.

On February seventh, nineteen thirty-eight, Harvey Firestone died at his winter home in Miami Beach, Florida. He was sixty-nine years old. His friend Edison was already gone, and Henry Ford would outlive him by almost a decade.

His company went on to become one of the biggest tire makers in the world. In nineteen eighty-eight, the Japanese company Bridgestone bought Firestone for more than two billion dollars. The name remains on the road to this day.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Harvey Firestone?

The first lesson is to spot the future early. Firestone sold rubber tires for buggies, and he saw that the automobile would change everything. He moved to Akron at the right moment, and he made sure his tires were on the cars that mattered.

The second lesson is to build relationships. Ford and Edison were his friends, not only his customers. A great deal of Firestone's early growth came from trust, and from being a man whom powerful people could count on.

The third lesson is to control your supply. When a foreign scheme made rubber too expensive, Firestone did not complain and wait. He went out and built his own source. Companies that depend on a single supplier can be squeezed.

And the last lesson is a warning about the cost. The same plantation that protected his company brought suffering to the workers who built it. A business can be brilliant and still be unjust, and the people who do the hardest work deserve to be treated as more than a line on a budget.

Harvey Firestone began as a farm boy who noticed that people would pay for a smoother ride. He ended up putting a continent on wheels. If you ever drive a car, remember the man from Ohio who bet on rubber.
