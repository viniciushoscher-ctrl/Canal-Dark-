### 00_hook
Nineteen fifteen. The most powerful man in Europe, the Holy Roman Emperor, owes a banker from the German city of Augsburg a fortune. Years earlier, that same banker had lent a fortune to bribe the princes who elected the emperor. Without his money, the emperor would not even wear the crown.

One day, the emperor visits the banker's house. The banker, wanting to make a point, lights a fire in the fireplace using cinnamon sticks, an expensive spice. Then, in front of his guest, he takes the emperor's promissory notes, worth a huge sum, and throws them into the flames, forgiving the debt.

It is a story that may be a legend. But the man at the center of it was real, and the wealth was real. His name was Jakob Fugger, and he is often called the richest man who ever lived, with a fortune estimated at about two percent of the entire European economy.

This is the story of the merchant who turned money into power, and power into money, and who invented modern capitalism.

### 01_cap1
Jakob Fugger was born on the sixth of March, fourteen fifty-nine, in Augsburg, a free imperial city in southern Germany. His family were weavers and merchants who had come from a village nearby, and who traded in cloth. They were already prosperous. His father, Jakob the Elder, had ten sons, and Jakob was the second youngest.

Because he was a younger son, he was meant to join the Church. At the age of fourteen, he was sent to learn a career as a priest. But when his older brothers died, the family business needed him, and the family sent him to Venice, then the greatest trading city in Europe.

Venice was where Jakob learned his craft. He studied at the Fondaco dei Tedeschi, the trading house for German merchants. There he learned double-entry bookkeeping, the new method of recording debts and profits that Italian bankers used, and which almost no German knew. He saw the spice trade, the banks, the long chains of credit that made the city rich.

Jakob learned something that would shape his life. Trade was not about goods. It was about information, trust, and credit. A merchant who could read a balance sheet better than his rivals would win.

### 02_cap2
Jakob returned to Augsburg in fourteen seventy-eight. In the family firm, he started with textile trading, and he also took a risk in a different business, mining.

The Fuggers lent money to Archduke Sigismund of Austria, called the Rich in Coin. In return, Jakob received a share of the output of the silver mines of Tyrol. It was the beginning of a new strategy. Lend to a ruler, take the mine as collateral, and then run the mine better than anyone else.

When his brothers died one by one, Jakob took control of the firm in fourteen ninety-four. He was only thirty-five. He then expanded into copper, a metal in great demand for cannons and coins. He bought mines in Hungary and in the Tyrol, and he built smelters, with a network of foundries and workshops.

Within a few years, the Fuggers controlled much of the copper and silver supply of Europe. He used a trick. He bought the whole production in advance, and then set the price himself. Such a cartel would be called a monopoly today. In those days, it was just good business.

### 03_cap3
But the real genius of Jakob Fugger was his understanding that the real money was in lending to princes.

In the late fourteen hundreds, Europe's kings and emperors needed constant cash for their wars, and their courts. They borrowed from bankers. Fugger lent to the Habsburgs, the ruling dynasty of the Holy Roman Empire, and he took their mines and their revenue as security. Over time, he became the Habsburgs' main financier.

In fourteen ninety-three, Maximilian the First became Holy Roman Emperor. He was famously short of money, and he borrowed constantly from Fugger. In exchange, Jakob received more mining rights. The loans grew, and so did Fugger's influence.

He also built a new kind of business. He sent agents across Europe, in Rome, Antwerp, Lisbon, Venice, Budapest, and Krakow. They wrote letters back to Augsburg, with news of prices, wars, and politics. The Fugger newsletters became a kind of early intelligence service, and they gave him information that no one else had. Jakob knew what was happening before anyone else.

By the early sixteenth century, the House of Fugger was the biggest company in Europe, with branches in dozens of cities.

### 04_cap4
In fifteen nineteen, the Holy Roman Emperor Maximilian died. The crown would pass to one of two candidates, the young Charles of Spain, a Habsburg, or King Francis the First of France. The seven electors, the princes who chose the emperor, would vote, and they could be bought.

Fugger was determined that Charles would win, because he feared that a French emperor would ruin his loans. He organized the money. He lent Charles about five hundred and fifty thousand florins, a huge sum, to bribe the electors. The bribes worked, and Charles was elected as Emperor Charles the Fifth.

Later, when Charles was slow to repay, Fugger sent him a famous letter, in which he reminded the emperor that without Fugger's help, he could not have won the crown. It is one of the most remarkable letters in business history. A banker told an emperor, politely, to pay his debts.

Fugger was now the kingmaker of Europe. And he was also a friend to the pope. In fifteen oh six, he lent money to Pope Julius the Second to pay the Swiss Guard, the soldiers who still protect the Vatican today. His family became bankers to the papacy.

### 05_cap5
There was another side to the business. In fifteen seventeen, a monk named Martin Luther nailed his ninety-five theses to the door of a church in Wittenberg, protesting the sale of indulgences, certificates that promised to reduce punishment for sins.

Fugger was in the middle of the story. The Archbishop of Mainz, Albert, had borrowed a huge sum from the Fuggers to pay the pope for his office. To repay the loan, the Church sold indulgences, and Fugger's agents collected the money. Luther's anger was aimed at that system. In a way, the Reformation began as a revolt against a financial scandal.

Fugger was also a pious Catholic, and a man of conscience in his own way. In fifteen fourteen, he started building something that would become his lasting legacy, the Fuggerei, in Augsburg. It was a small walled district of houses, built for poor Catholic workers, who could rent an apartment for a very low price. The annual rent was one Rhenish guilder, which is still the rent today, less than one euro per year.

The Fuggerei is the oldest social housing complex in the world, and it still stands, more than five hundred years later.

### 06_cap6
By fifteen twenty-three, Jakob Fugger was sixty-four years old, and his wealth was staggering. His assets were estimated at about two million gulden. Some historians, comparing his fortune to the size of Europe's economy, believe he was the richest private person in the history of the world.

He was also a man of business to the end. He kept working, and he even dictated his own epitaph, which said that he had been a man who wanted to gain as long as he could.

He built a grand palace in Augsburg. He financed churches, and a chapel for his family, where he is buried. And he hired artists, such as Albrecht Durer, who painted a famous portrait of him, showing a lean man in a red cap, with a sharp look.

On the thirtieth of December, fifteen twenty-five, Jakob Fugger died in Augsburg, at the age of sixty-six. He had no children. The firm passed to his nephew, Anton Fugger, who expanded it further.

For some years, the Fuggers remained the greatest bankers in Europe, lending to Charles the Fifth for his wars. But there was a flaw. Their wealth was tied to the Habsburgs.

### 07_cap7
In fifteen fifty-six, King Philip the Second of Spain, who had inherited most of the Habsburg lands, declared that Spain could not pay its debts. It was the first of many royal bankruptcies. Because the Fuggers had lent so much to the Habsburgs, they lost fortunes. Their business slowly declined.

By the end of the sixteenth century, the Fugger bank was no longer a leading power. The family kept some of its estates and its titles, and members of the family are still alive today. They still run a foundation that manages the Fuggerei.

Jakob's real legacy lies in how he changed business. He turned banking into an international enterprise. He used accounting, networks of agents, and information as weapons. He understood that money and politics were intertwined. And he showed how a private citizen could outdo kings.

Historians say that he was a pioneer of the modern corporation. He was not only a rich man. He was the first global capitalist.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Jakob Fugger?

The first lesson is to learn the craft. Jakob went to Venice and learned accounting at a time when most merchants kept records in their heads. Better information beats brute force.

The second lesson is to build networks. His agents in a dozen cities gave him news before anyone else. Knowing first is a kind of wealth.

The third lesson is a warning about concentration. Fugger's empire depended on the Habsburgs. When they failed, he fell. Never tie your whole fortune to a single borrower.

And the final lesson is that wealth can build something that outlasts you. The Fuggerei still houses the poor, five centuries later. A single gift, wisely designed, can last longer than any bank.
