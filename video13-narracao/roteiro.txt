### 00_hook
Nineteen twenty-nine. A young Texas banker named Conrad Hilton has just spent a decade building a chain of small hotels. Then the stock market crashes, and the banks that financed him begin to fail. Within three years, he is nearly broke. He owes his creditors a fortune, and his hotels are being taken away one by one. He is reduced to sleeping on the floor of a hotel lobby, and borrowing money from a bellboy.

Most men would have given up. Instead, Hilton did something that sounds crazy. He kept a single hotel room as his home, kept his head up, and said that someday he would own the greatest hotel in the world, the Waldorf Astoria.

His name is Conrad Hilton. And this is the story of how a boy from a dusty town in New Mexico built the first truly global hotel empire, by spending his whole life believing in a simple prayer.

### 01_cap1
Conrad Nicholson Hilton was born on December twenty-fifth, eighteen eighty-seven, in San Antonio, New Mexico, a tiny desert town. His father, Gus, was a Norwegian immigrant who ran a general store, and his mother, Mary, was a devout Catholic who taught the children to pray every day.

Gus was a hard worker and a good trader. He sold groceries and hardware to miners and cowboys, and little Conrad learned to count change before he could read well. When the store began to fail, Gus had an idea. He turned the family home into a boardinghouse, renting rooms to traveling salesmen and railroad men for a dollar a night.

Conrad carried luggage, cooked breakfast, and cleaned the rooms. He saw how much people would pay for a clean bed and a warm meal, and he never forgot it. His mother told him to pray for the right thing, and be kind to strangers. He kept that advice for the rest of his life.

### 02_cap2
At twenty-five, Hilton served in the New Mexico legislature, and then joined the army during the First World War. When he came home in nineteen nineteen, his father had died. The family business had collapsed, and he felt a duty to rebuild it.

Conrad decided to become a banker. He traveled to Texas, where the oil boom was bringing new fortunes, and he planned to buy a small bank in the town of Cisco. But when he arrived, the owner raised the price at the last minute. Hilton was furious, and he had nowhere to sleep. He found a hotel called the Mobley, where the lobby was crowded with oilmen, and every room was full.

He watched the clerk rent the same room in three shifts, every eight hours. The hotel earned more than any bank he had seen. Hilton had an idea that would change his life. He asked the owner what he wanted for the building, bought it that same week, and became a hotelier by accident.

### 03_cap3
Hilton ran the Mobley the way an oil well is run. He kept every room rented all day. He turned the lobby into a rented shop, rented the corners to newsstands and barbers, and even moved his own bed into the hallway. Within a few months, the hotel doubled its profits.

He bought a second hotel, and then a third. By the mid nineteen twenties, he had hotels across Texas, in Dallas, Waco, El Paso, and Fort Worth. In nineteen twenty-five, he built the first hotel that carried his own name, the Dallas Hilton. It was a skyscraper, and the opening party drew the whole city.

Hilton was a showman. He carried a banker's briefcase, smiled for photographers, and gave each new hotel an opening ceremony. He also introduced a rule to his managers. Every guest must be greeted with a smile.

By nineteen twenty-eight, his hotels were earning two million dollars a year. Conrad Hilton was thirty-nine years old and one of the youngest hotel owners in America.

### 04_cap4
Then came the crash. In October nineteen twenty-nine, the stock market collapsed. Within months, business travelers stopped traveling, and tourists stayed home. Hotel after hotel across America went empty and went bankrupt.

Hilton's hotels were heavily mortgaged. Banks demanded payment, and he had no money. His brother and his partners begged him to sell. He had to cut salaries, fire staff, and finally watch lenders take away hotel after hotel. By nineteen thirty-one, he owed more than half a million dollars.

He was told to declare bankruptcy, but he refused. He believed that a man's word was his most valuable asset. He went to each creditor and explained that he would pay, if only they would give him time. Many agreed. Some did not, but they respected his honesty.

It was in these days, he later wrote, that he nearly lost everything except his faith. His marriage with Mary Barron, whom he had married in nineteen twenty-five, also ended in divorce. He slept in hotel lobbies, and at one low point, he borrowed five dollars from a bellboy for a haircut.

### 05_cap5
Slowly, a recovery began. In the late nineteen thirties, he rebuilt step by step. In nineteen thirty-nine he opened a new Hilton in Albuquerque, close to his boyhood home, and then he took over hotels in California, in San Francisco and Los Angeles, and in Chicago.

During the Second World War, hotels were full of soldiers and officials. Hilton grew his chain again, and bought the Palmer House in Chicago, one of the largest hotels in the world, for almost twenty million dollars.

In nineteen forty-six, he made the move that defined his empire. He formed the Hilton Hotels Corporation and listed it on the stock exchange. And in nineteen forty-nine, he finally reached the dream he had cherished since the Depression. He bought the Waldorf Astoria in New York, the most famous hotel on Earth, for about three million dollars.

It was the one hotel that he had pinned to his office wall, years before. Nobody had believed it would ever be his.

### 06_cap6
Hilton had a second dream, to take his hotels around the world. In nineteen fifty-four, he bought the Statler Hotels chain for one hundred and eleven million dollars, the biggest real estate deal in history. And in nineteen fifty-five, he opened the Istanbul Hilton, followed by hotels in Cairo, Madrid, Berlin, and Havana.

His idea was to make each hotel a symbol of America. He said that every Hilton was a little America, a place where a traveler could find clean sheets, air conditioning, and ice water, wherever he was. Hotels became a weapon of the Cold War, spreading American style.

His personal life made headlines too. In nineteen forty-six, he married the actress Zsa Zsa Gabor, and the marriage lasted only a few years. His son Barron took over the business, and his great-granddaughter Paris would one day make the family name famous in a very different way.

Conrad Hilton died on January third, nineteen seventy-nine, at ninety-one. His company had hundreds of hotels in dozens of countries. Today, there are more than eight thousand Hilton properties around the world.

### 07_lesson
Conrad Hilton's story teaches two lessons. The first is that hardship can reveal what you truly value. He lost almost everything in the Depression, but he kept his name and his word, and creditors helped him recover because they trusted him.

The second is to keep a picture of your dream where you can see it. A photograph of the Waldorf Astoria hung on his wall for twenty years, before he could afford to buy it.
