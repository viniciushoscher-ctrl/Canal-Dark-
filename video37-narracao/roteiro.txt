### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-six. In a small town in the south of Sweden, a furniture company employee is trying to load a table into a car. It will not fit. He gets frustrated, and then he has a strange idea. He unscrews the legs.

The table slides in. The legs go in beside it. And a billionaire's company is born from a flat box.

The man who made that idea famous drove a twenty-year-old Volvo, flew economy class, and bought his clothes at flea markets. He once said that wasting money was a sin. Yet he built the largest furniture company on earth, with stores in dozens of countries and a catalog that was printed in hundreds of millions of copies.

He started at the age of seventeen, selling matches from a bicycle. He ended as one of the richest men on the planet. This is the story of Ingvar Kamprad, the farm boy who taught the world to build its own furniture.

### 01_cap1
Ingvar Feodor Kamprad was born on the thirtieth of March, nineteen twenty-six, on a farm called Elmtaryd, near the village of Agunnaryd, in the province of Småland, in southern Sweden. Småland is a land of stones, forests, and small farms, where the soil is poor and the winters are long. People there have a reputation across Sweden for being thrifty, stubborn, and hard working. Kamprad grew up to be all three.

His grandmother was a strong influence, and so was his father, Feodor. As a small child, he learned that if you wanted anything, you had to earn it. He was also dyslexic, which made school difficult, but he discovered something he was very good at. He was good at selling.

At the age of five, he started to sell matches. He bought them in bulk from Stockholm, at a very low price, and sold them to neighbors for a small profit. He found that if he went on his bicycle, he could reach more homes. Then he added other goods. Fish, Christmas decorations, seeds, and later, pens and pencils.

It was not a game. It was a lesson in the secret of trade. Buy cheap, in large amounts, and pass part of the savings to the customer.

### 02_cap2
In nineteen forty-three, when Ingvar was seventeen, he graduated from school with good grades. His father rewarded him with a small sum of money. With it, the young man registered his own company.

He named it IKEA. The name came from his initials, I and K, plus the first letter of his family farm, Elmtaryd, and the first letter of his village, Agunnaryd. Ingvar Kamprad, Elmtaryd, Agunnaryd. A seventeen year old with a bicycle and a rubber stamp had just founded what would become a global giant.

For the first few years, IKEA sold whatever he could find at a low price, and he delivered it by mail. In nineteen forty-five he produced his first catalog, a small leaflet sent to customers around the region. Orders came in by post, and the goods were delivered by the milk truck that passed through the village every day. It was cheap, and it was clever.

In nineteen forty-eight he added furniture, made by local carpenters in the neighborhood of Småland. Furniture was a big step. It was expensive, it took space, and it was bought only a few times in a lifetime. But Kamprad saw an opening. In postwar Sweden, thousands of young families were moving into new homes, and they had very little money.

### 03_cap3
In nineteen fifty-one, he made an important decision. He stopped selling pens and watches and focused on furniture. And he published the first IKEA catalog, filled with simple, modern pieces at prices that ordinary families could afford.

The next problem was proving that the furniture was good. Customers in the mail order business could not touch what they were buying, and rival furniture dealers spread doubts. So in nineteen fifty-three, Kamprad opened a showroom in the town of Älmhult, in an old factory building, where customers could sit on a chair and test it before they ordered.

It was a great success. People came by bus from miles around. The showroom was also a threat. Swedish furniture makers saw a small company undercutting them by selling stylish furniture at a fraction of the price. They struck back, banning IKEA from the big trade fairs and pressuring its suppliers to stop doing business with the company.

Then came the table. In nineteen fifty-six, an employee named Gillis Lundgren removed the legs of a table to fit it into a car, and the idea of flat packing was born. A table in pieces could be shipped by the thousands, stored in small spaces, and carried home by the customer. It cut damage, and it cut costs. It was a revolution.

### 04_cap4
The boycott gave Kamprad an even greater idea. If Swedish factories would not sell to him, he would find other factories. In the early nineteen sixties, he turned to Poland, then behind the Iron Curtain, where good workers and good materials were cheap. It was a risky move, but it cut costs sharply. IKEA now had prices that nobody could match.

In nineteen fifty-eight, he opened the first real IKEA store in Älmhult. It was the largest furniture display in Scandinavia, and it was designed with a new philosophy. Customers walked through a path of showrooms, picked up their own goods from a warehouse, put them in a flat package, and carried them home. There were no salesmen, and no delivery vans. Self service, like a supermarket.

In nineteen sixty-five, he opened a huge store near Stockholm, with a cafeteria, a nursery for children, and room for thousands of visitors. People waited in line to get in on the opening day, and the store had to be closed for a while because the crowd was too large.

Kamprad called his idea democratic design. Good design, he said, should be for everybody, not only for the rich. A beautiful chair should cost less than a bad one. He pushed designers to cut every unnecessary cost, and he rewarded those who made the price lower.

### 05_cap5
In nineteen seventy-three, IKEA opened its first store outside Scandinavia, in Switzerland, and a year later in Germany. Then came France, Britain, and the United States. In nineteen eighty-five, the company opened its first American store near Philadelphia. By the end of the century, it had stores on every continent except Antarctica.

The business grew by being strict about costs. Kamprad insisted on cheap, simple products, and clear prices. The famous Billy bookcase, launched in nineteen seventy-nine, became one of the best selling pieces of furniture in history. Every year, the company printed a catalog that reached hundreds of millions of homes, more than almost any book in the world.

But while his customers saw a friendly Swedish brand, the company behind it was one of the most secret in the world. In nineteen seventy-six, Kamprad moved to Switzerland to avoid high Swedish taxes. In nineteen eighty-two, he gave away IKEA to a foundation in the Netherlands, named Stichting INGKA, and the stores were owned by a separate network of companies, with another foundation in Liechtenstein collecting the royalties. Critics said it was a tax shelter. Kamprad said it protected the company from being sold, and kept it alive for the long term.

The structure made IKEA nearly impossible to buy, and nearly impossible to understand.

### 06_cap6
Everything about Kamprad's personal life was the opposite of what people expected from a billionaire. He drove an old Volvo for two decades. He flew economy class. He told his managers to write on both sides of the paper, and to use the stairs and not the elevator. He bought his clothes at flea markets, and he recycled tea bags. When he stayed at a hotel, he went to the minibar to see what was cheapest, and then took a walk to the supermarket.

He was also a man with a hidden past. In nineteen ninety-four, a Swedish journalist found letters showing that, as a teenager in the nineteen forties, Kamprad had been a member of a Swedish fascist movement and had written admiring things about its leader. The story was a shock.

Kamprad did not deny it. He wrote an open letter to all his employees, in which he called it the greatest mistake of his life, and said that he was deeply ashamed. He said that he had been young, and wrong, and that he had spent the rest of his life trying to make up for it. Some people accepted it, and others never did.

He also had a personal problem that he never hid. He drank too much, and he talked about it openly. Honesty about his weaknesses was one of his traits, and one reason his workers trusted him.

### 07_cap7
In nineteen eighty-six, Kamprad stepped down as chief executive, and began to prepare the next generation. In nineteen ninety-eight, he gave up the chairmanship, and in two thousand and thirteen he left the board, passing the leadership to his youngest son, Mathias.

He had written, in the nineteen seventies, a short document called The Testament of a Furniture Dealer. It was nine rules for his company, printed on a small booklet and given to every worker. It said that simplicity is a virtue, that it takes humility to see the future, and that most things still remain to be done. A great future awaits.

He died on the twenty-seventh of January, two thousand and eighteen, at his home in Småland, at the age of ninety-one. Not far from the farm where he had sold matches as a child. At the time of his death, IKEA was one of the most successful retailers on earth, with hundreds of stores and hundreds of thousands of employees, and Kamprad was among the richest men in the world.

Yet when people asked him what he valued most, he did not talk about money. He said that the most important thing was that ordinary people could afford to live well.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Ingvar Kamprad?

The first lesson is that a great business can begin with a very small idea. Matches. A bicycle. A leaflet. He did not need millions to begin. He needed a customer, and a way to serve that customer a little better than everyone else.

The second lesson is to turn every obstacle into an advantage. When Swedish makers boycotted him, he went to Poland. When a table would not fit in a car, he took off the legs. His greatest ideas were born from his biggest problems.

The third lesson is that low cost is a discipline, not an accident. Kamprad was frugal at home and frugal at work. He made thrift a culture, and the whole company lived it.

And the last lesson is to own your mistakes. He was ashamed of his youth, and he said so in public, and he kept working. A man is more than his worst chapter, but he must be willing to read it aloud.
