### 00_hook
Nineteen hundred. At the Paris World Exhibition, a strange electric car stands on display. It has no engine in the front, and no engine in the rear. Instead, it has a motor inside each of its front wheels. It is silent, smooth, and ahead of its time by a century. It was designed by a twenty-five-year-old Austrian engineer, who had never been to university.

The young man is Ferdinand Porsche. Over the next fifty years, he will design some of the most important vehicles in history. He will design the Volkswagen Beetle, one of the best-selling cars of all time. He will design racing cars that win championships, and tanks that terrorize the battlefields of a world war. And, with his son, he will create a sports car that will become a legend, with his own name on it.

But his life was also a story of ambition and moral failure. Porsche worked for Hitler, and he used slave labor in his factories. This is the story of Ferdinand Porsche, the brilliant engineer who built a car company, and who paid a heavy price for the choices he made.

### 01_cap1
Ferdinand Porsche was born on the third of September, eighteen seventy-five, in Maffersdorf, a village in Bohemia, which was then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire. His father, Anton, was a tinsmith, and ran a small metalworking shop. Ferdinand was expected to join the family trade.

He did. As a teenager, he worked in his father's shop. But he had a different passion. Electricity. In his village, one of the first houses to have electric lighting was his own, and he had built the system himself, in secret, when he was fifteen. His father reluctantly allowed him to pursue his interests, and at the age of eighteen, he went to Vienna.

In Vienna, he found a job in the electrical company Bela Egger. He attended evening lectures at the technical university, without being enrolled. He soon showed his talent, and in eighteen ninety-nine, he joined a famous carriage maker, Lohner, which wanted to build motor vehicles.

There, in nineteen hundred, he built the Lohner-Porsche. It used electric hub motors, in the front wheels, powered by batteries. It won a prize at the Paris Exposition. He then added a petrol engine to charge the batteries, and created the first hybrid car in the world.

### 02_cap2
Porsche's fame grew. In nineteen oh five, he became the technical director at Austro-Daimler, a company that built cars and engines. He designed powerful racing cars, and in nineteen oh nine, he won the prestigious Prince Henry Trial, driving a car of his own design. The Austrian emperor gave him an honorary doctorate. From then on, he was known as Doctor Porsche.

During the First World War, he designed engines for aircraft and tractors, and military transport. After the war, he moved to the Daimler company in Stuttgart, Germany, where he designed the Mercedes supercharged sports cars, the S and SS, which won many races. In nineteen twenty-three, he became the technical director of Daimler-Benz.

But he had a quarrel with the board, who did not share his vision for small and affordable cars. In nineteen twenty-eight, he left, and the next year, he went to Steyr, in Austria. But the Great Depression struck, and Steyr went bankrupt, and he found himself out of work.

At the age of fifty-five, Porsche took a bold step. In nineteen thirty-one, he opened his own engineering office in Stuttgart, called Dr. Ing. h.c. F. Porsche. It had no factory, and no car brand. It simply designed vehicles for others.

### 03_cap3
Porsche had a dream. He wanted to build a small, cheap, reliable car for ordinary people. He called it the people's car, Volkswagen in German. At that time, only the rich owned cars in Germany. Porsche believed that every family should have one.

Car makers rejected his designs, because they believed that there was no market for a cheap car. But then, in nineteen thirty-three, Adolf Hitler came to power. Hitler wanted a car for the masses, and he announced a project to build one. In nineteen thirty-four, he gave Porsche the job, with a demanding set of specifications. It had to carry two adults and three children, travel at one hundred kilometers an hour, and cost no more than a thousand reichsmarks.

Porsche delivered. His design had a rear-mounted, air-cooled engine, a rounded body, and a simple and robust construction. It would be known as the Beetle. The first prototypes were tested in nineteen thirty-six and thirty-seven, and in nineteen thirty-eight, Hitler laid the foundation stone of a huge factory, in a town that would be named Wolfsburg.

The car was a technical triumph. But it was tied to the Nazi regime. And Porsche had to pay a price for working with it.

### 04_cap4
When the Second World War began in nineteen thirty-nine, the Volkswagen factory switched to military production. Porsche turned his talents to weapons. He designed military vehicles, such as the Kubelwagen, a light army car, and he helped to design heavy tanks. The Porsche Tiger tank, and the giant Maus tank, were among the most powerful of the war, though neither was a success.

The Volkswagen factory used forced labor. Thousands of prisoners, concentration camp inmates, and civilians from occupied countries were made to work in terrible conditions. Porsche, who was a director of the company, knew about it. After the war, historians would document the suffering, and call it a stain on his legacy.

Porsche was awarded the Nazi title of Wehrwirtschaftsfuhrer, a leader of the war economy, and he received a high civil honor. He was a loyal servant of the regime, and an ambitious man. He later said that he was only an engineer, but that excuse never convinced his critics.

In nineteen forty-five, as the war ended, the Allies took control of the Volkswagen factory. And Porsche, along with his son Ferry, was arrested.

### 05_cap5
After the war, Porsche was held by the French as a possible war criminal. He was imprisoned for almost two years. His son Ferry, who ran the company during his absence, was also jailed for a short time, but released.

Ferry Porsche, then thirty-six, was determined to keep the family business alive. The company had moved to a sawmill in the Austrian village of Gmund. With a few dozen workers, and few resources, he set out to build a car that carried the family name.

He wanted a light, nimble sports car, made from the Beetle's parts. In nineteen forty-eight, the first car to carry the Porsche name, the three fifty-six, was built by hand in Gmund. It had an aluminum body, and a small engine in the rear. It was fast and beautiful.

The French authorities demanded a bail of one million French francs for Ferdinand's release. In August nineteen forty-seven, Ferry raised it, partly by selling the new Porsche design to a Swiss customer, and partly with the help of friends. Ferdinand was released, but he was in poor health, a broken man.

Ferdinand Porsche died on the thirtieth of January, nineteen fifty-one, at the age of seventy-five, in Stuttgart, after a stroke.

### 06_cap6
Ferry Porsche took the company forward. In nineteen fifty, the company moved back to Stuttgart, and began to produce the three fifty-six at a larger scale. It was an immediate success, especially in the United States, where it was loved by racing drivers and movie stars, among them James Dean.

In nineteen sixty-four, the company launched a new model, the nine eleven. It had a distinctive curved shape, and a rear-mounted six-cylinder engine. It would become the most famous sports car ever built. It is still produced today, more than sixty years later, and its design has barely changed.

Porsche also became a legend in racing. In nineteen seventy, the Porsche nine fifty-one won the twenty-four hours of Le Mans for the first time. The company has since won Le Mans nineteen times, more than any other manufacturer.

The company stayed in the family. It became Porsche AG. But in the nineteen nineties, it was almost bankrupt, because of weak sales. A new chief executive, Wendelin Wiedeking, rescued it, with the Boxster, and the Cayenne SUV, which became a huge hit.

### 07_cap7
In the two thousands, Porsche attempted something audacious. It tried to take over Volkswagen, the company that Ferdinand had designed. In two thousand and eight, it built up a huge position in Volkswagen shares, and it looked as though the small sports car maker would swallow the giant. But the financial crisis struck. Porsche ran into heavy debts, and in the end, it was Volkswagen that took over Porsche, in two thousand and twelve.

The Porsche and Piech families, who descend from Ferdinand, still control Volkswagen through a holding company. In two thousand and twenty-two, Porsche returned to the stock market, in one of the largest listings in European history, valuing the company at around seventy-five billion euros.

Today, Porsche sells over three hundred thousand cars each year. It is among the most profitable car makers in the world.

Ferdinand Porsche left behind a legacy of brilliant engineering. The Beetle sold more than twenty-one million cars. His company still produces some of the most desired sports cars. But he also left a moral lesson. Genius does not excuse complicity.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Ferdinand Porsche?

The first lesson is to learn by doing. Porsche had no university degree. He taught himself, by building. Curiosity and practice can beat credentials.

The second lesson is to design for the real customer. The Beetle was not a luxury. It was a car for ordinary families, and it changed the world.

The third lesson is to build something that outlasts you. Ferry took his father's work, and made it a brand. A family business can survive for generations when the next generation carries the vision.

And the final lesson is a warning. Talent without conscience can serve evil. Porsche worked for a regime that murdered millions. A great career does not wash away the cost.
