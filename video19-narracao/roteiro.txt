### 00_hook
Nineteen eighty-five. A thirty-year-old man, the co-founder of one of the most famous companies in the world, walks into his office at Apple and finds that he has no power left. The board has stripped him of every responsibility. His desk is still there, but his job is gone. A few months later, he is forced out of the company he created in his parents' garage.

He sells almost all of his stock. He tells the press that he feels like a man whom everyone has given up on. Silicon Valley laughs.

Twelve years later, that same man returns to Apple, which is ninety days from bankruptcy. Within a decade, he turns it into the most valuable company on Earth. His name is Steve Jobs. And this is the story of a man who was fired from his own company, and who came back to change five different industries.

### 01_cap1
Steven Paul Jobs was born on February twenty-fourth, nineteen fifty-five, in San Francisco. His birth mother was a young graduate student, who decided she could not raise a child. She gave him to a couple named Paul and Clara Jobs, who lived in Mountain View, California.

Paul was a mechanic, and he built a workbench in the garage for his son. He taught Steve that even the back of a cabinet, which nobody sees, should be made beautifully. Steve never forgot it. Clara was a bookkeeper. They were not rich, but they sacrificed to send him to good schools.

Steve was a brilliant, rebellious student. At Homestead High School, he met a quiet engineer named Steve Wozniak, five years older, who built electronics for fun. The two became friends, and they played pranks together. Jobs enrolled at Reed College in Oregon, but dropped out after six months, because he did not want to spend his parents' savings. He stayed on campus for another year and a half, sleeping on friends' floors, and attending only the classes that interested him, including calligraphy.

### 02_cap2
Jobs returned to California, and took a job at the video game company Atari. Then he traveled to India for seven months, searching for spiritual meaning. He came back with a shaved head and robes, and an interest in Zen Buddhism that shaped his taste for simplicity.

In nineteen seventy-six, Wozniak designed a small computer that anyone could use. Jobs saw its potential. He sold his Volkswagen van, and Wozniak sold his calculator, and together they raised about thirteen hundred dollars. They assembled the first Apple computers in Jobs's parents' garage in Los Altos.

Their first big order came from a local store, which wanted fifty computers. A venture capitalist named Mike Markkula then invested two hundred and fifty thousand dollars. Markkula also taught the young founders how to run a real company. He made them write a business plan, hire managers, and think about the customer. In nineteen seventy-seven, Apple launched the Apple Two, one of the first mass-produced personal computers. It was a hit.

When Apple went public in nineteen eighty, Jobs was twenty-five years old. His stake was worth more than two hundred million dollars.

### 03_cap3
In nineteen eighty-four, Jobs introduced the Macintosh with a famous Super Bowl commercial, directed by Ridley Scott. It showed a woman smashing a screen that showed Big Brother. The message was that Apple would save people from a gray, controlled future. It remains one of the most famous ads in history.

The Macintosh was revolutionary, with a graphical interface and a mouse. But it was slow, expensive, and short of software. Sales disappointed. Meanwhile, Jobs was a difficult boss. He insulted engineers, demanded the impossible, and played favorites. People called his ability to bend reality to his will a reality distortion field.

The company's chief executive, John Sculley, whom Jobs had famously lured from Pepsi with the question, do you want to sell sugar water for the rest of your life, or do you want to change the world, turned against him. In nineteen eighty-five, the board sided with Sculley. Jobs was removed from his position.

He was thirty years old, and humiliated. Later, he said that being fired from Apple was the best thing that could have happened to him.

### 04_cap4
He later described the years that followed as a time of lightness. The pressure of success was replaced by the freedom of being a beginner again.

Jobs started again. He founded a computer company called NeXT, which built powerful workstations for universities. But they were expensive, and sold poorly. Then, in nineteen eighty-six, he paid ten million dollars to buy a small computer graphics division from George Lucas. He called it Pixar.

For almost a decade, Pixar lost money. Jobs poured in tens of millions of his own savings. At one point, he nearly sold the company. Then, in nineteen ninety-five, Pixar released Toy Story, the first fully computer-animated feature film. It earned over three hundred and sixty million dollars. Pixar went public the same week, and Jobs became a billionaire.

Meanwhile, back at Apple, things were collapsing. Without Jobs, the company released confusing products, lost market share, and nearly went bankrupt. In nineteen ninety-six, Apple needed a new operating system. It bought NeXT for four hundred million dollars, and Jobs came back as an advisor.

### 05_cap5
By nineteen ninety-seven, Apple was ninety days from running out of cash. The board fired the CEO, and Jobs took over as interim CEO. He cut dozens of products, laid off thousands of employees, and made a surprising deal with his old rival, Microsoft, which invested a hundred and fifty million dollars in Apple.

He also reshaped the way the company worked. Jobs told his team that focus meant saying no to a thousand good ideas. He reduced the product line to just four computers, one for each of four customers, and put all of the engineers' energy into making each one great.

He launched a campaign called Think Different. And in nineteen ninety-eight, he introduced the iMac, a bright, translucent computer that looked like nothing else on the market. It sold millions. Apple was profitable again.

Then he looked beyond computers. In two thousand one, Apple launched the iPod, with the promise of a thousand songs in your pocket. In two thousand three, it opened the iTunes Music Store. Music would never be the same.

The iPod made Apple cool. Sales exploded. But Jobs was already thinking of the next idea. He realized that the phone was about to eat the iPod.

### 06_cap6
On January ninth, two thousand seven, Jobs stood on a stage in San Francisco and said that Apple was introducing three revolutionary products. A widescreen iPod with touch controls. A revolutionary mobile phone. And a breakthrough internet communications device. Then he repeated the three words, and revealed that it was one device. The iPhone.

The iPhone changed the world. It put the internet in everyone's pocket. Within a few years, Apple was selling more phones than any company on Earth. In two thousand ten, he launched the iPad, creating another new category.

But Jobs was ill. In two thousand three, he had been diagnosed with pancreatic cancer. He delayed surgery, trying alternative treatments, which many believe cost him precious time. In two thousand nine, he received a liver transplant. In August two thousand eleven, he resigned as CEO.

Steve Jobs died on October fifth, two thousand eleven, at age fifty-six. His last words, according to his sister, were, oh wow, oh wow, oh wow. At the time of his death, Apple was the most valuable company in the world.

### 07_lesson
Steve Jobs' story holds two lessons. The first is that failure can be a gift. Being fired from Apple freed him to build Pixar and NeXT, and he came back wiser, more disciplined, and ready to win.

The second is that taste is a strategy. Jobs did not invent the personal computer, the music player, or the smartphone. But he made each of them simple and beautiful, and that made all the difference.
