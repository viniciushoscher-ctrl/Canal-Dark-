### 00_hook
Nineteen seventy-three. In Rome, a sixteen-year-old boy is dragged into a car by masked men and vanishes. A few days later, a letter arrives. The kidnappers want seventeen million dollars.

His grandfather is the richest private citizen in America, and he gives a famous answer. He says he has fourteen other grandchildren, and if he pays one ransom, he will have fourteen more kidnapped grandchildren.

Months later, a package arrives at a newspaper office. Inside is a human ear. His name is Jean Paul Getty. And the question that haunts his story is simple: why did the richest man in the world refuse to pay?

### 01_cap1
Jean Paul Getty was born on December fifteenth, eighteen ninety-two, in Minneapolis, Minnesota. His father, George, was a lawyer who had moved the family into the booming oil business, and strict Methodist discipline ruled the house.

Young Paul was bright, restless, and spoiled by his mother. He was sent to boarding school in California, studied at Oxford, and spent his early twenties more interested in parties than in work.

But his father had a rule. If Paul wanted money, he would have to earn it. And in nineteen fifteen, Paul went to Oklahoma, where oil fever was sweeping across the plains, and he began buying leases, the right to drill on a piece of land.

He lived in dirty boarding houses, wore muddy boots, and negotiated with farmers face to face. In nineteen sixteen, a well he had a stake in came in, and in a single year he made his first million dollars. He was twenty-four.

### 02_cap2
At twenty-four, with a million dollars, Getty announced that he was retiring. He moved to Los Angeles, and for two years he lived like a playboy, chasing women and spending his money.

It was a mistake, and he knew it. He went back to work, and this time he learned the real rules. He studied balance sheets. He read every contract. He learned that in the oil business, the winners were the men who bought when others panicked.

In the nineteen thirties, during the Great Depression, oil was almost worthless. Companies were failing. Getty began buying their shares, quietly, for pennies. He bought the Pacific Western Oil company, and later Tidewater and Skelly, building a company slowly from the ruins.

His father had left him a smaller fortune than expected, but Getty needed no one. By the end of the thirties, he was a major oil man, and he still wanted more.

In nineteen thirty-two, he found a bargain nobody else wanted. A giant company called Pacific Western Oil owned huge fields, and its shares had collapsed. Getty bought them, bit by bit, and then he forced his way into the board. By the end of the decade he had a controlling stake.

He understood something that other people missed: in a crisis, the rich do not make money by working harder. They make money by buying what the frightened are throwing away.

### 03_cap3
The next move changed everything. In nineteen forty-nine, Getty took a risk that frightened his own advisers. He went to the Middle East, to the Neutral Zone, a barren strip of desert between Saudi Arabia and Kuwait, where no one believed there was any oil.

The Saudi king wanted money. Getty offered a deal, a payment of nine and a half million dollars up front, and a million dollars a year in rent, whether or not any oil was found.

It was a huge bet. For four years, his company drilled and found nothing. His directors begged him to stop. Getty refused. He had already seen that the geology matched fields nearby.

In nineteen fifty-three, the well finally came in. It produced oil in vast quantities, and Getty owned the rights almost alone. The bet that looked like madness had made him a billionaire.

The partnership with Saudi Arabia was not easy. His rivals, the giant American companies, had spent decades building relationships in the Middle East, and they laughed at the small independent who wanted to drill in the desert. Getty's engineers crossed the sand in trucks, sleeping in tents, and drilled well after well that came up dry.

Each dry hole cost him a fortune. Each month, he still paid his million-dollar rent. Friends said he was throwing money into the sand. Getty only said that the oil was there, and that patience would pay.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen fifty-seven, Fortune magazine named Getty the richest living American, with a fortune of about one billion dollars. He barely changed his life.

Getty moved to England and bought a Tudor mansion called Sutton Place, outside London. He filled it with art, antiques, and rare carpets. Then he installed something strange in the front hall: a pay telephone for guests.

Visitors had to use coins to make calls. The richest man in the world was unwilling to let people use his phone for free. Reporters loved the story, and it followed him for the rest of his life.

He also became a collector, with a passion for ancient Greek and Roman statues and French furniture. He bought a ranch house in Malibu and filled it with art, then built a replica of an ancient Roman villa to hold his collection. The museum opened in the nineteen seventies, and it was the only place where Getty seemed truly generous.

He was married five times, and every marriage ended in divorce. He admitted, in his memoir, that he was a better businessman than husband. He said that a man can be successful in business, or in marriage, but rarely both.

### 05_cap5
Getty lived by strict rules. He believed in thrift, hard work, and above all in controlling costs. He read financial reports on the toilet and did his own laundry in hotel sinks. He paid for his suits to be mended instead of replaced.

He also gave advice in a book called How to Be Rich. His formula was simple. Rise early. Work hard. Strike oil. And above all, hold on to your cash.

But his behavior with his own family was cold. His son Paul Junior struggled with addiction and a failing marriage. Getty cut him off. His grandchildren barely knew him, and he saw them as obligations, not as family.

He also had strange habits. He insisted on rooms kept at an exact temperature, he refused to use an elevator in a hotel if the stairs were free, and he kept a guard dog in his bedroom. He believed that most people wanted to take advantage of his money, and he treated friends like possible thieves.

In his own mind, he was protecting his fortune. In everyone else's mind, he was protecting it from the people who should have mattered most.

### 06_cap6
Then, in July nineteen seventy-three, the kidnappers took his sixteen-year-old grandson, Paul the Third, off a street in Rome.

The kidnappers demanded seventeen million dollars. Getty refused. He said he would not pay a penny, and the world was shocked. Months passed. In November, an envelope arrived at a newspaper with a lock of hair and a human ear.

Only then did Getty relent. He negotiated the ransom down to about two point two million dollars, the maximum amount that he could deduct from his taxes. He lent his son the rest, at four percent interest.

Paul the Third was released. He lived, but he was never the same, and the family never recovered. Getty never visited him.

The scandal changed how the world saw Getty. Newspapers called him the miser who let a boy lose his ear. Defenders said that if he had paid, every child in his family would have become a target. Historians still argue about who was right.

Three years later, on June sixth, nineteen seventy-six, Jean Paul Getty died at Sutton Place at the age of eighty-three, with a fortune of about two billion dollars.

### 07_lesson
Getty left most of his wealth to a museum he had built in Malibu, California. It became one of the richest art institutions in the world, and it carries his name today.

His story has two lessons. The first is that patience beats panic. Getty made his fortune by betting while others ran away.

The second is the cost of counting every coin. A man who treats money as the only thing that matters can win the world, and still lose everything else.

Money is a tool, and Getty knew how to use it better than almost anyone. But he never learned that the people closest to him were the one thing his fortune could not replace.
