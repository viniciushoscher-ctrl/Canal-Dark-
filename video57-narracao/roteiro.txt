### 00_hook
Nineteen eighty-four. New York City. A thirty-five-year-old Frenchman, the son of a construction businessman from the north of France, has just arrived in America. He is there to escape the socialist government that he thinks will ruin his country. He rents an apartment, and he spends his days wandering the streets of Manhattan, observing how people live and buy.

He notices something. In France, luxury is thought of as old, and dusty. In America, people want brands, with a story. They want to belong. He decides that luxury will be the great business of the future.

He returns to France, and he does something that stuns the business world. With the money of a financier, he buys a bankrupt textile empire, whose crown jewel is a failing fashion house, Christian Dior. He fires thousands of workers, and sells off most of the businesses. Critics call him the Terminator, and the Wolf in Cashmere.

But in the next forty years, he will build the largest luxury empire in the world, and become one of the richest men on earth. His name is Bernard Arnault. This is how he did it.

### 01_cap1
Bernard Jean Etienne Arnault was born on the fifth of March, nineteen forty-nine, in Roubaix, in the north of France, near the border with Belgium. It was a textile city, with chimneys and mills. His family was prosperous. His father, Jean Leon Arnault, ran a construction company, called Ferret-Savinel, and his maternal grandfather was an industrialist.

Bernard was a studious boy, with a taste for music. He played the piano seriously, and still does. His grandmother, a strong woman, had a great influence on him. He attended a Catholic school, and later went to the prestigious Ecole Polytechnique in Paris, one of the best engineering schools in France.

After graduating in nineteen seventy-one, he returned to Roubaix, and joined his father's firm. At twenty-five, he persuaded his father to sell the construction business, and focus on real estate. The firm was renamed Ferinel. He became the chief executive in nineteen seventy-eight.

He proved to be a skilled manager, and a bold dealmaker. But his eyes were elsewhere. He wanted to build a business of his own on a larger scale.

### 02_cap2
In nineteen eighty-one, France elected a socialist president, Francois Mitterrand. The new government raised taxes on the wealthy, and nationalized banks and industries. Many businessmen were afraid. Arnault, who was thirty-two, left for the United States with his family, and lived there for about three years. He later said that the stay made him think globally.

In nineteen eighty-four, back in France, he saw his chance. The Boussac group, a giant textile and retail empire, was bankrupt. It owned a department store, Le Bon Marche, a diapers brand, and the fashion house Christian Dior. The government wanted to find a buyer who would save jobs.

Arnault put together a bid. Backed by the investment bank Lazard, he offered the symbolic price of one franc, and promised to invest in the company, and to keep it going. The government accepted. He promised to keep nine thousand jobs. But after the purchase, he cut about half of them, and sold off most of the unprofitable parts. He kept Dior, and the department store.

The press attacked him, and public opinion was hostile. But he had the keys to the most valuable brand in the group.

### 03_cap3
Arnault realized that great brands were rare, and could be built into global names. Dior had a glorious past, but it had lost its shine. He invested in it, and hired new designers, and it began to recover.

His next target was Louis Vuitton Moet Hennessy, called LVMH, a group created in nineteen eighty-seven, by the merger of the leather goods firm Louis Vuitton and the champagne and cognac house Moet Hennessy. But inside the group, the two founding families, Henry Racamier and Alain Chevalier, were fighting bitterly. Arnault saw his chance.

Secretly, he began to buy shares in LVMH, with the help of the British drinks group Guinness, and he built up a large position. In January nineteen eighty-eight, he became a major shareholder. By nineteen eighty-nine, after a vicious fight, he had taken control. He pushed out Racamier, and became the chairman and chief executive of LVMH.

It was a bold takeover, and it made him the master of the world's largest luxury group. It also made him enemies in the French establishment, who thought he was an upstart.

### 04_cap4
Under Arnault, LVMH became a machine for acquiring and nurturing great brands. His method was simple. He bought historic houses that had lost their way, he kept the heritage and the craftsmanship, and he injected money, discipline, and fresh creative talent.

He hired the young British designer John Galliano, then Marc Jacobs, and many others. In nineteen ninety-seven, he bought the fashion house Celine, and the department store chain Le Bon Marche became a luxury destination. In nineteen ninety-nine, he took on the Gucci Group, in a famous battle against the French rival, Francois Pinault, who eventually won control of Gucci. Arnault, however, kept growing in other fields.

In the two thousands, he added Fendi, Givenchy, Kenzo, Marc Jacobs, Bulgari, in two thousand and eleven, and the champagne house Krug. He bought Tiffany, the American jeweler, in two thousand and twenty-one, for about fifteen and a half billion dollars, the largest luxury acquisition in history.

The group grew into more than seventy great brands, grouped in six divisions, wines and spirits, fashion and leather goods, perfumes and cosmetics, watches and jewelry, selective retailing, and more.

### 05_cap5
Arnault was a very different kind of luxury boss. He did not design anything. He was a manager and a collector. He believed that luxury was the opposite of fashion. It had to last. It had to feel timeless, even when it was new.

He insisted that prices should rise, not fall. A Louis Vuitton bag was never discounted, never put on sale, and never made in cheap factories. The scarcity made it more desirable. He also kept the company's production in France and Italy, where the craftsmen worked by hand.

He was also a ferocious competitor. He expanded in Asia early, where a new middle class loved Western brands. He recognized that Chinese customers would become the biggest market for luxury. He built a huge retail network, with boutiques in every global capital.

He was a hard, demanding boss. He worked from early morning until late at night, and he expected the same from his managers. His five children all joined the family business, and each was given a role. He prepared them for the future, and he made it clear that the empire would stay in the family.

### 06_cap6
In the two thousand and tens, LVMH reached new heights. Sales grew from about twenty billion euros in two thousand and ten, to more than eighty billion euros in two thousand and twenty-three. The company became the largest luxury group in the world, and the most valuable company in Europe, worth more than four hundred billion dollars.

Arnault's fortune soared with it. In two thousand and twenty-three, he briefly became the richest person on earth, with a fortune of more than two hundred billion dollars, ahead of Elon Musk. He was the first European to reach that rank in the Forbes list.

He also became a patron of the arts. In two thousand and fourteen, he opened the Fondation Louis Vuitton, a spectacular museum designed by Frank Gehry, in the Bois de Boulogne in Paris. After the fire at Notre Dame in two thousand and nineteen, his family pledged two hundred million euros to rebuild it.

He remained a controversial figure. In two thousand and twelve, he applied for Belgian citizenship, which led to a scandal. He also drew criticism for the closeness of his group to politicians. And he was often seen as ruthless. But the French also saw him as a national champion.

### 07_cap7
Today, Bernard Arnault is in his mid-seventies, but he shows no sign of stepping back. He has set the retirement age for the chief executive of LVMH at eighty, giving himself time. His five children hold key jobs in the group. Delphine runs Dior. Antoine heads communications and image. Alexandre is at Tiffany. Frederic and Jean lead other divisions.

LVMH is one of the most successful companies in the world. It owns brands like Louis Vuitton, Dior, Moet and Chandon, Hennessy, Sephora, Bulgari, and Tiffany. It has built a business based on the idea that the dream is worth more than the product.

Arnault has often said that he is not a fashion person, but an industrialist. He sees luxury as an industry of art, and he believes in patience. It takes decades to build a brand, he says, and only a moment to destroy it.

He started with one franc, and a bankrupt company. He now owns the world's most valuable collection of luxury names.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Bernard Arnault?

The first lesson is to buy the brand, not the balance sheet. He saw the value in a name that had lost its shine, and he restored it.

The second lesson is to protect scarcity. Luxury is about desire. A product that is always on sale is not a luxury.

The third lesson is to be bold when others are afraid. When France turned socialist, he went to America and watched. When Boussac fell, he moved.

And the final lesson is to think in decades. Arnault builds for the long term, from craftsmanship to family succession. Patience, applied for forty years, can build an empire.
