### 00_hook
Buffalo, New York, November of eighteen ninety-six. A small crowd stands in a power station beside Niagara Falls, and a switch is thrown. Electricity from the falls begins to flow along a wire, twenty-six miles, to light the city of Buffalo. It is the moment when alternating current wins.

Behind it stands a man that most people have never heard of. He was a Civil War soldier, an inventor, and a stubborn industrialist who bet his whole company on an idea that the most famous inventor in America called deadly.

His name is George Westinghouse. And this is the story of the engineer who won the war of the currents.

### 01_cap1
George Westinghouse Junior was born on October sixth, eighteen forty-six, in Central Bridge, New York. His father owned a small shop that made farm machinery, and George grew up among lathes and belts. He was a practical boy, who was happiest when he was making something with his hands.

In eighteen sixty-three, at sixteen, he ran away to join the Union Army, in the middle of the Civil War. He served first in the cavalry, and later in the navy as an engineer. After the war, he went to college for a short time, but he did not like the classroom, and he left.

At nineteen, he received his first patent, for a rotary steam engine. He was already thinking like an inventor, and a businessman.

### 02_cap2
His big idea came from a train. In eighteen sixty-six, he saw two trains collide head-on, and he wondered why the brakes had been so slow. At that time, brakemen had to run along the tops of the cars, and turn hand brakes one by one. It was slow, and deadly.

Westinghouse invented a brake that used compressed air, so that the engineer could stop all of the cars at once. In eighteen sixty-nine, at only twenty-two, he founded the Westinghouse Air Brake Company, in Pittsburgh.

Railroad owners were skeptical. But after tests, the air brake was adopted, and it later became the standard on railroads across the country. Westinghouse was rich, and he was not yet thirty.

### 03_cap3
He did not stop. He improved railway signals, and invented ways to carry natural gas safely into homes. Then, in the eighteen eighties, he became interested in electricity.

At that time, Thomas Edison was the king of electric power, and his system used direct current. It worked, but it could not travel far, so power plants had to be built about every mile. Westinghouse learned about alternating current, which could be sent through wires over long distances, at high voltage, and then reduced for use at home.

In eighteen eighty-six, he founded the Westinghouse Electric Company. He bought the American rights to patents for an alternating current transformer, and he hired a young engineer named William Stanley to build a working system.

### 04_cap4
Then came the war of the currents. Edison saw the new competition as a threat, and he fought back with fear. His supporters argued that alternating current was dangerous, and some public demonstrations killed animals. Harold Brown, an associate of Edison, even helped design the first electric chair, to link alternating current with death.

Westinghouse refused to fight dirty. He argued with facts. In eighteen eighty-eight, he hired a brilliant Serbian-American engineer named Nikola Tesla, and he bought his patents for a new alternating current motor.

The battle was costly. Westinghouse's company almost went bankrupt in the early eighteen nineties, and the bankers forced him to give up some of his rights. But he did not abandon alternating current.

### 05_cap5
His chance came in eighteen ninety-three. The World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago needed lighting, and Westinghouse offered to light it, with alternating current, for a much lower price than General Electric, Edison's old company. About two hundred and fifty thousand lamps lit up the White City, and millions of visitors saw it.

The next big victory came at Niagara Falls. In eighteen ninety-three, Westinghouse was chosen to build the generators for the first big hydroelectric plant. On November sixteenth, eighteen ninety-six, the power began to flow to Buffalo.

The contest was over. Alternating current became the standard in the world, and it still is today, in almost every wall socket.

### 06_cap6
Westinghouse built many companies, and he treated his workers well. He introduced the half-day on Saturdays, and he built houses and parks for employees. He was known for his honesty, and for the loyalty of the people who worked for him.

But the financial panic of nineteen oh seven hit hard. His company went into receivership, and Westinghouse lost control of the company that he had built. He was sixty.

He continued to work in smaller businesses. George Westinghouse died on March twelfth, nineteen fourteen, at the age of sixty-seven. He holds a place in Arlington National Cemetery, as a Civil War veteran. During his life, he received about three hundred and sixty patents.

### 07_lesson
Westinghouse teaches that the best idea can win even when the loudest voice is against it. He did not have the fame of Edison, but he had better engineering, and he had the patience to explain it.

Fight with facts, treat the people who work for you well, and be willing to risk your own money on a better way. History does not always remember the winners as it should, but the lights in every home still do.
