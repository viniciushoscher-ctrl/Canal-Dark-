### 00_hook
Menlo Park, New Jersey, the night of December thirty-first, eighteen seventy-nine. Three thousand people have taken a special train to a small village, to see something that many scientists said was impossible. A cheap, safe electric light, that could burn for hours without fire. Thomas Edison walks out, and turns a switch. The lamps glow, and the crowd goes quiet.

Only a few years before, this man was a deaf telegraph operator who had been fired from job after job. He would go on to hold more than a thousand patents, and he would help build the modern world.

His name is Thomas Edison. And this is the story of the inventor who turned the night into day.

### 01_cap1
Thomas Alva Edison was born on February eleventh, eighteen forty-seven, in Milan, Ohio. He was the youngest of seven children. When he was seven, his family moved to Port Huron, Michigan.

School did not work for him. He asked too many questions, and a teacher called him slow. His mother, Nancy, who had been a teacher herself, took him out of school after only a few months, and taught him at home. She gave him books, and he read them hungrily. At about ten, he built a small chemistry laboratory in the basement.

At twelve, he started to sell newspapers and candy on the Grand Trunk Railway. He even set up a printing press in a baggage car, and printed his own newspaper on the moving train. His hearing was already fading, and he later said that the silence helped him think.

### 02_cap2
Edison became a telegraph operator, one of the best jobs a young man could find at the time. He traveled from city to city, and he was fired more than once, often because he was busy with experiments.

In eighteen sixty-nine, he moved to New York, broke and hungry. By luck, he fixed a broken stock ticker machine at a brokerage, and he was hired to maintain the system. He then improved the machines, and sold his designs for forty thousand dollars, a fortune at the time.

He used the money to open a workshop in Newark, New Jersey. Then, in eighteen seventy-six, he made a decision that changed the history of invention. He moved to the village of Menlo Park, and built what he called an invention factory.

### 03_cap3
Menlo Park was the first industrial research laboratory in the world. Edison hired machinists, chemists, and mathematicians, and they worked on many projects at once. They worked day and night, and Edison often slept on a table.

In eighteen seventy-seven, he invented the phonograph, a machine that could record a human voice and play it back. When he showed it to the public, people called it a miracle, and newspapers named him the Wizard of Menlo Park.

But the phonograph was only the beginning. Edison now turned to a much bigger problem. Cities were lit by gas lamps, which were smoky, dirty, and dangerous. An electric lamp that was cheap and safe would change everything.

### 04_cap4
Many inventors had made electric lights, but none were practical. They burned out in minutes, or they were far too expensive. Edison and his team tested thousands of materials for the filament, the thin thread inside the bulb. He reportedly tried more than six thousand different plant fibers.

In October of eighteen seventy-nine, a carbonized cotton thread glowed for more than thirteen hours. Later, a filament made of bamboo lasted for over a thousand hours. On the last night of that year, he showed the lamps to the public.

But a bulb alone was not enough. To sell light, he also had to build the entire system, the generators, the wires, the meters, and the switches. In eighteen eighty-two, his Pearl Street Station in New York began to supply electricity to customers in lower Manhattan.

### 05_cap5
Edison's companies grew quickly, and they later merged into what became General Electric. He opened a great new laboratory in West Orange, New Jersey, and there his team worked on motion pictures. The kinetoscope, a device for watching short films, appeared in the early eighteen nineties.

But he also made costly mistakes. In the war of the currents, Edison backed direct current, while George Westinghouse and Nikola Tesla pushed alternating current, which could travel much farther. Edison fought hard to convince the public that alternating current was dangerous, and he lost the fight.

He also spent years and a fortune on a failed plan to mine iron ore, which cost him much of his money. In eighteen ninety-two, the financiers who controlled his electric company pushed his name out of it.

### 06_cap6
Edison did not give up. He kept inventing, and he improved the storage battery, cement, and the movie camera. During the First World War, he worked on projects for the navy.

In December of nineteen fourteen, a fire destroyed much of his West Orange laboratory. He was sixty-seven years old. He is said to have told his son to call his mother, because she would never see a fire like it again. The next morning, he began to rebuild.

Edison died on October eighteenth, nineteen thirty-one, at the age of eighty-four. Across the United States, people mourned the man who had given them light.

### 07_lesson
Edison used to say that genius is one percent inspiration and ninety-nine percent perspiration. He did not succeed because he was lucky. He succeeded because he was willing to try thousands of things that did not work, and to learn something from each one.

Build a system that lets you fail quickly, and cheaply. And remember that the idea is only the start. Edison won not just because he invented a lamp, but because he built the whole world around it.
