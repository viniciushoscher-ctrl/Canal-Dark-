### 00_hook
Nineteen ninety-nine. In a small apartment in Hangzhou, China, eighteen people sit on the floor, crowded around a few old computers. They are listening to a former English teacher, a short, skinny man, with a boyish face and a restless smile. He tells them that they are going to build an online marketplace, for Chinese small businesses, that will one day be the biggest in the world.

Most of them have never used the internet. The man himself has no technical training. He cannot write code. He had been turned down by thirty jobs, including one at KFC, where twenty-three people applied and he was the only one rejected. He was rejected by Harvard ten times.

The company he founded that night is called Alibaba. Twenty-five years later, it will be worth hundreds of billions of dollars. In two thousand and fourteen, its public listing will be the largest in history. And Jack Ma will be the richest man in China.

This is the story of a teacher who became a tycoon, and then, suddenly, disappeared.

### 01_cap1
Jack Ma was born Ma Yun on the tenth of September, nineteen sixty-four, in Hangzhou, a city of lakes and gardens, south of Shanghai. His parents were performers of a traditional form of storytelling, called pingtan. The family was poor. And the times were hard, because it was the era of Mao Zedong, and of the Cultural Revolution.

Ma was a small, thin boy, and not a good student. He failed his math exams, and he was rejected by schools. But he had a passion, English. When he was twelve, he began to ride his bicycle, every morning, for forty minutes, to a hotel near the West Lake, where foreign tourists stayed. He offered to guide them for free, just to practice his English.

He did this for nine years. A foreign visitor gave him the English name Jack, because he could not pronounce Ma Yun. The tourists became his teachers, and also his window to the outside world. He learned that the world beyond China was very different from what he had been taught.

He failed the college entrance exam twice. On the third try, at the age of twenty, he was admitted to Hangzhou Teachers Institute, a minor college, to study English. He graduated in nineteen eighty-eight, and became an English teacher.

### 02_cap2
Ma taught for five years, with a salary of about twelve dollars a month. He was popular with the students, but he dreamed of more. In nineteen ninety-four, he started a translation company, Hope, that struggled, and made little money.

In nineteen ninety-five, a local government sent him to the United States, to help resolve a business debt. In Seattle, a friend showed him the internet for the first time. He typed one word into a search engine. Beer. He saw results from many countries, but nothing from China. He then typed China, and found almost nothing.

He realized the opportunity. He returned to Hangzhou, and told his friends that the internet would change China. They thought he was crazy. In nineteen ninety-five, he founded China Pages, one of the first Internet companies in China, to create websites for Chinese businesses. It did not do well. He left, and spent a few years working for a government project in Beijing.

But in nineteen ninety-nine, he was ready. He returned to Hangzhou, and gathered his friends, his former students, and colleagues. They put together about sixty thousand dollars, and started Alibaba.

### 03_cap3
The name Alibaba was a stroke of luck. Ma was in a cafe in San Francisco, and asked a waitress if she knew the story of Ali Baba. She did. He asked other people around the world, and they all knew it too. The name was easy to say, easy to remember, and carried the idea of opening a door to treasure.

Alibaba's first product was a website for Chinese exporters to find foreign buyers. It was a business-to-business marketplace. The idea was to help small and medium companies, which were ignored by the big banks, and had no way to reach customers abroad.

The early years were hard. The company had few funds, and little revenue. But in two thousand, Ma met a young Japanese investor, Masayoshi Son, the founder of SoftBank. After a six-minute conversation, Son agreed to invest twenty million dollars. It was one of the best investments in history. It would grow to be worth about sixty billion dollars.

Then came the dot-com crash. Many internet firms died. Alibaba nearly ran out of cash. Ma told his team to survive, and to keep the cash reserves. He famously said that today is hard, tomorrow is harder, but the day after tomorrow will be beautiful.

### 04_cap4
In two thousand and three, Ma started a new site, Taobao, a marketplace for consumers, to compete with eBay, which had just taken over the Chinese market. eBay was a giant, with money and experience. Most people thought Ma would lose.

Ma had a different strategy. He made Taobao free, with no fees for sellers. He understood that the Chinese needed trust, and he created Alipay, an online payment system that held the money until the buyer received the goods. It solved the biggest problem in Chinese e-commerce, fraud.

Within three years, Taobao had crushed eBay in China. Ma said that eBay was a shark in the ocean, and he was a crocodile in the Yangtze River. In the river, he would win.

Alibaba grew rapidly. In two thousand and eight, Taobao Mall, later renamed Tmall, was launched, for big brands. In two thousand and nine, Ma launched Singles Day, on the eleventh of November, an online shopping festival, which grew to be the largest shopping event in the world. In a single day, Alibaba's platforms sold tens of billions of dollars of goods.

### 05_cap5
On the nineteenth of September, two thousand and fourteen, Alibaba went public on the New York Stock Exchange. It raised about twenty-five billion dollars, the largest initial public offering in history at that time. The company was valued at more than two hundred and thirty billion dollars. Ma, who had never been a manager in the usual sense, was suddenly the richest man in China.

He stood on the floor of the exchange, wearing a suit with a silly smile. He had invited some of his customers, including a young woman who sold goods online, and a delivery driver, to ring the bell with him. He wanted to show that Alibaba was built by small people.

In the following years, Ma stepped back. In two thousand and thirteen, he stepped down as the chief executive. In two thousand and eighteen, on his fifty-fourth birthday, he announced his retirement as chairman, passing the job to Daniel Zhang. He said he wanted to return to education, and to philanthropy.

He also invested in Ant Group, the financial technology company that grew from Alipay. By two thousand and twenty, Ant was a giant, serving more than one billion users, and preparing for the biggest public listing in history.

### 06_cap6
Then, everything changed. On the twenty-fourth of October, two thousand and twenty, at a financial summit in Shanghai, Ma gave a speech. He criticized Chinese financial regulators, and compared the banks to pawn shops. It was a mild criticism by Western standards, but in China, it was daring.

Within days, the authorities struck. On the third of November, two thousand and twenty, just two days before the planned listing, the Shanghai stock exchange suspended the Ant Group IPO. The offering would have raised about thirty-seven billion dollars. It was cancelled.

Ma disappeared from public view. For about three months, he made no public appearances. Rumors spread. Then in January two thousand and twenty-one, he appeared in a video. He later lived abroad, in Japan, and other countries, and he painted, and studied agriculture.

The government also fined Alibaba about two point eight billion dollars, in April two thousand and twenty-one, for antitrust violations. The company's stock fell by more than seventy percent. Ma's fortune shrank.

The lesson was clear. In China, even the greatest tycoon operates at the pleasure of the state.

### 07_cap7
In two thousand and twenty-three, Ma returned, to teach at universities, and to visit Alibaba. He has appeared at Chinese schools, and has met with leaders. Alibaba has reorganized itself into six units, and its stock has partly recovered.

He remains one of the richest people in China, with a fortune of about twenty-five billion dollars, far below the peak of sixty billion. He is also a philanthropist, and has donated to education, and to health charities, including supplies to many countries during the Covid pandemic.

His reputation is mixed. Some admire him as a self-made man who proved that a poor English teacher could build a world-class company. Others criticize the culture of long working hours, called nine nine six, which the company tolerated.

Whatever the verdict, Jack Ma changed China, and the world. He brought e-commerce to a billion people, and he showed that a small man, with a big dream, could take on giants.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Jack Ma?

The first lesson is to keep trying. Ma failed exams, was rejected from jobs, and was turned down by Harvard ten times. He kept going.

The second lesson is to solve the problem people really have. Chinese buyers did not trust sellers. Ma built Alipay, and trust became the product.

The third lesson is to build a team better than yourself. Ma could not code. He hired people who could, and he gave them a mission.

And the final lesson is to know the rules of the game you play in. Ma's fall shows that success does not make you untouchable. Respect the power around you.
