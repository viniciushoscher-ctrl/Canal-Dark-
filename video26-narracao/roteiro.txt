### 00_hook
Sacramento, California, eighteen sixty. In a small hardware store, four men sit around a table, and listen to an engineer unroll a map. He claims that a railroad can be built across the Sierra Nevada, one of the hardest mountain ranges in America. Almost everyone in the city thinks he is a dreamer. One of the four, a hardware merchant, decides to believe him.

He will spend the next forty years building railroads, ships, and an empire. He will be called a genius, and a villain, and both of them will be true.

His name is Collis Huntington. And this is the story of the hardware merchant who built the Southern Pacific.

### 01_cap1
Collis Potter Huntington was born on October twenty-second, eighteen twenty-one, in Harwinton, Connecticut. He was one of nine children, in a poor farming family. At fourteen, he left school to work, and he started selling small goods as a peddler, traveling from town to town.

He was a careful, tough worker. He saved his money, and he learned how to bargain. He opened a small business in Oneonta, New York, and slowly grew his savings. He did not drink, and he did not waste a dollar.

In eighteen forty-nine, news of gold in California spread, and Huntington caught the fever. But he had a different idea from most. He would not dig for gold, he would sell to those who did.

### 02_cap2
He sailed from New York to Panama, and crossed the jungle, in a terrible, dangerous journey. It took months. He stopped in Panama, and made money selling goods to travelers who were waiting for a ship. He reached Sacramento, and in eighteen fifty-five, he opened a hardware store with a partner named Mark Hopkins.

The business was a success. The miners needed picks, shovels, and nails, and Huntington and Hopkins supplied them. They were thrifty, honest, and suspicious of risk. Their store became one of the best known in the city.

Then in eighteen sixty, an engineer named Theodore Judah came to them. He had surveyed a route through the Sierra, and he needed backers.

### 03_cap3
Huntington listened to Judah, and checked the numbers, and he did not trust anyone's word. But he saw that the survey was real. Together with Hopkins, Leland Stanford, and Charles Crocker, he formed the Central Pacific Railroad. They would later be known as the Big Four.

Huntington's job was the hardest. While Stanford was governor, and Crocker supervised the construction, Huntington went to Washington, and to New York, to raise money, buy iron, and win favors from Congress. He used lobbying and favors to obtain government loans, and he was very good at it.

In eighteen sixty-nine, the Central Pacific met the Union Pacific in Utah. The first transcontinental railroad was finished.

### 04_cap4
For many men, that would have been enough. Huntington wanted more. He built the Southern Pacific, a railroad that ran from California across the Southwest. In eighteen eighty-three, it reached New Orleans, and gave the Big Four a route across the whole southern edge of the country.

He also looked east. He took control of the Chesapeake and Ohio Railway, which carried coal from West Virginia to the sea. At the end of the line, in Virginia, he founded a town, and a shipyard.

That shipyard was Newport News Shipbuilding, which he started in eighteen eighty-six. It grew into one of the greatest shipyards in the world, and it still builds aircraft carriers for the United States Navy.

### 05_cap5
Huntington's methods made him many enemies. In California, farmers and small businessmen called the Southern Pacific the Octopus, because its tentacles reached into every part of their lives. The railroad charged high rates, and it controlled the legislature. He was famously blunt, and he did not hide it.

The most famous fight was over the Port of Los Angeles. Huntington wanted the Southern Pacific to control the harbor at Santa Monica, but the people of Los Angeles wanted a free harbor, at San Pedro. After years of struggle, in eighteen ninety-six, the government chose San Pedro, and Huntington lost.

It was a rare defeat. It also showed how much the public hated monopoly.

### 06_cap6
In his later years, Huntington lived in New York, in a large mansion on Fifth Avenue. He had married twice. His first wife Elizabeth died in eighteen eighty-three, and he married Arabella, who became a famous art collector.

Collis Huntington died on August thirteenth, nineteen hundred, at his camp in the Adirondack Mountains. He was seventy-eight. His nephew Henry Huntington inherited a part of his business, and later built a library and an art museum, in California.

The railroad he built is now part of Union Pacific. The shipyard he founded is still working. And the Sierra route he financed is still the path of trains today.

### 07_lesson
Huntington teaches that the person who sells the shovels can win bigger than the person who digs. He was not the engineer, or the face of the company. He was the one who found the money, and made the deals.

He also teaches a hard lesson. Power without limits creates enemies, and eventually rules. Build something that lasts, but build it in a way that the public can live with.
