### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-six. A twenty-five-year-old man sits at a kitchen table in Omaha, Nebraska, with seven friends and relatives, and he asks them for money. Together they give him a hundred thousand dollars. He promises to invest it carefully, and to charge them nothing unless he beats the market. He is shy, he is awkward, and his voice squeaks when he speaks to a crowd.

Fifty years later, that same man will have a fortune of more than sixty billion dollars. He will live in the same gray house he bought in nineteen fifty-eight, for thirty-one thousand five hundred dollars. He will eat breakfast at a fast-food restaurant for three dollars and fifty cents. And he will announce that he is giving almost everything away.

His name is Warren Buffett. And this is the story of the greatest investor in history, a man who got rich with patience, and who never got tired of the same simple idea.

### 01_cap1
Warren Edward Buffett was born on August thirtieth, nineteen thirty, in Omaha, Nebraska. His father, Howard, was a stockbroker and later a congressman, an honest man who hated government waste. His mother, Leila, had a famous temper.

Warren was a strange child. He loved numbers. At six, he sold packs of gum door to door, buying them for twenty-five cents and selling single sticks. At eleven, he bought his first stock, three shares of a company called Cities Service, for thirty-eight dollars each. The stock dropped to twenty-seven. When it recovered to forty, he sold, and then watched it climb to two hundred. It was a lesson in patience he never forgot.

At thirteen, he filed his first tax return, deducting his bicycle and his watch as business expenses. By the time he finished high school, he had saved nearly six thousand dollars from paper routes, pinball machines, and used golf balls.

### 02_cap2
Buffett had also taken a Dale Carnegie public speaking course, because he was terrified of speaking in front of people. He hung the certificate on the wall of his office, and it is still there. He was rejected by Harvard Business School, which was a painful blow. Instead, he read a book called The Intelligent Investor, by a professor at Columbia University, Benjamin Graham. It changed his life. Graham taught that every stock is a piece of a real business, and that the price can differ wildly from the value. An investor should always demand a margin of safety.

Buffett enrolled at Columbia just to study with Graham. He earned the only A-plus Graham ever gave. After graduation, he begged Graham for a job, even offering to work for free. Graham refused. Buffett went home to Omaha and sold stocks for his father's firm.

Two years later, Graham finally offered him a job. Buffett worked at Graham's firm until nineteen fifty-six, when Graham retired. That year, he returned to Omaha and started the investment partnership that began at the kitchen table.

### 03_cap3
Buffett's method was simple. He looked for companies that were cheap compared to their true worth. He bought them, and waited. He ignored forecasts, rumors, and the daily noise of the market. He called the market a voting machine in the short run, but a weighing machine in the long run.

The results were extraordinary. Between nineteen fifty-six and nineteen sixty-nine, his partnership returned about twenty-nine percent a year, while the market returned about eleven. A thousand dollars invested with him turned into many thousands. His partners were delighted, and new ones joined, including a young doctor who invested all of his savings.

In nineteen sixty-nine, with the market at a peak, Buffett did something shocking. He closed the partnership and returned every dollar. He told investors that he could not find any good bargains. Most managers would have grabbed the fees. Buffett preferred honesty.

He also kept a famous rule, which he called his two rules of investing. Rule number one, never lose money. Rule number two, never forget rule number one.

He did, however, keep one investment for himself. It was a failing textile company in Massachusetts, called Berkshire Hathaway.

### 04_cap4
Buffett took over Berkshire in nineteen sixty-five. He later called it his dumbest decision, because the textile business was dying. But he used it for something else. He used the cash from the old business to buy insurance companies.

Insurance had a magical feature that Buffett loved: float. Customers pay premiums upfront, and claims are paid out years later. In the meantime, the insurer holds billions of other people's money, which Buffett could invest. It was like borrowing at zero interest.

With the float, he began buying stocks and entire companies. He bought a candy company called See's Candies in nineteen seventy-two, for about twenty-five million dollars. It earned more than a billion dollars over the years. He bought the Washington Post, and GEICO. In the eighties, he bought a large stake in Coca-Cola.

He never bought what he did not understand. He once said that risk comes from not knowing what you are doing, and he stayed inside what he called his circle of competence for more than sixty years.

He liked businesses with what he called a moat, a lasting advantage that protects a company from competitors. A brand like Coca-Cola, a cheap and trusted product that people want every day. He said that the best business was one that an idiot could run, because sooner or later, one would.

### 05_cap5
By nineteen eighty-seven, Buffett had become a billionaire. In nineteen ninety, he was among the richest men in America. But his lifestyle did not change. He still lived in Omaha, and drove his own car. He ate hamburgers, and drank cherry Coke.

In nineteen ninety-one, he faced a crisis. A bond trader at Salomon Brothers, one of the firms in which Berkshire had invested, was caught cheating in Treasury auctions. Buffett stepped in as interim chairman. He told the employees one thing: lose money for the firm, and I will be understanding. Lose a shred of reputation, and I will be ruthless.

He later guided the company through the scandal and saved it from collapse. The episode proved that his greatest asset was his word.

In the late nineties, while the dot-com bubble was rising, critics said that Buffett was finished. He refused to buy technology stocks, because he did not understand them. Then the bubble burst in two thousand, and Berkshire's shares recovered, while the Nasdaq plunged. Buffett had been right again.

### 06_cap6
For all his wealth, Buffett lives frugally. He still has no cell phone, and no computer on his desk. He once paid more attention to a coupon for a hamburger than to a thousand-dollar suit.

In two thousand eight, after the financial crisis, Buffett invested billions of dollars in Goldman Sachs and General Electric when banks were desperate. He said that the time to be greedy is when others are fearful. His investments earned billions of dollars in profit.

That year, Forbes named him the richest man in the world, with a fortune of sixty-two billion dollars. He did not care. In two thousand six, he had announced that he would give about eighty-five percent of his wealth to charity. Most of it went to the Bill and Melinda Gates Foundation, and to foundations run by his children.

With Bill Gates, he started the Giving Pledge, a campaign to ask the world's richest to give away most of their fortunes. More than two hundred people have signed.

Buffett turned ninety in two thousand twenty. He still goes to work every day. He still lives in the same Omaha house. And his company, Berkshire Hathaway, is worth about a trillion dollars.

### 07_lesson
Warren Buffett's story contains two lessons. The first is that time is the most powerful tool in finance. Almost all of his wealth came after his sixtieth birthday, because compound interest works slowly, and then suddenly.

The second is that character is a competitive advantage. Buffett said it takes twenty years to build a reputation, and five minutes to ruin it. In a world full of noise, he built a billion-dollar empire by being patient, honest, and boring.
