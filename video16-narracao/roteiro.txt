### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-four. A fifty-two-year-old milkshake machine salesman drives into San Bernardino, California, because a small restaurant has ordered eight of his machines at once. Eight. No one else in America is buying that many. He wants to see what could possibly need so many milkshakes.

At noon, he finds a line of customers stretching around the block. Hamburgers cost fifteen cents. Orders are handed over in less than a minute. There are no waitresses, no plates, and no silverware. The salesman stands in the parking lot, watching, and he is stunned. Then he does something that will make him a billionaire, and leave two honest brothers with a lifetime of regret.

His name is Ray Kroc. And this is the story of how a man in his fifties, with diabetes and arthritis, built the largest restaurant company on Earth, partly with an idea that was not his own.

### 01_cap1
Raymond Albert Kroc was born on October fifth, nineteen-oh-two, in Oak Park, a suburb of Chicago. His parents were Czech immigrants, and the family was modest. As a boy, he sold lemonade at a stand, and worked in a store that sold soda.

At fifteen, he dropped out of high school. During the First World War, he lied about his age to become a Red Cross ambulance driver. He was sent to Connecticut for training, but the war ended before he could be deployed. One of his fellow trainees was a young man named Walt Disney, who would one day become his friend, and a neighbor in history.

After the war, Kroc tried many jobs. He played piano in bands. He sold paper cups, and sold real estate in Florida just before the land boom collapsed. He was a salesman, and an optimistic one. He believed that the next deal was always right around the corner.

### 02_cap2
For nearly twenty years, Kroc sold paper cups for the Lily-Tulip Cup Company. He was a good salesman, and he made a decent living. But in the nineteen thirties, he heard about a new machine that could mix five milkshakes at once, called the Multimixer. It was made by a man named Earl Prince.

Kroc was so impressed that he acquired the exclusive rights to sell it across the country. He quit his stable job, and in nineteen forty-one, he started selling Multimixers to soda fountains and drive-ins.

It was a hard business. He drove thousands of miles, from town to town. When the war came, the sugar shortage hurt his sales. In the fifties, new machines also appeared and copied his product. By the age of fifty, Kroc was a tired man with a failing business and bad health. He had lost his gall bladder and most of his thyroid gland. He was close to retirement, with little to show for it.

### 03_cap3
Then came the order from San Bernardino. The restaurant belonged to two brothers, Richard and Maurice McDonald. They had invented what they called the Speedee Service System. The menu had been cut down to nine items. Burgers were cooked in an assembly line, each worker doing one task. The food was served in paper wrappers, in thirty seconds.

The prices were low. The quality was consistent. There was no need for waitresses, or dishwashers, or tipping. The McDonald brothers were already making about a hundred thousand dollars a year, a fortune at the time.

Kroc stood in the parking lot and watched. Teenagers, families, and workers came in a constant stream. He later wrote that he saw a gold mine. He felt his heart beating faster than it had in years. He asked the brothers a simple question. Why not open many restaurants like this across America?

Richard McDonald answered honestly. They had tried to franchise, but it had not worked. And besides, they were happy with their life. They did not want the stress of managing a national chain. Kroc smiled, and offered to do it for them.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen fifty-five, Kroc signed a deal to become the franchising agent for the McDonald brothers. They would get a half percent of sales. He would build the chain.

On April fifteenth, nineteen fifty-five, he opened his first restaurant in Des Plaines, Illinois. On opening day, sales were three hundred and sixty-six dollars. By the end of the year, he had a handful of locations.

Kroc's genius was not the burgers. It was the system. Every restaurant had to serve the same food, in the same way, with the same taste. A hamburger in Illinois must taste exactly the same as one in California. Even the french fries were controlled by a formula, with exact potato sizes and frying times. Franchisees had to follow strict rules, and attend a training center that he called Hamburger University.

He also refused to compromise on cleanliness. He demanded that parking lots be swept, and windows washed. He fired owners who cut corners. His motto was, quality, service, cleanliness, and value.

By nineteen sixty, there were more than two hundred restaurants. But the profits were small, because the brothers took a share and franchisees kept most of the rest. Kroc was almost broke again.

### 05_cap5
The turning point came from a man named Harry Sonneborn, a financial expert. He told Kroc that he was not in the hamburger business. He was in the real estate business. Instead of just selling franchises, the company should buy or lease the land under each restaurant, and rent it to franchisees at a profit.

It was a brilliant idea. The company would earn rent from every location, whether or not the franchisee made money. And as the land became more valuable, so did Kroc's company.

Kroc then wanted full control. In nineteen sixty-one, he offered the McDonald brothers two point seven million dollars to buy them out entirely. After taxes, each brother would get one million. They agreed, because they were tired of the fight.

But there was a catch. The brothers had asked for a royalty, one percent of future sales, as a handshake agreement. Kroc never put it in writing. And he refused to pay it. The brothers also lost the right to use their own name on their original restaurant in San Bernardino. They renamed it The Big M, and Kroc opened a new McDonald's a few blocks away, which drove them out of business.

### 06_cap6
He worked sixteen-hour days into his seventies. He liked to say that he was not a smart man, but he was persistent, and that nothing in the world could take the place of persistence. Talent would not, and genius would not, and education would not.

Kroc grew the company with relentless energy. In nineteen sixty-five, McDonald's went public. The share price soared. In nineteen sixty-seven, it opened its first restaurants outside the United States, in Canada and Puerto Rico. In nineteen sixty-eight, the Big Mac appeared. Ronald McDonald, the clown, became a symbol for children.

By nineteen seventy-two, there were over two thousand restaurants. Kroc himself became a multimillionaire, and he later bought the San Diego Padres baseball team. He gave away much of his money to charity.

Ray Kroc died on January fourteenth, nineteen eighty-four, at the age of eighty-one. By that time, McDonald's had over seven thousand restaurants. Today, there are about forty thousand, in more than a hundred countries.

Richard McDonald lived to age eighty-nine, and he never complained in public. He said that he was proud of what they had built. But history remembers Kroc as the founder, and the brothers as the footnote.

### 07_lesson
Ray Kroc's story contains two lessons, and one warning. The first lesson is that it is never too late. Kroc started McDonald's at fifty-two, after a lifetime of failures. The second is that the system matters more than the product. He did not invent the burger, but he turned it into a machine that could be copied a thousand times.

The warning is that success built on a broken promise leaves a shadow. Kroc became a legend, but the brothers whose idea he used never got their share.
