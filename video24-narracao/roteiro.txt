### 00_hook
Nineteen twelve. A small, white-haired man of eighty-two is carried in a wheelchair into a private railroad car. The train begins to roll, and at the front of the line waits a crowd that has traveled for days to see it. A brass band plays. The engine crosses a bridge that seems to float on the open sea, and rolls into the city of Key West, a hundred and fifty miles from the mainland.

Newspapers had called the project Flagler's Folly. For seven years, hurricanes had destroyed his work. Hundreds of men had died building it. Experts said it was impossible to build a railroad across the ocean.

The old man at the window is Henry Flagler. Fifty years earlier, he was a failed salt merchant who was almost broke. He became the partner of John D. Rockefeller, helped found Standard Oil, and then used his fortune to turn a swamp into the state of Florida. This is the story of the man who invented Florida.

### 01_cap1
Henry Morrison Flagler was born on January second, eighteen thirty, in Hopewell, New York. His father was a poor Presbyterian minister, and the family could barely pay the bills. At fourteen, with only an eighth-grade education, Henry left home with a few dollars in his pocket. He walked to Ohio, to live with his half-brother and work in a store.

He was a hard worker, and a natural at business. He earned just five dollars a month, plus room and board, but within a few years he had saved enough to become a partner. He then married Mary Harkness, whose family was wealthy. Through the Harkness family, he met a young bookkeeper from Cleveland called John D. Rockefeller.

In the eighteen fifties, Flagler tried to make his own fortune in the salt business. He opened a salt works in Michigan. For a time, it thrived. Then the Civil War ended, prices collapsed, and the business went bankrupt. Flagler lost everything, and a part of the family savings. He went back to Ohio, owing money.

### 02_cap2
Flagler was thirty-five, and nearly broke. He returned to Ohio and worked as a grain broker. One day, he met Rockefeller again, who was starting an oil refinery. Rockefeller needed money. Flagler persuaded his wealthy relatives, the Harkness family, to lend him a hundred thousand dollars.

In eighteen seventy, Rockefeller, Flagler, and others founded Standard Oil. Flagler was a partner, and the company's top dealmaker. He understood railroads, and he negotiated secret deals with them. In exchange for guaranteeing large shipments of oil, Standard Oil paid lower rates than any competitor. These rebates gave it a huge advantage, and helped it swallow most of its rivals.

The two men were an odd pair. Rockefeller was cautious, quiet, and obsessed with numbers. Flagler was outgoing, ambitious, and loved big ideas. They shared an office in Cleveland, and ate lunch together every day, walking home side by side in the evening to talk about the business.

Rockefeller later called Flagler the brains of the early firm. He said, the idea of Standard Oil was Flagler's. By the early eighteen eighties, the company controlled most of the oil refining in the United States. Flagler was a multimillionaire.

### 03_cap3
But Flagler's life was shaken by sorrow. His first wife, Mary, was ill with tuberculosis. Her doctor recommended a warm climate for her health, and in eighteen seventy-seven, he took her south to Jacksonville, Florida. She died in eighteen eighty-one.

During the trip, Flagler noticed something. Florida was beautiful, but it was a wilderness. There were few good hotels, and the railroads were slow and primitive. He believed that Florida could become a winter paradise for rich Northerners, if there were comfortable trains and elegant hotels.

He remarried in eighteen eighty-three, and took his new wife, Ida Alice, to St. Augustine, on the northeast coast. It was a charming, sleepy old Spanish town. He saw an opportunity. He was fifty-three years old, one of the richest men in America, and tired of oil. He decided to start a new life.

### 04_cap4
In eighteen eighty-five, Flagler began building the Hotel Ponce de Leon in St. Augustine. It was a palace of Spanish-style architecture, with towers, courtyards, and stained glass by Louis Comfort Tiffany. It was one of the first buildings in America to be lit by electricity throughout, and Flagler hired Thomas Edison to install it. The hotel opened in eighteen eighty-eight, and was an instant success.

But the guests needed a way to reach the hotel. The local railroad was a mess. So Flagler bought it, and rebuilt it, widened the tracks, and replaced the bridges. Then he began to extend the line south, down the east coast of Florida.

To fill his hotels, Flagler advertised heavily across the North. Posters showed palm trees, sunny beaches, and orange groves, at a time when New York was frozen in snow. He sold more than a hotel room. He sold a dream of winter sunshine, and people believed it.

Town by town, the railway turned wilderness into settlements. At Daytona, at Palm Beach, and at Miami, he built hotels, laid out streets, and financed water systems, hospitals, and churches. In eighteen ninety-four, he built a vast resort on Palm Beach called the Royal Poinciana, then the largest wooden hotel in the world.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen ninety-six, Flagler's railroad reached a tiny settlement on the Miami River. A local landowner, Julia Tuttle, had written to Flagler offering him land if he would extend the railway. Flagler hesitated. Then a freeze destroyed crops across Florida, except in Miami, where orange blossoms were found on Tuttle's trees. She sent him a blossom as proof. He sent his surveyors.

When the train arrived in April eighteen ninety-six, Miami had about three hundred residents. Within a few years, it had thousands. Flagler dredged the harbor, built a hotel, and the city incorporated. The people wanted to name it after him. He refused.

His personal life grew darker. His second wife, Ida Alice, developed severe mental illness, and Flagler eventually committed her to an asylum. He asked the Florida legislature to make incurable insanity a ground for divorce. The law passed, and the press called it the Flagler divorce law. In nineteen-oh-one he married Mary Lily Kenan, a woman thirty-seven years younger.

### 06_cap6
Flagler's greatest project began in nineteen-oh-five. He decided to extend the railroad from Miami across the ocean, to Key West, the closest deep-water port to the Panama Canal, which was then under construction. It would be a hundred and twenty-eight miles over islands, swamp, and open water. Engineers called it impossible.

Over seven years, four thousand workers fought mosquitoes, heat, and storms. A hurricane in nineteen-oh-six killed more than a hundred men. In nineteen-oh-nine and nineteen-ten, more storms destroyed parts of the line. But Flagler pushed on, even as he turned eighty.

On January twenty-second, nineteen twelve, the first train rolled into Key West. Flagler, frail and in a wheelchair, said, now I can die happy. A year later, he fell down the marble stairs of his Palm Beach mansion. He died on May twentieth, nineteen thirteen, at age eighty-three.

The Key West line was an engineering marvel. Its centerpiece was the Seven Mile Bridge, which crossed open water on concrete arches. People said that Flagler had built a railroad that went to sea.

The railroad survived until the great Labor Day hurricane of nineteen thirty-five destroyed much of it. Today, its route is the Overseas Highway.

### 07_lesson
Henry Flagler's story holds two lessons. The first is that a second act can be bigger than the first. He started his Florida career at fifty-three, and built the state for the next thirty years.

The second is that infrastructure creates value. Flagler didn't just buy land. He built the railroad, the hotels, and the towns, and each one made the others more valuable. In doing so, he showed that to build a fortune, you may first have to build the place where it can exist.
