### 00_hook
September, nineteen eighty-two. Mexico announces that it can no longer pay its debts to foreign banks. The peso collapses. Banks are seized, money flees the country, and the president, in tears, tells his people that the party is over. Businessmen run for Miami, Houston, and Switzerland, taking everything they can carry.

One man in Mexico City does the opposite. He walks into the wreckage, and he starts to buy. A department store chain. A tire factory. A cigarette company. A bank. Companies that everyone else considers hopeless. His friends think he has lost his mind.

Ten years later he would buy the national phone company, and twenty-five years later he would become the richest man on earth.

He still lives in the same modest house where he raised his children. He wears a plain suit, and he has never owned a yacht. This is the story of Carlos Slim, the man who learned to buy when the world was afraid.

### 01_cap1
Carlos Slim Helú was born on the twenty-eighth of January, nineteen forty, in Mexico City. His father, Julián Slim Haddad, was a Lebanese immigrant who had arrived in Mexico as a teenager in nineteen oh two, fleeing the troubles of the Ottoman Empire, with almost no money and no Spanish. His mother, Linda Helú, was the daughter of another Lebanese family. Both of them taught their six children a sharp lesson about work.

Julián opened a small store in downtown Mexico City, called The Star of the East, selling clothes and household goods. It prospered, and during the Mexican Revolution, he bought real estate in the center of the city, when others were fleeing. It was a strategy his son would one day copy.

The father taught the boy about business like a teacher. At the dinner table, he talked about money, and he made his children keep a notebook of their savings. At the age of eleven or twelve, Carlos bought his first government savings bond. When he was twelve, he opened a bank account. He studied his father's accounts and learned to read a balance sheet before he was fifteen.

He also loved baseball, and he was a good student. He would later say that he learned from his father the difference between a rich man and a poor man. A poor man spends and then saves what is left. A rich man saves first.

### 02_cap2
Slim studied civil engineering at the National Autonomous University of Mexico, the country's biggest university. He was good at mathematics, and he taught algebra to other students while he was a student. But his real education was in finance. After school, he went to work at the brokerage houses of Mexico City, and he studied the stock market.

He was young, ambitious, and patient. He invested small amounts of savings, and slowly he built a portfolio. In nineteen sixty-five, at the age of twenty-five, he founded a real estate company, called Carso, a name that joined his own first name, Carlos, with that of Soumaya Domit, the daughter of another Lebanese family, whom he would marry two years later.

In those years, Slim made a habit of buying shares and small companies when they were cheap, and holding them for years. He sat on boards, learned industries, and always paid attention to costs. His managers learned that he could read a financial statement in minutes, and spot an error nobody had seen.

By the late nineteen seventies, he had assembled a small group of firms in real estate, construction, and mining. He was already a rich man. But he was about to learn, in the worst year of Mexican history, how to become a very rich man indeed.

### 03_cap3
Mexico in the nineteen seventies had been fueled by oil and borrowed money. The government borrowed billions from foreign banks, betting that oil prices would keep rising. But in nineteen eighty-two, oil prices fell, interest rates rose, and Mexico could not pay. The country defaulted. The peso lost most of its value. President López Portillo nationalized the banks, and the stock market crashed.

It was the worst crisis since the revolution. Businessmen panicked, and many sold their companies for almost nothing, trying to get their money out of Mexico. They believed the country was finished.

Slim saw something else. He had studied Mexican history, and he knew that crises came and went. He believed Mexico would recover, and that when it did, good companies would be worth many times their price. So he bought. In those years, he acquired a large share of Sanborns, a famous chain of restaurants and department stores. He bought a tire maker, a paper mill, a cigarette firm, a mining company, and a stake in a bank.

He paid low prices, and he used his own cash, with little debt. His rule was simple. Buy a good company when the owner is afraid. Never borrow more than you can pay in a bad year.

### 04_cap4
By the end of the nineteen eighties, his group, which he named Grupo Carso, had become one of the biggest in Mexico. But the biggest opportunity of his life was still to come.

In nineteen ninety, the Mexican government decided to sell Telmex, the national telephone company. It was an old, inefficient monopoly. Phones were scarce, expensive, and unreliable. Waiting for a line could take years. The government wanted to modernize it, and to do so, it needed new owners.

A group led by Slim won the auction. With partners from France Telecom and the American company Southwestern Bell, Grupo Carso paid about one point seven six billion dollars for control of Telmex. It was a huge sum. Many experts thought he had overpaid for a dinosaur.

He did not see a dinosaur. He saw a country of ninety million people who badly needed telephones. He set to work to modernize the network. He invested billions in digital switching, fiber optic cables, and new lines. Within a few years, the number of lines grew many times, and the quality improved.

The business became a cash machine. Telmex was almost the only phone company in Mexico, and for years it earned giant profits. With that cash, Slim began to expand.

### 05_cap5
In the nineteen nineties, Mexico suffered another crisis. In nineteen ninety-four, the peso collapsed again, and many banks and businesses went bankrupt. Once again, Slim saw an opportunity. He bought stakes in banks, retail chains, and industrial firms that were suffering.

At the same time, he turned his eyes to wireless phones. In two thousand, he spun off the mobile phone business of Telmex, and created America Movil. It started in Mexico, but quickly expanded across Latin America. He bought networks in Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, and many other countries. By the end of the decade, America Movil had become one of the largest mobile phone companies in the world, with hundreds of millions of customers.

Slim also kept buying other businesses. A famous construction firm, a giant bank called Inbursa, and shares in a long list of companies. He also invested in the United States, buying a large stake in The New York Times, which he later sold.

His business style was unusual. He did not use fancy offices, and he did not hold many meetings. He made decisions quickly, and he looked at every number. His managers said that he knew the sales figures of each of his stores by heart.

### 06_cap6
In two thousand and ten, a list published by Forbes made it official. Carlos Slim, with a fortune of more than fifty billion dollars, was the richest man in the world, passing even Bill Gates. A boy from a family of Lebanese immigrants, in a country many people considered poor, was now at the top of the world.

He was a strange billionaire. He did not buy yachts, or private islands, or fleets of cars. He lived in the same house in Mexico City for more than forty years. He wore simple clothes, and he went to the office every day. He said that money was a kind of tool, not a goal.

His wife Soumaya died in nineteen ninety-nine, after a long illness. It was a terrible blow. After her death, he built the Soumaya Museum in Mexico City, to house his huge collection of art, including works by Rodin, Dali, and Mexican masters. The museum is free to the public.

He also faced fierce criticism. Many Mexicans said that Telmex was a monopoly that kept prices high. Regulators and rivals accused him of blocking competition. Reformers pushed for new laws, and the company was forced to share its network. Slim answered that he had invested billions and had given the country a modern phone system.

### 07_cap7
In his later years, Slim began to step back from the daily business, and to hand over to his three sons and three daughters. He remained the chairman, and he stayed active well into his eighties.

He also expanded his charity work. Through the Carlos Slim Foundation, he financed health, education, and training programs. He said that the best way to fight poverty was to create jobs, and he argued that charity alone would never be enough.

He never stopped investing. He bought into real estate, mining, oil, and energy. He took a stake in a famous Spanish builder, in a Latin American airline, and in many other businesses. Always, he looked for firms that were out of fashion and cheap.

He liked to say that he did not retire, because retirement was a mistake. He called it a kind of living death. Work, he said, was what kept a person alive, and he promised himself he would keep working as long as his mind remained clear.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Carlos Slim?

The first lesson is to start saving early. His father taught him to save before he spent, and to read a balance sheet as a boy. Wealth is mostly a habit built over decades.

The second lesson is to buy when others are afraid. In nineteen eighty-two, and again in nineteen ninety-four, Slim bought great companies for low prices while everyone else was running away. Fear is the best discount in the world.

The third lesson is to keep your costs low and your debts small. Slim paid with cash, and he lived modestly. When the next storm came, he was ready.

And the last lesson is to think in decades. He bought a phone company that most people hated, and he waited years for it to pay. Patience, he showed, can be a strategy.
