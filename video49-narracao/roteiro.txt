### 00_hook
Cairo, the year thirteen twenty-four. The great city, one of the richest in the world, is used to wealth. Its markets overflow with silk, spices, and gold. But nothing prepares its people for the caravan that arrives from the south.

Sixty thousand men and women march into the city. Twelve thousand servants, each dressed in silk brocade, carry bars of gold. Eighty camels, each loaded with three hundred pounds of gold dust. Hundreds of attendants, with golden staffs. And at the center of it all, a king, riding in majestic calm.

For the next several months, he gives away so much gold in the city that its price collapses, and the Egyptian economy takes more than a decade to recover.

He is Mansa Musa, the ruler of the Mali Empire, and by most accounts, the richest person who ever lived. Today's economists struggle to put a number on his wealth, and many say that it cannot be measured. This is the story of the African king who made the world stop and stare.

### 01_cap1
The empire of Mali was born in the thirteenth century, in West Africa, along the upper reaches of the Niger River. Its founder, Sundiata Keita, a legendary warrior-king, united the Mandinka people and defeated a rival kingdom around twelve thirty-five. His descendants expanded the empire to the Atlantic coast in the west, and to the edge of the Sahara in the north.

Mansa Musa was probably born around twelve eighty. His grandfather's brother was Sundiata's heir, and his family belonged to the royal line. The word Mansa means king, or emperor. Little is known about his early life.

What we do know is that, around thirteen twelve, the throne of Mali was held by a ruler named Abu Bakr the Second. According to a famous account from the Arab scholar Al-Umari, Abu Bakr was curious about what lay beyond the Atlantic Ocean. He sent out a fleet of two hundred ships, and then a second one of two thousand, and they never returned. Abu Bakr then decided to go himself, and left Musa in charge. He, too, never came back.

And so Musa became the Mansa of Mali, around thirteen twelve.

### 02_cap2
Why was Mali so rich? The answer lies in the ground. In West Africa, there were great gold fields, in the regions called Bambuk and Bure. Mali controlled much of them. At that time, perhaps half of all the gold in the Old World came from West Africa.

And there was salt. In the Sahara, salt was as precious as gold, because people could not survive without it. Mali sat on the crossroads of the trade routes. Caravans of camels crossed the desert from the north, carrying salt, cloth, and horses. They returned with gold, ivory, and slaves. Mali taxed every ounce.

Mansa Musa also built a powerful empire. At its height, it covered an area larger than Western Europe, stretching for about two thousand miles from the Atlantic Ocean to the middle of the Niger. It included dozens of cities and many peoples. He ruled over millions of subjects, with an army of tens of thousands of soldiers, including cavalry, and an administration of governors and officials.

In the early years of his reign, he expanded further. He conquered cities such as Gao, and the legendary Timbuktu. These became the jewels of his kingdom, and centers of trade, learning, and faith.

### 03_cap3
Mansa Musa was a devout Muslim, and in thirteen twenty-four, he set out on the pilgrimage to Mecca, one of the five pillars of Islam. The Hajj was a journey of about four thousand miles across the Sahara and the Sinai, and it took more than a year.

But this was not an ordinary pilgrimage. Musa wanted the world to see the might of Mali. Contemporary sources say that his caravan included about sixty thousand people. Twelve thousand were slaves, who each carried a four-pound bar of gold. There were also thousands of soldiers, merchants, scholars, and officials. His senior wife was said to travel with five hundred of her own maids. Heralds went ahead of the caravan, carrying golden staffs, to announce the arrival of the king.

Along the way, he gave gold to the poor and to the leaders of the cities he passed, and he bought gifts and souvenirs. He spent so generously that wherever he went, the price of everything went up.

When he reached Cairo in July thirteen twenty-four, he stayed for about three months. The Egyptian historian Al-Umari, who visited the city twelve years later, found that people still talked about it. He wrote that the gold was so plentiful that its value fell, and for years the price of gold in Egypt remained low.

### 04_cap4
But the journey was more than a show. In Mecca and Medina, Musa met scholars, jurists, and architects from across the Islamic world. He decided to bring them home. On the return, he persuaded several scholars, poets, and architects to join him in Mali.

One of them was Abu Ishaq al-Sahili, an Andalusian poet and architect, who later designed the Great Mosque of Djinguereber in Timbuktu, and a palace in the capital. Their style used mud brick, in a structure that still stands today, more than six hundred years later.

Musa spent so much money in Cairo and on the road that, on his way home, he ran out. The story goes that he had to borrow money from merchants in Cairo, at very high interest. When he returned to Mali, he sent men to repay every debt in full.

The great journey did something more. It put Mali on the map. Before Musa's pilgrimage, Europeans barely knew about West Africa. After, merchants and mapmakers began to draw it. In thirteen seventy-five, the famous Catalan Atlas, made in Majorca, showed a crowned king on a throne, holding a gold nugget, labeled Musa Mali, lord of the negroes of Guinea.

### 05_cap5
Timbuktu changed under Mansa Musa. Before, it was a trading post on the edge of the desert. Under his rule, it became one of the intellectual capitals of the world. He built the Djinguereber Mosque, and founded Sankore, a mosque that grew into a great university. Thousands of students came to study law, astronomy, mathematics, and medicine. Scholars wrote and copied manuscripts, and in time Timbuktu held hundreds of thousands of books.

Gao and Niani also flourished. Mansa Musa brought a new level of organization to the empire. He founded schools, and he supported poets and jurists. He also brought a form of justice that travelers praised. The Moroccan traveler Ibn Battuta, who visited Mali about thirty years after Musa's reign, wrote that the people hated injustice more than anything, and that there was complete security throughout the land. A traveler, he said, had no reason to fear robbers.

Musa himself was known as a wise and generous ruler. He was also a master of ceremony. In his court, he sat on a throne under a dome of silk, with a gold-headed spear beside him, and thousands of soldiers stood at attention. Everyone approached him on their knees.

### 06_cap6
How rich was Mansa Musa? It is almost impossible to say. In two thousand and twelve, a website called Celebrity Net Worth estimated his wealth at four hundred billion dollars, a number that made headlines around the world. Historians, however, caution that we cannot translate medieval wealth into modern dollars. What we know is that he controlled the world's largest source of gold, and that his empire was among the biggest and richest of its time.

Time magazine, in a list of the richest people in history, put him at the top. Many economists, asked about the question, say that no one has ever had so large a share of the world's resources.

But the wealth had a price. Mali was an empire built on trade routes and the labor of enslaved people, and Musa's pilgrimage also inflated prices in the Middle East. Some historians argue that the gold glut hurt the Egyptian economy so badly that it was hard to recover. Others point out that he used his wealth for education and architecture, which lasted far longer than the gold.

Mansa Musa also left a mystery. After his pilgrimage, the records fall silent. We do not know exactly how he died.

### 07_cap7
Mansa Musa probably died around thirteen thirty-seven. His son Maghan took the throne, but the empire began to weaken. Later kings could not hold its vast territory together, and rivals grew. In the fifteenth century, the Songhai Empire, based in Gao, overtook Mali and became the greatest power in West Africa.

When the Portuguese arrived on the African coast in the fifteenth century, Mali was already a shadow of what it had been. But its fame remained. The legend of Mansa Musa shaped what Europeans believed about Africa, a land of gold and mystery.

In Timbuktu, his works live on. The great mosques still stand, and a library of ancient manuscripts survives. In two thousand and twelve, rebels in northern Mali burned some of the manuscripts, but many were saved by local librarians, who smuggled them to safety.

Today, historians view Mansa Musa as one of the greatest rulers in history. He showed that Africa was not a backward land, but a center of wealth, scholarship, and power long before the arrival of the Europeans.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Mansa Musa?

The first lesson is to control the source. Mali's wealth came from owning the gold and the trade routes. Whoever holds the scarce resource holds the power.

The second lesson is that wealth is amplified by reputation. Musa's pilgrimage made Mali famous in a single journey. A bold public act can change how the whole world sees you.

The third lesson is to invest in knowledge. Timbuktu's universities outlasted the gold. The things we build in minds last longer than the things we build in vaults.

And the final lesson is a warning. Even the richest empire fades if it does not prepare for the future. Mali's greatness was brilliant, but it depended on one man.
