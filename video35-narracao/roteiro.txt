### 00_hook
October, nineteen twenty-nine. High above Manhattan, a secret is about to be revealed. For weeks, workers have been assembling a strange steel needle inside the top of a skyscraper, hidden from every eye in New York. Across the island, a rival architect has just announced the tallest building in the world, and he thinks the race is over.

Then, on a cold morning, a crane lifts the needle through the roof, and in about ninety minutes it rises into the sky and locks into place. The Chrysler Building is now taller than anything ever built by human hands.

The man whose name is on that tower did not start in New York. He did not start in an office, or in a university. He started in a railroad shop in Kansas, hammering iron for five cents an hour.

He would take apart a car to see how it worked, save two failing companies, and build the third largest car company in America from nothing. This is the story of Walter Chrysler, the mechanic who built a skyscraper.

### 01_cap1
Walter Percy Chrysler was born on the second of April, eighteen seventy-five, in Wamego, Kansas, and grew up in the little railroad town of Ellis. His father, Henry, was a locomotive engineer on the Union Pacific line. To a boy in Ellis, that was the greatest job in the world. The trains were the only thing that connected the prairie to the rest of the country, and the man who drove them was the closest thing the town had to a hero.

Young Walter wanted to be just like him. He was a tinkerer from the start. He took apart clocks, locks, and anything with a spring in it. He once saved up to buy a pocket watch, not to tell the time, but to see what was inside.

When he finished school, his father gave him a surprising order. Before you drive a locomotive, he said, you have to learn to build one. So at seventeen, Walter began an apprenticeship as a machinist in the Union Pacific shops, earning five cents an hour. He swept floors, filed steel, and learned how every piece of a great machine fit together.

He was good at it. Very good. And while other apprentices dreamed of going home, Walter was learning a skill that would make his fortune later. He could look at any machine and understand how it was made, and what it would cost to make it better.

### 02_cap2
For the next fifteen years, Chrysler worked his way across the American railroad map. He took jobs in Texas, Colorado, Utah, and Iowa, always chasing a better position and a better machine. In nineteen hundred and one he married Della Forker, a local girl from Ellis. They would have two daughters, Thelma and Bernice.

By nineteen oh eight he was thirty-three years old, and he had become the superintendent of the Chicago Great Western Railway shops in Oelwein, Iowa. He had a good salary, a respected job, and a house. By any standard, he had already made it.

Then he went to the Chicago Auto Show.

There, under the lights, sat a beautiful Locomobile touring car, painted ivory, with a price tag of five thousand dollars. That was more than most families earned in several years. Chrysler had barely seven hundred dollars saved. But he could not stop looking at it. He asked a banker friend for a loan, and borrowed the rest, forty-three hundred dollars. When his wife heard what he had done, she was horrified.

He had not bought it to drive. He had a different plan. When the car arrived in Iowa, he took it apart, down to the last bolt. He spent months studying every gear, every spring, every casting. Then he put it back together. By the time he was done, he understood the automobile better than most men who built them.

### 03_cap3
Chrysler was now obsessed. In nineteen ten, the American Locomotive Company offered him a job as works manager in Pittsburgh, and he took it. But the car business was calling him, and soon a man named Charles Nash made him an offer he could not refuse.

Nash was the president of Buick, then the biggest car company in America. He wanted Chrysler to run the Buick factory in Flint, Michigan. The pay was less than Chrysler was already earning. His friends told him he was crazy to give up a safe job for an industry that might not last. Chrysler said yes anyway.

When he arrived in Flint in nineteen eleven, the plant was a mess. Production was slow, the costs were high, and nobody knew exactly why. Chrysler walked the floor every day with a notebook, watching, measuring, and asking questions. Within a short time, he had cut the cost of every car, and doubled how many came out of the factory.

His boss, a bold dealmaker named William Durant, noticed. In nineteen sixteen, Durant made him president of Buick, with a salary of half a million dollars a year. For a man who started at five cents an hour, it was a staggering number.

But with Durant, nothing was ever peaceful. Chrysler and his boss clashed over how to run the business. In nineteen nineteen, Walter Chrysler walked out, a wealthy man with millions in the bank. He was forty-four years old. He told his family that he was finished.

He was not finished.

### 04_cap4
Retirement lasted about a year. The banks had a problem, and they needed a mechanic.

Two big car companies, Willys-Overland and Maxwell, were sinking under debt. Both owed enormous sums, and bankers were desperate for somebody who could save them. They came to Chrysler, and he named his price. One million dollars a year, a fortune in nineteen twenty. They paid it.

Chrysler moved into the Willys plant, then took on Maxwell. He cut costs, fired the useless, sold what could be sold, and made peace with creditors. But in the middle of the rescue, he did something much more important. He quietly hired three brilliant engineers from another company, Fred Zeder, Owen Skelton, and Carl Breer. Together they were known as the Three Musketeers.

Their assignment was secret. They would build a car that no one had seen before, a car with a high compression engine, four wheel hydraulic brakes, and a smooth, fast ride, all at a price an ordinary family could afford. For three years they worked in an old house in Elizabeth, New Jersey, building prototypes and testing them on country roads.

In January of nineteen twenty-four, at the New York Auto Show, the first Chrysler Six was unveiled. Crowds mobbed it. Visitors said it drove faster and smoother than cars costing twice as much. In its first year, the company sold more than thirty-two thousand cars, the best debut in American history.

### 05_cap5
In nineteen twenty-five, Chrysler reorganized the old Maxwell company and gave it his own name. The Chrysler Corporation was born, and by the end of the decade, he was building the fastest growing car company in America.

But he was just getting started. In nineteen twenty-eight, he launched two new brands in the same year. One was a cheap car meant to take on Ford and Chevrolet. He called it the Plymouth. The other was a mid priced car called the DeSoto, aimed at buyers in between.

Then came his biggest gamble. Dodge Brothers, a famous car company, was for sale. It had a huge factory in Detroit, a great dealer network, and a reputation for toughness. The price was about one hundred and seventy million dollars, one of the biggest business deals in American history up to that time. Chrysler had been in the business of manufacturing cars for only a few years. Everyone thought he was reaching too far.

He made the deal.

Overnight, Chrysler Corporation had four car brands and a place among the giants. Together with Ford and General Motors, it became one of the Big Three, a position it would hold for the next ninety years. In nineteen twenty-eight, Time magazine named him Man of the Year. The farm kid from Kansas was on the cover of the most famous magazine in the world.

### 06_cap6
And now, with money and fame, Walter Chrysler wanted something more. He wanted a monument.

He chose a corner in the middle of Manhattan, on East Forty-second Street, and hired an architect named William Van Alen. The goal was simple, and absurd. Build the tallest building on earth.

Van Alen designed a skyscraper like no other. The walls were covered in gleaming stainless steel. At the corners, huge eagle heads, copied from Chrysler's own car hood ornaments, jutted out over the city. The top was crowned by a series of shining steel arches, with triangular windows glowing like the sun. It looked like a machine, and it looked like a dream.

But the race was tight. Just downtown, another architect, H. Craig Severance, was building a rival tower at forty Wall Street, and his team kept raising its height. At one point, Severance announced that his building would reach nine hundred and twenty-five feet, and claimed the title.

Chrysler and Van Alen had a secret. Hidden inside the crown of their tower, in the fire shaft, workers were building a long steel spire, which no one outside the team knew about. In October of nineteen twenty-nine, the spire was pushed out through the roof and bolted into place. The Chrysler Building rose to one thousand and forty-six feet. It was the tallest structure ever built, and Severance had been beaten.

Weeks later, the stock market crashed.

### 07_cap7
The timing could not have been worse. The Great Depression crushed the market for new cars, and Chrysler's sales fell sharply. But he fought back, as he always had. Plymouth, his cheap car, became a lifeline, and by the early thirties Plymouth had passed Chevrolet to become the second best seller in America.

In nineteen thirty-four came a different kind of gamble. Chrysler and his engineers launched the Airflow, a car designed in a wind tunnel, with a curved body that reduced drag, and a design that looked fifty years ahead of its time. Customers hated it. They said it was strange and ugly, and sales were poor. The Airflow became one of the great failures of the decade. Yet its ideas, aerodynamics and strong frames, would later become standard in every car on the road.

In nineteen thirty-five, Chrysler's health began to fail. He stepped back from the presidency, and handed the daily running of the company to his longtime lieutenant, K. T. Keller. The great mechanic was tired.

Walter Chrysler died on the eighteenth of August, nineteen forty, at his home in Great Neck, New York, at the age of sixty-five. He left a fortune of many millions, a car company that is still alive today, and a building that still defines the skyline of New York.

The Chrysler Building is no longer the tallest building in the city. But many people consider it the most beautiful.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Walter Chrysler?

The first lesson is to learn the craft before you try to lead it. Chrysler did not start in the boardroom. He spent years in the shop, with grease on his hands, and he knew every part of the machine. When he walked a factory floor, no one could fool him.

The second lesson is to take apart what you admire. When he bought that Locomobile, he did not drive it. He dismantled it, and he learned what made it great. Every great builder studies what came before.

The third lesson is to bet on great people. His Three Musketeers designed the car that built his company. Chrysler had the courage to give them time, money, and freedom.

And the last lesson is that a big dream is also a business. The Chrysler Building was a skyscraper, but it was also the loudest advertisement in the world. It told every person in New York that this company was here to stay.
