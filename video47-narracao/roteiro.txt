### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-five. A thirty-one-year-old lawyer in Pasadena, California, sits in a hospital room beside his nine-year-old son, Teddy, who is dying of leukemia. In those days, there was no cure. The little boy would die within months.

The father was already a divorced man, with almost no money. He had lost his first marriage, and now he would lose his child. One evening, after leaving the hospital, he walked the streets alone, sobbing. He later said that he had never been so low.

He could have stayed broken. Instead, he did something quiet and extraordinary. He rebuilt his life, one decision at a time. He built a law firm. He began to invest. He read, every day, for the rest of his life.

That man was Charlie Munger. He would become the right hand of Warren Buffett, and a billionaire many times over. And he would give the world a new way of thinking, a collection of mental models that would turn a whole generation of investors into students. This is the story of the man behind the man, and the best thinker in the history of American business.

### 01_cap1
Charles Thomas Munger was born on the first of January, nineteen twenty-four, in Omaha, Nebraska. His father, Alfred, was a lawyer. His grandfather, Thomas Munger, was a federal judge. It was a family that valued reading, argument, and honesty.

Omaha was also the hometown of another young man, Warren Buffett, who was six years younger. As a boy, Charlie worked at a grocery store owned by Buffett's grandfather, Ernest Buffett, for two dollars a day. Neither of them knew yet that their lives would be linked.

Young Charlie was a fierce reader. He devoured biographies, and he adored one man above all, Benjamin Franklin. Franklin's mixture of curiosity, practical wisdom, and humor shaped him. For the rest of his life, he would quote Poor Richard's Almanack, and he named his approach to life after a spirit of constant learning.

He studied mathematics at the University of Michigan, and then, in nineteen forty-two, he joined the Army Air Corps during the Second World War. The army sent him to study meteorology at Caltech, and he served as a weather officer. After the war, with no college degree, he talked his way into Harvard Law School, and he graduated in nineteen forty-eight, near the top of his class.

### 02_cap2
Munger moved to California, where he was married, and began to practice law in Los Angeles. He was brilliant, and in nineteen sixty-two, he left to form his own firm, Munger, Tolles and Olson. It was successful. But he had never been interested in billing hours. He wanted to build wealth.

His first great financial moves were in real estate. He designed and built small housing projects in Los Angeles, and he made his first real money. At the same time, he started a small investment partnership, Wheeler, Munger and Company, in nineteen sixty-two. He was fantastically good at it. Over the next fourteen years, until nineteen seventy-five, his partnership compounded at about nineteen percent a year, compared with five percent for the market.

But he was a shrewd observer of risk. His partnership went through terrible years, too. In nineteen seventy-three and seventy-four, when the stock market collapsed, his fund lost more than half its value. He later called it the worst period of his investing life. But he never gave up. He told others that if you could not stomach a fifty percent fall, you should not be in the market.

Through these decades, there was a quiet thread. In nineteen fifty-nine, at a dinner party in Omaha, he had been introduced to Warren Buffett. The two men talked for hours.

### 03_cap3
Buffett and Munger became close friends. They talked by phone almost every day, for decades. Buffett, then running his own partnership, was always looking for investments. Munger had a different view of what a great business was.

Buffett had learned from his teacher, Benjamin Graham, to buy cheap, mediocre companies, which he called cigar butts, with one last puff of value. Munger argued that this was a mistake. It was far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.

Munger changed Buffett's thinking. Together, they began to look for businesses with a durable advantage, a moat, that could earn high returns for decades. They found See's Candies, a California chocolate company, in nineteen seventy-two. They paid twenty-five million dollars for it. Over the years, it generated billions in cash.

In nineteen seventy-eight, Munger became the vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway, the textile company that Buffett had turned into an investment vehicle. He closed his own partnership in nineteen seventy-six and moved into a full partnership with Buffett.

For the next forty-five years, the two men ran Berkshire together. Buffett was the public face. Munger was the sharp-tongued conscience, who warned against stupidity, envy, and greed.

### 04_cap4
Under their leadership, Berkshire invested in a string of great companies. They bought a stake in The Washington Post, in Coca-Cola, in American Express, in Wells Fargo, and in Gillette. They bought entire businesses like Geico, the insurance company, and later, the railroad Burlington Northern Santa Fe.

Munger had a remarkable ability to say no. He once said that investing was not about being clever, but about avoiding stupidity. He believed that most of the great fortunes came from a few big ideas, not from endless activity. He liked to say that Berkshire made its money by sitting on its rear end.

He also had a deep belief in the importance of mental models. He argued that a person who knew only one discipline, like economics, would see every problem as an economic problem, like a man with only a hammer sees every problem as a nail. Instead, he believed in a latticework of models drawn from many fields, psychology, physics, biology, history, and mathematics.

He gave a famous series of talks on the psychology of human misjudgment. In them, he listed more than twenty biases that lead people to bad decisions, from envy and social proof to the tendency to defend what we already believe. Many investors still study these talks today.

### 05_cap5
Munger's life was never only about money. He was a family man, with eight children, and a stepfamily. He was also a prolific builder of his own wealth, and a creative donor.

In nineteen seventy-nine, he joined the board of a hospital in Los Angeles, and he quietly gave a great deal to causes he believed in. He donated tens of millions of dollars to Harvard-Westlake School, and to the University of Michigan, where he funded a graduate residence designed with unusual windowless rooms, which later drew criticism. He funded a new science building at the Stanford Law School, and also a home for the Good Samaritan Hospital.

He made no secret of his habits. He was a voracious reader, and his children joked that he was a book with legs. He wore a plain suit, drove an old car, and ate at ordinary restaurants. In his late years, he lost the sight in one eye after a surgery, and he went blind in the other, yet he stayed sharp.

And he was famous for his sense of humor, and his blunt opinions. He called bitcoin rat poison. He said that he had nothing to add, when he had nothing to add. And when a young investor asked him for advice, he answered, simply: Be consistently not stupid, instead of trying to be very intelligent.

### 06_cap6
By the nineteen nineties and two thousands, the two men had become folk heroes. Every year, in Omaha, tens of thousands of shareholders gathered for the Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, which people called the Woodstock of Capitalism. Buffett would speak, and Munger would give short, sharp, funny answers, often beginning with the words, I have nothing to add.

Munger's own fortune grew. He was a major shareholder in Berkshire, and he also held a large stake in a Los Angeles company, the Daily Journal Corporation, which published legal newspapers and built software for courts. At the peak, he was worth about two billion dollars, although much of it he gave away.

In two thousand and eight, during the financial crisis, he and Buffett were criticized for holding large positions in banks. But they stood firm. They believed in the long term, and they were right again. Berkshire survived and grew.

By two thousand and twenty-three, Berkshire Hathaway was among the ten most valuable companies in the world, worth about nine hundred billion dollars. And the man who had once been a divorced young lawyer with a sick child had helped build it.

### 07_cap7
Charlie Munger died on the twenty-eighth of November, two thousand and twenty-three, in Santa Barbara, California. He was ninety-nine years old, just thirty-three days short of his hundredth birthday.

Warren Buffett, who had been his partner for more than sixty years, said that Berkshire Hathaway could not have been built without Charlie's inspiration, wisdom, and participation. He called Munger the architect of Berkshire.

Munger left behind a fortune of about two and a half billion dollars, and a body of thought that became a kind of philosophy. His collected speeches were published as a book called Poor Charlie's Almanack. The book sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and it is still handed from one investor to another.

He also left behind something quieter. He was a man who had lost a child, and who had not let tragedy turn him bitter. He believed that you must accept the world as it is, and keep learning, and keep working, and keep being kind.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Charlie Munger?

The first lesson is to never stop learning. Munger read every day, and he believed that the best investment was in your own mind. A person who does not read is crippled by his own ignorance.

The second lesson is to think in many disciplines. The world does not come in subjects. Use many mental models, and you will see what others miss.

The third lesson is to avoid stupidity. It is easier to win by not making mistakes than by being brilliant. Invert, always invert, he said. Ask yourself how to fail, and then avoid it.

And the final lesson is to be patient. He sat on his rear end for sixty years, and let compounding do its work.
