### 00_hook
Eighteen thirty-four. A sixty-nine-year-old German immigrant walks into his office in New York and announces that he is leaving the business that made him a millionaire. Employees are stunned. The fur trade is booming, and he controls most of it.

He sells everything. The fur posts, the ships, the trading routes across a continent. His rivals are puzzled, because it looks like retirement.

But John Jacob Astor is not retiring. He is making the smartest bet of his life, and it is a bet on dirt. When he dies, he will be the richest man in America, and the reason will not be furs.

### 01_cap1
John Jacob Astor was born on July seventeenth, seventeen sixty-three, in the village of Walldorf, in Germany. His father was a butcher, and the family was poor. There were four sons, and Johann Jakob was the youngest.

At sixteen, he left home with almost nothing. He went to London to join his brother, who made musical instruments, and he learned English. But London did not offer much. His brothers were already in America, and letters spoke of a new country with endless land.

In seventeen eighty-three, as the American Revolution ended, the twenty-year-old boarded a ship to Baltimore. He had a few flutes to sell and little else. Then, during the voyage, he met a fellow German who had worked in the fur trade, and who told him how much money could be made in beaver pelts.

The story goes that Astor listened all night. By the time the ship arrived, he knew exactly what he would do.

Furs were more than fashion. In those years, a good beaver pelt was a kind of currency. A hat made from beaver felt was the sign of a gentleman in London, Paris, and Vienna. The trade moved millions of pelts across the Atlantic, and the men who controlled the supply controlled a river of money.

### 02_cap2
In New York, Astor took a job beating furs for a dealer. He learned how to grade pelts, how to spot cheap ones, and how to bargain with the Native hunters and trappers. Then, in seventeen eighty-six, he opened his own small shop.

He walked the woods of upstate New York, buying furs directly from trappers and carrying them on his back. He traveled to Montreal, where the biggest fur markets were, and made a deal with the British trading companies.

His business grew. He sent furs to London, and he sold them for huge profits. By the seventeen nineties, he was one of the biggest fur dealers in the country. He married Sarah Todd, who brought a small dowry and a sharp business mind. She helped him judge furs, and she ran the shop while he traveled.

By eighteen hundred, he was wealthy. But he wanted to be bigger than the British.

He also made a bold choice. When Britain and America signed a treaty in seventeen ninety-four, Astor saw that the border forts would open to American traders. He quickly sent agents into the Great Lakes and the West, and built relationships with Native trappers who had traded with the British for generations.

### 03_cap3
In eighteen-oh-eight, Astor founded the American Fur Company. His plan was audacious: control every step of the trade, from the trapper in the forest to the buyer in China, where sea otter pelts sold for fortunes.

He decided to build a trading post at the mouth of the Columbia River, on the Pacific coast, and call it Astoria. His ships would carry goods around Cape Horn to the Pacific, take furs to China, and return with silk, tea, and porcelain.

But the plan was risky. One ship, the Tonquin, was destroyed in a fight near Vancouver Island. A land expedition crossed mountains, starving and lost. And then, in eighteen twelve, the war between Britain and America arrived. A British warship sailed toward Astoria, and the local managers, who were Scottish and Canadian, sold the fort to a British company for far less than it was worth.

The loss hurt, but the legend of Astoria did not die. Years later, the Oregon Territory would become part of the United States, and settlers followed his trail. The tiny town of Astoria still stands at the mouth of the Columbia River, one of the oldest American settlements on the Pacific coast.

Astor lost about four hundred thousand dollars. He was furious, but he did not give up.

### 04_cap4
After the war, Astor lobbied Congress to ban foreign traders from American territory. Congress agreed, and overnight his British rivals were shut out. The American Fur Company grew into a monopoly.

He used every tool. Agents offered whiskey to trappers, advanced credit to keep hunters in debt, and drove smaller competitors out of business. His company controlled most of the fur trade across the Great Lakes and the West.

But Astor saw something that others missed. Beaver hats were going out of style. In Europe, fashion was turning to silk, and the supply of beavers in America was already shrinking.

So in eighteen thirty-four, he sold the American Fur Company. The buyers thought they had won a prize. Astor knew they were buying a sinking ship, and within a decade the fur trade collapsed.

His critics hated him. Editors accused him of cheating Native hunters, of using alcohol to lower prices, and of crushing smaller traders. Astor answered that he was only following the market.

### 05_cap5
With his money, Astor turned to another business. In the eighteen-hundreds, New York was a small city at the bottom of Manhattan island. Astor looked at the maps and saw that it would grow north, because it had nowhere else to go.

He began buying land, farms, and empty lots on the edge of the city. He did not build on most of it. He simply leased it, usually for twenty-one years, so that tenants would build the houses and pay him rent.

When the city grew, rents soared. When a panic crushed the value of land, Astor bought even more from desperate sellers. When the tenants' leases ended, the buildings reverted to him.

He also built something to remind New York who he was: the Astor House, a grand hotel on Broadway, opened in eighteen thirty-six. It was one of the first luxury hotels in America, with running water and a marble lobby.

It was the perfect machine. The land made money while he slept, and every immigrant who arrived in New York became a customer.

### 06_cap6
Astor lived modestly in his old age. He was heavy, stubborn, and lonely. He kept a small circle of friends, including the writer Washington Irving, and he worked from morning to night.

When the Panic of eighteen thirty-seven struck, he foreclosed on mortgages and picked up property for pennies. He also loaned money to farmers who could not repay. In the end, he owned thousands of lots across Manhattan.

He suffered a stroke in his eighties and lost his ability to manage his business. He died on March twenty-ninth, eighteen forty-eight, with a fortune of about twenty million dollars, making him the richest man in the United States.

He left four hundred thousand dollars to found a public library in New York, the Astor Library. It later became part of the New York Public Library. And for generations, his heirs, the Astors, remained among the wealthiest families on earth.

His son William Backhouse Astor inherited the empire and doubled it. William's descendants built mansions on Fifth Avenue, and one of them founded the Waldorf Hotel, which later joined with the Astoria Hotel to become the Waldorf Astoria. In nineteen twelve, his great-grandson John Jacob Astor the Fourth died on the Titanic, the richest passenger on the ship.

Over time, the family became a symbol of American aristocracy. But the whole dynasty was built on one immigrant who arrived with a few flutes and a good idea.

### 07_lesson
John Jacob Astor made a fortune twice. First in furs, and then in land. He knew when to sell a good business, and he understood that the best investments are the ones that grow with the people around them.

His story has two lessons. The first is to be early. Astor bought land when it was cheap and empty, long before anyone else saw its value.

The second is to know when to leave. He sold the fur trade at its peak, and that single decision created the larger fortune.

And a third: wealth compounds quietly. Astor did not live in a palace or chase publicity. He simply owned things that grew in value, and he had the patience to let time do the work.
