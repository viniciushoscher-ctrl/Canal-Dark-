### 00_hook
Nineteen twenty-nine. A thirty-one-year-old Italian race car driver stands in the garage of a small workshop in Modena, with a handful of mechanics and a dream. He has never built a car. He has no factory, no investors, and no reputation as an engineer. All he has is a love of speed, and an almost reckless confidence.

He announces that he is founding a racing team, Scuderia Ferrari, named after himself. Its emblem is a black prancing horse on a yellow shield, which he has taken from the fuselage of a dead war hero's airplane.

Twenty years later, he will build his first car under his own name. Within a decade, those cars will be the most coveted machines on earth. His name will become a synonym for passion, beauty, and speed, and for the price of a single one, a family could buy a house.

But the man behind it all was a hard, secretive, and often cruel figure, who once said that he did not build cars for people to drive. He built them to win. This is the story of Enzo Ferrari.

### 01_cap1
Enzo Anselmo Ferrari was born on the eighteenth of February, eighteen ninety-eight, in Modena, in northern Italy. His family had a small metalworking business, making railway parts. His father, Alfredo, was a stern man, and his mother, Adalgisa, was warm. Enzo had an older brother, Alfredo Junior, whom he admired.

When Enzo was ten, his father took both boys to see a car race in Bologna. Enzo watched the roaring machines, and the drivers, and it changed his life. He decided that he would be a racing driver. He was not a good student, and he left school early, but he read the sports pages.

Then tragedy struck. In nineteen sixteen, his father and brother both died, within weeks of each other. His father of an illness, and his brother, probably of the same. Enzo was seventeen. Then came the First World War. He was drafted into the army, and sent to work with mules. In the great influenza epidemic of nineteen eighteen, he fell critically ill and nearly died.

He survived, and he came home to a ruined family business, with no father, no brother, and no money. He was twenty years old. But he had a dream, and he would not let go.

### 02_cap2
After the war, Enzo went to Turin, the home of the Italian car industry, and asked for a job at Fiat. They turned him down. He said later that he stood on the sidewalk and cried.

He found work with a small company, which converted trucks into small cars, and soon he got a job as a test driver. He started to race. In nineteen nineteen, he took part in his first race, the Targa Florio, in Sicily, and finished ninth. In nineteen twenty, he joined Alfa Romeo, then a rising name in the racing world, and became one of its drivers.

He won several races, and in nineteen twenty-three, at the race track at Ravenna, he won a major prize. There he met the parents of Francesco Baracca, a famous Italian air force pilot, who had died in the war. The pilot's mother, Countess Paolina Baracca, gave Enzo a gift. She told him to put her son's emblem, a prancing horse, on his cars, and it would bring him good luck.

Enzo took the horse, and he added a yellow background, the color of his hometown. It would become one of the most famous logos in the world.

But Enzo was a good racing driver, not a great one. He knew his limits. He had a different gift. He could spot talent, and he could organize a team.

### 03_cap3
In nineteen twenty-nine, at the age of thirty-one, Enzo Ferrari founded Scuderia Ferrari in Modena. It was a racing team that ran Alfa Romeo cars, and also provided support to wealthy amateur drivers who wanted to race. The brilliant drivers of the era, like Tazio Nuvolari, drove for the team.

For a decade, the Scuderia was a success, and Enzo's reputation grew. But in nineteen thirty-nine, as war loomed, Enzo and Alfa Romeo fell out, and he left. He was told not to use the Ferrari name in racing for four years. He started a company that made machine tools, and during the war it produced parts for the Italian military.

In nineteen forty-four, Allied bombers hit his factory in Modena, and he had to rebuild it, moving the operation to the nearby town of Maranello, which would become the home of Ferrari. The war ended in nineteen forty-five. He was forty-seven, with a small factory, a few workers, and a burning wish to build a car of his own.

In nineteen forty-seven, the first car to carry the Ferrari name, the one hundred and twenty-five S, left the factory in Maranello. It had a small, twelve-cylinder engine, designed by Gioacchino Colombo. It was a sports car, and it raced in the Italian Mille Miglia, and quickly started to win.

### 04_cap4
Enzo Ferrari had a simple business plan. He sold road cars to fund racing. A wealthy customer would pay a lot for a Ferrari, and the money would pay for the race team. For Enzo, the road cars were a means to an end.

In nineteen fifty, Ferrari entered the new Formula One World Championship, and he has remained in it ever since. It is the only team that has been in Formula One since its beginning. In nineteen fifty-one, Jose Froilan Gonzalez gave Ferrari its first Grand Prix victory, at Silverstone, and Enzo cried. He said that he had killed his mother. He had beaten Alfa Romeo, the company that once employed him.

In nineteen fifty-two and nineteen fifty-three, Alberto Ascari won two world titles for Ferrari. In the nineteen fifties and sixties, Ferrari also dominated sports car racing, with many victories in the twenty-four hours of Le Mans.

Enzo would sit in his office in Maranello, in dark glasses, and manage drivers and engineers by telephone. He rarely attended races. He was said to be afraid of flying, and of crowds. But he knew every detail.

### 05_cap5
The cost of winning was high. In those years, racing was deadly. Ferrari lost many drivers, and Enzo was criticized for his cold response. He famously said that he treated drivers like horses, replacing them when they died.

In nineteen fifty-seven, during the Mille Miglia, a Ferrari driven by the Spanish nobleman Alfonso de Portago crashed, and killed him, his co-driver, and nine spectators, including five children. The Italian government banned the race, and the Vatican called Enzo a Saturn who devours his children. He was charged with manslaughter, and was later cleared.

His private life was also hard. In nineteen thirty-one, he and his wife Laura had a son, Alfredo, called Dino. Dino grew up to be a talented engineer, and Enzo loved him dearly. But Dino fell ill with muscular dystrophy, and died in nineteen fifty-six, at the age of twenty-four. Enzo was shattered. He visited the grave every day, and he named a line of cars Dino in his memory.

Enzo also had a secret second family. He had a son, Piero, with a woman named Lina Lardi. Piero was born in nineteen forty-five, but Enzo kept him hidden until after Laura's death in nineteen seventy-eight.

### 06_cap6
By the early nineteen sixties, Ferrari was famous, but the company was in financial trouble. Racing was expensive, and the road car business was small. Enzo needed a partner.

In nineteen sixty-three, the American carmaker Ford made an offer. Henry Ford the Second wanted to buy Ferrari, and use its racing fame to boost his brand. For a time, the talks were serious. But when Ford demanded control of the racing team, Enzo walked away, and insulted Ford personally. The humiliated Ford decided to build a car to beat Ferrari at Le Mans. The result was the Ford GT forty, which won in nineteen sixty-six, and ended Ferrari's streak. The story was made into the film Ford v Ferrari.

Then, in nineteen sixty-nine, Enzo sold fifty percent of the company to Fiat, and kept control of the racing team. The money saved the business. The Ferrari road car line, with models like the Daytona, the Testarossa, and the F-forty, became the world's most desired cars.

Enzo, however, did not care for luxury. He lived simply in Modena. He ate at the same restaurant, and wore dark glasses and a dark suit.

### 07_cap7
In his last years, Enzo Ferrari was an old man, but still the master of Maranello. In nineteen eighty-seven, the final car he approved, the F-forty, was unveiled to celebrate the company's fortieth anniversary. It was the fastest road car of its time.

He died on the fourteenth of August, nineteen eighty-eight, at the age of ninety, in Modena. His funeral was private, and the news was only announced after the burial, as he had wished.

After his death, Ferrari kept growing. In two thousand and fifteen, it became a separate listed company, and today it is worth tens of billions of dollars. Its cars still win in Formula One, and in the world's great races. And each year, it builds a small number of cars, kept deliberately scarce, so that each one is a prize.

Enzo Ferrari was admired and hated, but never ignored. He called himself a man who could not be satisfied. He said that his best car was always the next one.

His name became more than a car. It became a symbol of speed, and of Italian passion.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Enzo Ferrari?

The first lesson is to turn grief into purpose. Enzo lost his father, his brother, and his son. He poured the pain into work, and built something that has outlived him.

The second lesson is to use your money to fund your passion. Enzo sold road cars to pay for racing. Know what your real mission is, and make every dollar serve it.

The third lesson is to protect your brand. Ferrari built fewer cars than the market wanted. Scarcity made every car a dream.

And the final lesson is a warning. Obsession can be a gift and a curse. Enzo won everything, but he paid for it in human costs. Be driven, but do not forget the people around you.
