### 00_hook
June, nineteen sixty-one. On a racetrack on the Isle of Man, a small island in the middle of the Irish Sea, the most famous motorcycle race in the world is reaching its end. For decades, British and Italian riders have won every trophy. Japanese bikes, the experts say, are toys.

Then the finish line comes into view, and the first five motorcycles to cross it are all from the same company. Five Hondas, in the first five places. The crowd is stunned.

Two years earlier, the company had entered this race for the first time, and the crowd had treated it as a curiosity. And fifteen years earlier, it did not exist. Its founder was a man with grease on his hands, who had once been a boy sweeping floors in a repair shop, who had lost a factory to an earthquake and a bombing, and who had spent a whole year doing nothing but drinking and staring at the wall.

His name was Soichiro Honda, and he built two empires, from the repair of bicycles and from the stubbornness of a man who refused to be told that something could not be done.

### 01_cap1
Soichiro Honda was born on the seventeenth of November, nineteen oh six, in a small village near the city of Hamamatsu, in central Japan. His father, Gihei, was a blacksmith who also repaired bicycles. His mother, Mika, was a weaver. The family was poor, and Soichiro grew up in the smell of hot iron and oil.

He was not a good student. He was bad at reading and writing, and he was often in trouble. Later in life, he said that he never liked sitting in a classroom, because learning from books bored him. He wanted to touch things, to take them apart, and to put them together.

When he was about eight, he saw a car for the first time. It was a rare thing in a country village. The car leaked a little oil as it passed, and the boy ran to the spot and put his fingers in the oil, and smelled it. He said later that this was the moment he knew what he wanted to do with his life.

At the age of fifteen, in nineteen twenty-two, he left school and went to Tokyo, to work as an apprentice at a car repair shop called Art Shokai. At first, he spent most of his time cleaning the floors and babysitting the owner's child. But in the evenings he watched the mechanics, and slowly he learned the trade.

### 02_cap2
Honda spent six years at Art Shokai, and he became very skilled. During the great Kanto earthquake of nineteen twenty-three, he helped save cars from a burning garage, and the owner noticed his talent. By the age of twenty-one, he was trusted to build a racing car, and he even won a race with it.

In nineteen twenty-eight, the owner gave him a great honor. He allowed Honda to open a branch of the garage in Hamamatsu, his hometown. Honda was just twenty-two years old. The business did well, and he hired dozens of workers.

But Honda was never content with repairing. He wanted to invent. At the age of thirty, he decided to make piston rings, the small parts that seal an engine, and he founded a company called Tokai Seiki. He took a big risk. He sold his wife's jewelry for the money, and he threw himself into the work.

Then he got a huge order from Toyota, and delivered the first batch of rings. It was rejected. Out of fifty rings, only three passed the inspection. Honda was crushed.

He did not blame Toyota. Instead, he went back to school, a technical college, to learn the science of metals, and sat in the classroom at the age of thirty-one. A few years later, he got his rings accepted, and Tokai Seiki grew to a large factory.

### 03_cap3
Then came the war. Honda's factory was drafted into making parts for military planes. In nineteen forty-four, American bombers destroyed part of it. In nineteen forty-five, an earthquake destroyed the rest.

Honda decided not to rebuild. He sold what was left of the company to Toyota for four hundred and fifty thousand yen. He was thirty-nine years old, and he was out of work.

For the next year, he did almost nothing. Honda called it the year of idleness. He stayed at home in Hamamatsu, and he drank sake, and he made a kind of homemade liquor. He sat in his house and spent long days looking at the walls. He said he was resting, and thinking.

But Japan was in ruins, and there was no gasoline and no trains. People had to travel to the countryside to find food, and they had no way to get there. Honda noticed that a lot of bicycles were being used, and he saw an opportunity.

He bought a small engine, a surplus military generator, and attached it to a bicycle. It was a loud, slow, bumpy machine, and the neighbors called it the putt putt. But it worked, and people wanted one. His wife even rode it to the market. He started to build the machines in a small wooden shack. He had found his path.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen forty-eight, Honda formally founded the Honda Motor Company, in Hamamatsu. A year later, he released the first complete motorcycle of his own design, the Dream D-Type. It was a good machine, but he was an engineer, not a businessman. He did not know how to manage the company, how to sell, or how to pay for the next model.

Then, in nineteen forty-nine, he met the man who would save the company. His name was Takeo Fujisawa, a quiet and clever businessman. They met at a dinner, and talked for hours. Fujisawa said he would take care of the money and the sales, and Honda could do what he loved best, the engineering.

It was one of the greatest partnerships in business history. Honda was the dreamer, the one who loved the shop floor and the race track. Fujisawa was the planner, the one who understood contracts, banks, and distribution. They shared the company, and they never fought over power.

In nineteen fifty-two, the company almost went bankrupt, after a huge investment in new machines. Honda, always bold, put in even more. His bet paid off. In nineteen fifty-two, he released the Cub, a small motor that could be attached to a bicycle, and it became a hit.

### 05_cap5
Then, in nineteen fifty-eight, Honda released the machine that made him famous. It was called the Super Cub.

It was a small motorcycle, simple and cheap, with an engine of fifty cubic centimeters. It had a step through frame, so even a person wearing a skirt could ride it, and a semi automatic clutch, so it was easy to learn. It was reliable, and it used little fuel. It was designed for ordinary people, delivery men, housewives, and students, not for rebels.

The Super Cub was a worldwide phenomenon. In America, Honda ran a famous advertising campaign with the slogan, You meet the nicest people on a Honda. It changed the image of motorcycles from gangs and leather jackets to ordinary families. Over the decades, the Super Cub became the best selling motor vehicle in the history of the world, with over one hundred million units produced.

At the same time, Honda decided to challenge the best in the world. In nineteen fifty-four, he announced that he would win the Isle of Man races, even though his company had never built a racing bike. People laughed. Five years later, in nineteen fifty-nine, his team entered for the first time. And in nineteen sixty-one, they took the first five places.

### 06_cap6
Honda was now ready for the next step. In nineteen sixty-two, the government of Japan wanted to limit the number of car makers, to protect the existing companies. Honda was furious. He rushed to build a car before the law passed.

In nineteen sixty-three, Honda released its first vehicle, a small truck, the T360, and then a sports car, the S500. They were small, they were fast, and they showed that the motorcycle maker could build cars. But the real test came in the early nineteen seventies.

In nineteen seventy, the United States passed the Clean Air Act, which demanded a huge cut in car pollution. The American car companies said it was impossible. Honda's engineers, led by a stubborn man, found a solution. They designed a new engine that burned fuel cleanly, without a catalytic converter. It was called the CVCC.

In nineteen seventy-two, Honda put it into a small new car, the Civic. A year later, the oil crisis arrived, and gasoline prices skyrocketed. Suddenly, everybody wanted a small, efficient car. The Civic became a smash hit, and in nineteen seventy-six, the Accord followed. Honda had become one of the top car companies in the world.

### 07_cap7
In nineteen seventy-three, at the age of sixty-six, Honda did something unusual in the business world. He retired. And Fujisawa retired with him. They said it was time for younger men to lead.

He did not disappear. He traveled around the world, visited factories, and talked to young engineers. He loved to say that he cared more about his employees than about the cars. Honda was famous for the white overalls he wore at work, and for being willing to yell at an engineer, and then to hug him. He disliked tradition, bureaucracy, and neckties.

In nineteen eighty-two, Honda became the first Japanese carmaker to build a plant in the United States, in Marysville, Ohio. It was a symbol of the new global company he had created.

Soichiro Honda died on the fifth of August, nineteen ninety-one, at the age of eighty-four. At his funeral, thousands of Honda workers lined up to say goodbye. At his request, there was no ceremony and no large gathering of business leaders. He asked only that his employees be allowed to come and say farewell.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Soichiro Honda?

The first lesson is that failure is part of the process. His piston rings were rejected. His factory was destroyed. His company almost went bankrupt. Honda said that success is ninety nine percent failure, and that only the one percent matters.

The second lesson is to find a partner who complements you. Honda was a genius of machines, but he knew his limits. Fujisawa gave him the freedom to dream, and the company grew because each man did only what he did best.

The third lesson is to build for ordinary people. The Super Cub was not glamorous. It was practical, simple, and cheap, and it conquered the world.

And the last lesson is to aim impossibly high. He declared that he would win the Isle of Man races before he had a racing bike. He said he would make the cleanest engine before anyone believed it could be done. A big goal is a good tool.
