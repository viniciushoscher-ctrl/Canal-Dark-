### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-nine. On a yacht in the Mediterranean, one of the world's greatest opera singers dances on the deck, and a Greek shipping magnate watches her from across the table. At the same table sits Winston Churchill, the guest of honor. Everyone on board senses that several lives are about to change, and that not all of them will end well.

The host is a man who arrived in Argentina as a refugee with sixty dollars in his pocket, and who would become the richest man in Europe. He owned tankers larger than anything afloat, an entire airline, a private island, and he married the widow of an American president.

His name is Aristotle Onassis. And his story is the story of how a poor Greek boy turned the sea into gold, and how gold could not buy him what he wanted most.

### 01_cap1
Aristotelis Sokratis Onassis was born around January, nineteen-oh-six, in Smyrna, a rich port city in the Ottoman Empire, which today is Izmir in Turkey. His father, Socrates, was a tobacco merchant, and the family was comfortable, with servants and a large house.

That life ended in September nineteen twenty-two. During the war between Greece and Turkey, the city was burned, and tens of thousands of Greeks were killed or forced to flee. Aristotle was sixteen. His father was thrown into prison, and the family lost almost everything. Young Aristotle bribed a Turkish guard to free his father, and managed to smuggle out a little of the family's money. Then he fled, penniless, on a ship.

He never forgot the smell of that fire. He told friends later that after Smyrna, he was never afraid of losing money, because he had already lost everything once.

### 02_cap2
In nineteen twenty-three, at seventeen, Onassis landed in Buenos Aires, Argentina, with about sixty dollars. He took any job he could find, and he slept in a cheap boardinghouse with four other men. He washed dishes, carried bags, and worked nights as a telephone operator, where he listened in on the calls of rich businessmen and learned how they traded. He studied English and Spanish, and wrote down every phrase he heard.

At twenty-one, he noticed that Argentinians loved Turkish tobacco, so he used his father's old contacts to import it. He sold tobacco to cigarette makers, and by the age of twenty-five, he was a millionaire.

But the real fortune came from the sea. In nineteen thirty-three, the world was in the Depression, and shipowners were so desperate that they sold freighters for the price of scrap. Onassis bought six Canadian freighters for twenty thousand dollars each, when each had cost a million to build. He was twenty-seven. Everyone laughed. Then the world recovered, and the freighters became gold. He learned a rule that he would repeat all his life: when everyone is selling, look at the price, not at the fear.

### 03_cap3
During the Second World War, his ships carried war cargo, and he made another fortune. After the war, he saw an even bigger opportunity. Oil.

At that time, tankers were small, around twelve thousand tons. Onassis dreamed of bigger ones, because the larger the ship, the lower the cost per barrel. In nineteen forty-eight, he ordered one of the largest tankers ever built, in a German shipyard. Banks laughed at him. A tanker that big, they said, could never find enough oil to fill it, or a port deep enough to receive it. Onassis answered that every great ship begins as a ridiculous idea. Then he ordered even larger ones, and soon he owned dozens.

He also avoided taxes in a way that was brilliant and controversial. He registered his ships under flags of convenience, in countries like Panama and Liberia, where taxes were almost zero and regulations were few. Competitors complained, and the United States once charged him with fraud over a ship deal, and he paid seven million dollars to settle the case.

By the early nineteen fifties, Onassis ran the largest private fleet in the world. He owned over a hundred ships, and he was one of the richest men on Earth.

### 04_cap4
His boldest deal came in nineteen fifty-four, and it nearly made him the master of the world's oil. Onassis signed a secret contract with the king of Saudi Arabia to carry all of the kingdom's oil, in ships flying the Saudi flag. It was called the Jeddah Agreement. If it worked, he would own the oil routes of the Middle East.

But it threatened the biggest oil companies in the world. The Americans protested, and the British and the Americans together began to attack him. Reportedly, American intelligence even investigated his plans. Tankers were refused port, and his rivals tried to ruin him with a boycott.

Onassis fought back. He used the press, politicians, and his own charm. But the deal collapsed, and the Saudis cancelled it in nineteen fifty-five. He had lost the greatest gamble of his career. It cost him millions, but he was still rich enough to buy something he had always wanted: glamour.

### 05_cap5
In nineteen fifty-four, Onassis took control of the casino of Monte Carlo, with a majority of the shares in the company that owned it. He moved into a palace, and he bought a luxury yacht, the Christina, which he named for his daughter. It was a former warship from the Second World War. Onassis rebuilt it with a mosaic swimming pool, and bar stools covered in whale skin, as he loved to joke to his guests.

He filled the yacht with stars. Winston Churchill sailed on it. So did Greta Garbo, and Frank Sinatra. And in nineteen fifty-nine, he invited the world's most famous opera singer, Maria Callas, and her husband. Callas and Onassis fell in love. She was the greatest voice of her generation, and he was the man who could buy anything, but he could not give her the family she wanted. Both left their spouses, and the affair filled newspapers for almost a decade.

His business grew, too. In nineteen fifty-six, he bought the Greek national airline, and renamed it Olympic Airways. He also bought a private island in the Ionian Sea, called Skorpios, and he turned it into a paradise.

### 06_cap6
Then came the scandal that shocked the world. In nineteen sixty-eight, Onassis married Jacqueline Kennedy, the widow of the assassinated American president. Callas was devastated. The marriage was seen as a business deal, and critics called it a sale of the American saint to a Greek tycoon. A prenuptial agreement gave Jackie a generous allowance, but it was hardly a love match.

Tragedy followed. In nineteen seventy-three, his only son, Alexander, died in a plane crash at the age of twenty-four. Onassis was destroyed. He lost interest in work, he aged overnight, and he began to drink heavily. His marriage with Jackie fell apart. She stayed in New York, and he went back to Callas for comfort. Newspapers reported that he was preparing a divorce.

His health failed. He died in Paris on March fifteenth, nineteen seventy-five, at the age of sixty-nine, from respiratory failure. He was buried on Skorpios beside his son. His daughter Christina inherited a fortune of about five hundred million dollars, but the fortune did not bring her happiness either.

### 07_lesson
Onassis' story holds two lessons. The first is that a crisis can be the best time to buy. He bought his first ships when no one wanted them, and a fleet built on despair became an empire.

The second is that a great fortune cannot protect a family from grief. Onassis bought almost everything, and he bought it better than anyone, but he lost the things that mattered most.
