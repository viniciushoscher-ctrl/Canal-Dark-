### 00_hook
Eighteen hundred and twenty-six. A fourteen-year-old boy stands at the door of a bankrupt steel factory in Essen, a small town in western Germany. His father has just died, leaving behind debts, a few worn-out machines, and a handful of workers who have not been paid. His mother cannot manage the business. Everyone tells the family to close it, and sell what is left.

The boy refuses. He leaves school, and he takes charge of the factory. He is fourteen. He has no money, no experience, and no friends in business. All he has is stubbornness.

In the next sixty years, he will turn that tiny forge into the largest industrial company in Europe. His cast steel cannons will arm kings and emperors. His railway wheels will carry trains across continents. And his family name, Krupp, will become both a symbol of German power, and a stain on the history of the twentieth century.

This is the story of Alfred Krupp, the cannon king, and the dark empire he began.

### 01_cap1
Alfred Krupp was born on the twenty-sixth of April, eighteen twelve, in Essen, a small city in the Ruhr region. His family had lived there for generations. His grandmother, Helene Amalie Krupp, was a formidable widow who had started a trade in groceries and colonial goods, and had invested in a small iron company.

His father, Friedrich Krupp, was a dreamer. In eighteen eleven, he founded a factory to make cast steel, a material that the English had mastered, but which Germany could not produce. Friedrich believed that he could discover the secret. He poured his money, and the money of his family, into the project. But the steel was inconsistent, the customers were few, and the debts piled up.

Friedrich became ill, and weak. He died on the eighth of October, eighteen twenty-six, at the age of thirty-nine, in debt. Alfred was fourteen.

He had been to school, but he left after the death of his father. He later described that period as the hardest of his life. He worked from early morning until late at night, and sometimes ate only bread and potatoes. He signed his letters with the simple words, Alfred Krupp, manufacturer.

### 02_cap2
In those first years, the factory survived by making simple items, like tools, dies, and rollers for coins. Alfred spent every spare hour learning how to make better steel. He studied the English methods, and he conducted endless experiments, to find the right mix and temperature.

He also had a knack for invention. In eighteen thirty-eight, he developed a machine to make spoons, with a special roller, which became a modest hit. He sold it to makers in Germany and Austria. In eighteen forty-eight, when revolutions swept across Europe, he took over the whole business, with his brother Hermann, who left a few years later. By then, the company had only about sixty workers.

Alfred had two obsessions, quality, and control. He insisted on the highest standards, and he fired workers who were careless. He also demanded complete loyalty. He wrote a long set of rules, called the General Regulations, in which every worker was expected to obey without question. In exchange, he offered something unusual for the time. He paid fair wages, and later built homes, schools, hospitals, and pensions for his workers.

This combination of paternalism and iron discipline would define the Krupp company for a century.

### 03_cap3
The breakthrough came with the railroads. In the eighteen forties, trains were spreading across Europe. They needed axles and wheels that did not break. Iron ones cracked, and caused accidents. Alfred came up with a seamless steel railway tire, made of a single piece of cast steel, with no weld. It was far stronger, and lasted much longer.

He demonstrated the product in the eighteen fifties. In eighteen fifty-one, at the Great Exhibition in London, in the Crystal Palace, he displayed a huge block of cast steel, weighing about two tons, which was much bigger than anything the British could produce. He won a gold medal. The judges were astonished that a small German firm had beaten the might of Sheffield.

From then on, orders poured in. The railways of Germany, Austria, Russia, and later the United States, bought Krupp steel. The factory grew rapidly. By eighteen fifty, it had more than a hundred workers. By eighteen sixty, it had about one thousand. The logo of three interlocking rings, which represented the seamless tires, became the symbol of the firm, and it still appears on the company's products today.

### 04_cap4
Then Alfred discovered his real market, weapons. In the eighteen forties, he had made a small cannon out of cast steel, and he noticed that it did not burst, as bronze or iron ones did. He saw that cast steel would make a stronger, lighter, and more accurate gun.

The Prussian army was slow to listen. But Alfred kept at it. In eighteen fifty-five, he showed a breech-loading steel cannon, in Paris, and the response was cool. In eighteen fifty-nine, Prussia, which was preparing for war, began to buy his guns. In eighteen sixty-four, in the war against Denmark, and in eighteen sixty-six, against Austria, the Krupp guns performed well.

Then came the great test, the Franco-Prussian War of eighteen seventy to seventy-one. The French army, equipped with bronze cannons, was outgunned by the steel Krupp artillery, which fired faster and farther. At the Battle of Sedan, the German guns destroyed the French lines. The victory united Germany, and made Alfred Krupp a national hero.

He sold his guns not only to Prussia. He sold them to Russia, Austria, Italy, Turkey, Japan, Belgium, and others, in more than fifty countries. He did not care about politics. He cared about orders. Armies on both sides of future wars would be armed with weapons made at Essen.

### 05_cap5
By the eighteen seventies, Krupp was the largest industrial company in Europe. Its factory at Essen was a city within a city, with its own police, fire brigade, and railway. It employed more than twelve thousand people at Essen, and about twenty thousand across all its sites. It produced steel, cannons, ships' armor, locomotives, and tools. It owned coal mines and iron mines.

Alfred himself lived in the Villa Hugel, a huge mansion with more than three hundred rooms. But he was a restless man, who did not enjoy luxury. He ate simply, worked long days, and wrote letters to his managers in an endless flow. He was also a difficult boss, stubborn and demanding. He suffered from poor health, and became increasingly isolated.

He was proud of his workers, and of the community he built. He constructed thousands of homes, and a hospital. But he also insisted on loyalty. He did not allow unions. He wrote that he would never accept a worker who challenged his authority.

Alfred Krupp died on the fourteenth of July, eighteen eighty-seven, in Essen, at the age of seventy-five. He left behind a company of tens of thousands of workers, and a family dynasty.

### 06_cap6
Alfred's son, Friedrich Alfred Krupp, took over, and continued to expand. The company built warships' armor, and helped to build the German navy. In the First World War, the giant Krupp gun, nicknamed Big Bertha, a huge siege howitzer, shelled the forts of Belgium and France.

After the war, the Treaty of Versailles banned Germany from making weapons. But Krupp found ways to continue in secret, and to hide its work abroad. When Adolf Hitler came to power in nineteen thirty-three, Krupp, led by Gustav Krupp, one of the greatest of the German industrialists, embraced the new regime. It built tanks, guns, submarines, and ammunition for the Nazi war machine.

The darkest chapter came during the Second World War. Krupp used forced labor, including prisoners of war, concentration camp inmates, and people from occupied countries. At least one hundred thousand people worked for Krupp against their will, and many died. In a branch of the company, conditions were so bad that survivors compared them to the camps.

After the war, the Allies tried Alfried Krupp, Gustav's son, at the Nuremberg trials in nineteen forty-eight. He was found guilty of using slave labor, and sentenced to twelve years in prison, and the confiscation of his property.

### 07_cap7
But the story was not over. In nineteen fifty-one, the American High Commissioner, John McCloy, released Alfried Krupp, and restored his fortune, in the early years of the Cold War, when West Germany was needed as an ally. Alfried rebuilt the company, and it became a leader in the German economic miracle. He agreed to sell his coal and steel holdings, and he promised to give up armaments.

In nineteen sixty-seven, Alfried died, and the family line ended. His son Arndt gave up his claim to the inheritance, and the firm became a foundation. In nineteen ninety-nine, Krupp merged with its rival Thyssen, to form ThyssenKrupp, one of the largest steel and engineering firms in the world.

Today, the Krupp name is a symbol. For some, it stands for German technical excellence, the cast steel, the railway tires, and the great engineering. For others, it represents the dangers of unchecked industrial power, and the way a company can be drawn into war and crime.

Alfred Krupp built an empire. His descendants lost it, in a tragedy of their own making.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Alfred Krupp?

The first lesson is to turn a crisis into a mission. At fourteen, Alfred inherited a bankrupt factory. He did not run. He studied, he experimented, and he saved it.

The second lesson is to master a single technology. Krupp became the best in the world at cast steel, and then found uses for it everywhere, from railroads to cannons.

The third lesson is to show the world what you can do. At the Crystal Palace in London, a block of steel was enough to make his name.

And the final lesson is a warning. A company that serves any master, and sells to any buyer, can become part of terrible things. Success without a conscience can become a stain that no fortune can wash away.
