### 00_hook
September twenty-fourth, eighteen sixty-nine. On Wall Street, the price of gold is exploding, and brokers are screaming in the street as fortunes vanish in minutes. Men who were rich at breakfast are ruined by lunch.

Behind the chaos, in a quiet office, a small man with a black beard is watching the clock and saying almost nothing.

He has just cornered the gold market of the United States, and he is about to be hated by an entire country. His name is Jay Gould. And the strangest part of his story is that he still walked away with the money.

### 01_cap1
Jason Gould was born on December twenty-seventh, eighteen thirty-six, on a small farm in upstate New York. He was a thin, sickly boy with a quiet voice, and he hated the farm.

His father was poor, and Jay learned very early that nobody respects a poor man. He worked in a blacksmith's shop, and he studied at night by candlelight. When he was fifteen, he had already saved enough to buy a small set of books on surveying.

He walked from town to town, measuring land for farmers, drawing maps of counties, and learning who owned what. This was his first real education in power. A man who knows exactly where everything is can see opportunities that other people never notice.

At twenty-one, he used his savings and borrowed money to buy into a tannery in Pennsylvania. He worked hard, and the business grew. And then his partner, an older man, forced him out in a bitter fight. Gould never forgot the lesson: in business, the person who gets the first strike usually wins.

### 02_cap2
Gould moved to New York and discovered the railroads. They were the greatest machines of the age, and most of them were run badly. He began buying the shares of a small, failing line, and he learned how to turn almost worthless paper into power.

Then he found his real battlefield: the Erie Railroad. In eighteen sixty-eight, the richest man in America, Cornelius Vanderbilt, decided to take it over by buying up its stock. It should have been easy.

Gould and his partners, Daniel Drew and a flamboyant showman named Jim Fisk, had a trick. Whenever Vanderbilt bought shares, they simply printed more. The Erie company had a printing press, and they used it to create new stock out of thin air.

Vanderbilt bought and bought, and the number of shares kept growing. He lost millions. Furious, he got a judge to issue arrest orders, so Gould and his friends grabbed about six million dollars in cash and fled across the river to New Jersey, where they lived in a hotel guarded by cannons.

In the end, Vanderbilt settled. The newspapers called it the Erie War, and a thirty-two-year-old with a soft voice had just beaten the greatest tycoon in America.

### 03_cap3
But Gould was not satisfied. The next plan was bigger, and far more dangerous. He wanted to corner the gold market.

The idea was simple. If he could buy most of the gold in New York, the price would shoot up. Farmers in the West, who sold wheat for export, would benefit, and the government would not interfere. And all he needed was one thing: the promise that the Treasury would not sell gold.

So Gould and Jim Fisk got close to a man named Abel Corbin, who happened to be the brother-in-law of the president, Ulysses Grant. They gave Corbin gold, they flattered him, and they used him to whisper in the president's ear.

In September eighteen sixty-nine, they started buying. The price of gold climbed from one hundred and thirty to one hundred and sixty-two dollars. Brokers who had bet against gold were being crushed.

Then, on the morning of Friday, September twenty-fourth, Grant finally understood what was happening. He ordered the Treasury to sell four million dollars in gold.

The price collapsed in minutes. Hundreds of businesses went bankrupt, and the day became known as Black Friday.

Here is the twist. Gould had secretly sold his own gold the moment he sensed the danger. He kept his profit, perhaps ten million dollars, while Fisk and everyone else fought off angry mobs. The crowds wanted to hang them. And Jay Gould had already left.

### 04_cap4
After Black Friday, any normal man would have disappeared. Gould did the opposite. He started to collect railroads.

He took control of the Union Pacific, the great line that crossed the continent. He bought the Kansas Pacific, then the Missouri Pacific, and a web of smaller lines across the Southwest. Within a few years, he controlled about a ninth of all the railroad track in America.

He also bought Western Union, the company that controlled almost every telegraph wire in the country. He even bought a newspaper, the New York World, and used it to shape the news.

His method was always the same. He bought a weak railroad, made it look strong, then sold it at a high price. Or he forced a stronger competitor to buy him out at a premium.

His boldest move was against Western Union itself. Gould built a rival telegraph company, the American Union, and made it a threat. Western Union's owners panicked. In eighteen eighty-one, they let Gould buy their company, and he walked into the biggest communications network in the country almost without paying for it.

He did the same on the streets of New York. He bought the Manhattan elevated railways, the trains that ran above the avenues, and turned them into a machine that printed cash. Every commuter in the city was paying Jay Gould a nickel.

Critics called him the Mephistopheles of Wall Street. He barely seemed to care. At home, he was gentle and quiet, a man who grew orchids in a greenhouse and rarely drank or swore. The monster in the newspapers was, in private, almost boring.

### 05_cap5
Fisk had been Gould's loud partner, and in eighteen seventy-two, he was shot dead on the steps of a New York hotel by a rival over a woman. Gould lost his shield. From then on, he stood alone, and the public hatred came for him alone.

In eighteen eighty-six, the Knights of Labor, a powerful union, went on strike against his Southwestern railroads. For the first time, thousands of workers were fighting a railroad empire. Gould met the union leaders, and then he broke the strike. The union never recovered.

He is also said to have declared, in a famous line, that he could hire one half of the working class to kill the other half. Historians still argue about whether he really said it, but millions of people believed it. That is how his name was remembered.

There was another side to the hatred. In eighteen eighty-four, Gould's stock market fortunes crashed in a panic, and his enemies celebrated. He was said to have lost millions in days. Reporters wrote his obituary early.

Instead, Gould bought. He waited until panic had pushed stocks to the floor, and he picked up railroads at half price. When confidence returned, he sold them. It was the same tactic he had used at Black Friday: let everyone else be afraid, and be calm.

He was a master at one game, and the game was never about trains. It was about information. He knew which stocks were rising, which governments were weak, and which men would break.

### 06_cap6
But he could not buy his health. By his mid-fifties, Gould was sick with tuberculosis. He kept working until the very end, giving orders from his sickbed.

He died on December second, eighteen ninety-two, at the age of fifty-six. His fortune was about seventy-seven million dollars, which made him one of the richest Americans alive.

The public did not mourn him. Newspapers wrote that he had done more harm to the country than any other man of his time.

And yet his children inherited more than money. His son George tried to build the empire further and failed. His daughter Helen gave away millions, to schools, hospitals, and to the veterans of the Spanish-American War. Many Americans knew her as the kindest member of a family that history had decided to hate.

### 07_lesson
Jay Gould did not build anything lasting. He built fortunes out of other people's fear, and when he died, most of his empire dissolved.

His story has two lessons. The first is that information is power. A man who sees the future of one industry, one law, one election, one hour earlier than everyone else can win without a fight.

The second is the price. Gould had wealth, but he had no friends, no loyalty, and almost no one who would defend his name. A fortune built on tricking others can be large, but it is always fragile.
