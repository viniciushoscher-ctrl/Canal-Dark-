### 00_hook
November, nineteen sixty. On the night of the presidential election, a seventy-two-year-old man sits by a telephone in a house on Cape Cod, far from the cameras. He does not give speeches. He does not appear on television. But everyone in the room knows that the man by the telephone is the reason the night matters.

His son is about to become the youngest man ever elected president of the United States.

Thirty years earlier, that father had been one of the most talked-about financiers on Wall Street. He had made a fortune in stocks, in movies, and in real estate. He had run a government agency that was supposed to catch men exactly like him. And he had served as ambassador to Britain, until a single interview ended his political career.

He had one goal, and it was never to be rich. It was to build a family that would rule America.

This is the story of Joseph Kennedy, the man who bought his way into history, and paid for it with almost everything he loved.

### 01_cap1
Joseph Patrick Kennedy was born on the sixth of September, eighteen eighty-eight, in East Boston, Massachusetts. His grandparents had fled the Irish famine, and arrived in America with nothing. His father, Patrick Joseph Kennedy, rose from a dockworker to the owner of saloons and a liquor importing business, and then to the state senate. He was one of the most powerful Irish politicians in the city.

But in Boston, being Irish and Catholic meant being an outsider. The old Protestant families, the Brahmins, ran the banks, the clubs, and the best schools. They tolerated the Irish at the back of the room. Young Joe grew up watching this wall, and he decided that he would not just climb over it. He would knock it down.

His father sent him to the Boston Latin School, the oldest and best public school in America, and then to Harvard. At Harvard, Joe was a good baseball player and an excellent businessman, but the exclusive clubs rejected him because of his name. It was a wound he never forgot.

He graduated in nineteen twelve, and took a job as a bank examiner for the state of Massachusetts. It was a small job, but it taught him a great lesson. He learned how a bank really worked, where the money was, and how little most bankers understood about it.

### 02_cap2
In nineteen fourteen, an opportunity appeared. A small bank in East Boston, the Columbia Trust, was in danger of being swallowed by a larger rival. Kennedy, who knew the owners, raised the money to buy a controlling stake. At the age of twenty-five, he became the president of the bank. The papers called him the youngest bank president in the country.

That same year, he married Rose Fitzgerald, the daughter of John F. Fitzgerald, the popular mayor of Boston, known as Honey Fitz. Rose was educated, devout, and one of the most admired young women in the city. Together they would have nine children.

When the United States entered the First World War, Kennedy went to work at the Fore River shipyard, run by the Bethlehem Steel Company, as assistant general manager. There he met a young official from the Navy Department named Franklin Delano Roosevelt. It was a meeting neither man would forget.

After the war, Kennedy went to Wall Street. He opened his own office, and began to trade stocks for himself and for his friends. This was an era with few rules. A trader could join a pool, spread rumors, and push a stock up before selling at the top. Kennedy was brilliant at it. He had a gift for knowing when to buy, and, more important, when to get out.

By the middle of the nineteen twenties, he was a millionaire many times over.

### 03_cap3
But Kennedy's real ambition was not in stocks. He saw something new in American life. Millions of people were going to the movies every week, and almost nobody on Wall Street understood how the business worked.

In nineteen twenty-six he bought a small chain of theatres in New England, and then moved to Hollywood. He took control of a studio called Film Booking Offices, and then merged it with other companies. The result, in nineteen twenty-eight, was a new giant, called RKO Pictures.

He did not care about art. He cared about cost. He cut budgets, fired expensive executives, and turned out a stream of cheap pictures that made money. Hollywood people did not like him, but they respected how he worked. He also brought in a new technology, sound, and helped give the movies their voice.

During those years, he also began a famous affair with Gloria Swanson, the biggest star of the silent era, which his wife knew about and endured. He lost some money on her movies. But he did not lose his nerve.

By the time he left the movie business, he had made a profit of millions, and he had learned another lesson. The best businesses are those where the rules are not yet written.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen twenty-nine, the stock market was soaring. Everyone was getting rich. Taxi drivers gave stock tips. Shoeshine boys talked about the market.

According to the famous story, Kennedy was getting his shoes shined one day when the boy began to give him advice on which stocks to buy. Kennedy decided on the spot that when a shoeshine boy was giving tips, the market was too crazy to last. He went back to his office and began to sell.

Historians doubt whether it happened exactly that way. But the outcome is clear. Kennedy sold most of his stocks before the crash in October. When the market collapsed, millions of people lost their savings, and he kept his. Some say he even made money by betting on the fall. Whatever the truth, he came out of the Great Depression richer than he went in.

The crash taught him a deeper lesson, one that would shape the rest of his life. The system was full of cheats, and it would fall apart without a firm hand. He saw that the next fortunes would be made not by the speculators, but by the people who made the rules.

He did not yet know it, but the man who would give him the power to write those rules was already running for president. His old acquaintance, Franklin Roosevelt.

### 05_cap5
Kennedy supported Roosevelt, and gave money to his campaign in nineteen thirty-two. He expected a reward. But Roosevelt hesitated. Kennedy was a Wall Street speculator, and the public hated Wall Street.

In the meantime, Kennedy made another smart move. In nineteen thirty-three, as Prohibition was about to end, he arranged to become the American importer of famous British brands, including Dewar's scotch and Gordon's gin. When alcohol became legal again, his company was ready. It earned him millions. His enemies said that he had been a bootlegger during Prohibition. He denied it for the rest of his life.

Then, in nineteen thirty-four, Roosevelt made a stunning choice. He appointed Joe Kennedy as the first chairman of the Securities and Exchange Commission, the new agency created to regulate the stock market.

The press was shocked. Critics said it was like putting a fox in charge of the henhouse. But Roosevelt had calculated that a man who knew every trick would be the best person to stop them. And he was right. Kennedy ran the agency honestly and with energy. He introduced rules that forced companies to tell the truth about their finances, and he banned many of the tricks he had used himself. Wall Street grumbled. But the public began to trust the market again.

After a year, Kennedy resigned. He had done what the president needed, and his reputation was repaired.

### 06_cap6
In nineteen thirty-seven, Roosevelt gave him another post, the chairman of the Maritime Commission. And in nineteen thirty-eight, he gave him the job Kennedy had always wanted, the ambassador to Great Britain.

An Irish American from Boston, the grandson of a famine immigrant, now lived in a mansion in London and dined with the king. Rose and the children were photographed on the front pages of every newspaper. The Kennedys had become American royalty.

But the world was moving toward war. As Hitler's power grew, Kennedy took a position that would ruin him. He believed Britain could not win a war against Germany, and he supported appeasement, the policy of giving in to Hitler to avoid conflict. He told friends that democracy was finished in Britain.

When the Second World War began in nineteen thirty-nine, and especially during the bombing of London, Kennedy was seen as defeatist. Then, in November nineteen forty, he gave an interview to a Boston newspaper in which he said that democracy was all done in England. The headlines were devastating.

Roosevelt was furious, and Kennedy resigned. His political career was over. He would never hold public office again.

A man like Kennedy might have collapsed. Instead, he turned to something he trusted more than any office. His sons.

### 07_cap7
Joseph Kennedy Junior, the oldest, was the golden boy, the one his father expected to be president. In nineteen forty-four, he was killed when his bomber exploded over England during a secret mission. Kennedy was shattered. He turned his hopes to the next son, John.

In nineteen forty-one, he had approved a surgery on his daughter Rosemary, who was struggling with learning difficulties. The operation, a lobotomy, left her permanently disabled. She was sent away to an institution, and for years the family barely spoke of her. In nineteen forty-eight, his daughter Kathleen died in a plane crash in France.

Meanwhile, Kennedy kept building the machine. In nineteen forty-five he bought the Merchandise Mart in Chicago, then the biggest building in the world, for about thirteen million dollars. It became a source of income for the whole family. He invested in real estate, oil, and liquor, and by the nineteen fifties he was among the richest men in America.

And he used all of it to push John toward the Senate in nineteen fifty-two, and the White House in nineteen sixty. He hired the advisers, paid for the campaigns, and called in every favor he had. When someone asked him about the cost, he supposedly said that he would pay for a victory, but not for a landslide.

His son won. John Kennedy became president on the twentieth of January, nineteen sixty-one. But Joseph never saw his full glory. In December of that year, he suffered a massive stroke that left him unable to speak. He lived on for eight more years, silent, and he died on the eighteenth of November, nineteen sixty-nine, at the age of eighty-one. By then, he had buried three of his children.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Joseph Kennedy?

The first lesson is to know when to leave. In nineteen twenty-nine, Kennedy sold while everyone else was still buying. The hardest skill in any market is to walk away while the party is still going.

The second lesson is that the biggest opportunities are in new industries. He saw movies when Wall Street ignored them. He saw the end of Prohibition before it came. He always looked for the game where the rules were not yet written.

The third lesson is that your reputation is also an asset. When Roosevelt made him the head of the SEC, he used that chance to turn a bad name into a good one. And when he damaged it in London, it ended his career in a single week.

And the last is a hard one. Kennedy built an empire so that his sons could rule. He succeeded. But he paid a terrible price, and no fortune could buy back what was lost.
