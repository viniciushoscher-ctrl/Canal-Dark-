### 00_hook
January fifth, nineteen fourteen. Before sunrise, ten thousand men are standing in the freezing cold outside a factory gate in Detroit. They have heard a rumor that sounds impossible.

Inside, one man has just made a decision that newspapers around the world will call madness. He is about to double the wages of his workers, in a single day, for no reason his own investors can understand.

His name is Henry Ford. And the secret behind that decision did not make him poorer. It made him the richest industrialist in America.

### 01_cap1
Henry Ford was born on July thirtieth, eighteen sixty-three, on a farm in Michigan. His father expected him to inherit the land. Henry wanted nothing to do with it.

Farm work was slow, brutal, and endless, and even as a boy he was obsessed with one question: why does a human being have to do what a machine could do instead?

At twelve years old, he saw a steam engine moving down a country road on its own power, with no horse in front of it. He later said that moment changed his life. He jumped off the wagon and questioned the driver until the man ran out of answers.

A year later, his mother died. Ford said the house was never the same, like a watch without a spring. At sixteen he walked eight miles to Detroit, with almost no money, and took a job in a machine shop.

He was not the best student, and he was never the best talker. But he could take apart a watch and put it back together, and he could do it better than the men who sold them.

### 02_cap2
Ford spent his twenties working in the shadows. In eighteen ninety-one he joined the Edison Illuminating Company, and within two years he was chief engineer. It was a good, safe, respectable life.

But every night, in a small brick shed behind his house, he was building something else. On a rainy night in June eighteen ninety-six, he finished it: a rattling, four-wheeled machine he called the Quadricycle. It was so wide that he had to knock down part of the shed wall to get it out.

Then came the failures. His first company collapsed in nineteen hundred and one. Investors lost their money, and some of them started calling him a dreamer who would never finish anything.

His second attempt fell apart too. The partners hired an outsider to fix the company, and that outsider eventually put his own name on the cars. It became Cadillac.

Ford was thirty-eight years old, broke, and publicly humiliated. So he did the one thing that fit his character. He went racing.

In October nineteen hundred and one, he beat the most famous driver in America, Alexander Winton, in a single race. The crowd went wild, and money began to listen. Two years later, in June nineteen oh-three, Ford Motor Company was born, with twelve investors and about twenty-eight thousand dollars.

### 03_cap3
The early company sold cars to the rich, like everyone else. But Ford had a different idea, and he kept repeating it: build a motor car for the great multitude.

Rich men's toys were not the future. A car so simple that a farmer could fix it, and so cheap that a farmer could buy it, was the future.

In October nineteen oh-eight, the Model T arrived. It cost eight hundred and fifty dollars, it was tough, light, and easy to repair, and it could survive roads that barely existed.

America went crazy for it. Orders poured in faster than the factory could build. Farmers stopped feeling isolated. Families, for the first time, could drive to another town on a Sunday.

But Ford noticed something that troubled him. The demand was enormous, and the factory was too slow. Every car was built by hand, one at a time, by skilled workers walking around a stationary frame.

If he wanted a car for everyone, he would need to build them like nothing had ever been built before.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen thirteen, at the Highland Park plant, Ford's engineers watched a meatpacking idea in action: a moving line, with each worker doing a single task as the work came to him.

They tried it on a small part first, a magneto. Then on engines. Then, in the fall, on the entire chassis.

The result was almost frightening. A car that once took more than twelve hours to build now rolled out in about ninety-three minutes.

Prices began to fall. Eight hundred and fifty dollars became five hundred and ninety, then four hundred and ninety, then lower, again and again, year after year. Every time Ford cut the price, he sold more cars, and every time he sold more cars, his cost fell again.

But the line had a hidden cost. The work was monotonous, and men hated it. In nineteen thirteen, Ford had to hire nearly ten new workers for every one that he wanted to keep. The factory was bleeding talent.

So on January fifth, nineteen fourteen, he announced the five-dollar day, double the going wage. The press called it either socialism or genius.

It was neither. Here was the real calculation. Ford had figured out that a worker who earns enough to buy the product is the best customer the company can ever have. Turnover collapsed. Productivity jumped. And profits went up.

### 05_cap5
By the early nineteen twenties, Ford owned the largest industrial empire on earth. The Model T was down to two hundred and sixty dollars. Half of all the cars in the world were Fords.

And with power came control. In nineteen nineteen, he bought out every shareholder, including the Dodge brothers, who had helped fund him. The price was about one hundred and six million dollars, and from that day, the company belonged to Henry Ford alone.

He built the giant River Rouge complex, where iron ore and coal came in one end, and finished cars came out the other.

But the same man who understood wages so well could not understand people. He insisted on controlling not only his workers' hours, but their private lives. He published a newspaper, the Dearborn Independent, that spread hateful lies against Jewish Americans, and the damage it caused was real and lasting. Years later, Ford apologized, but the stain never fully washed away.

He refused to change the Model T for almost two decades while competitors, led by General Motors, offered color, comfort, and choice. By nineteen twenty-seven, sales had collapsed, and he had to shut down the entire plant for months to launch the Model A.

His own son, Edsel, tried to modernize the company, and Henry fought him at every turn. Edsel died in nineteen forty-three, at only forty-nine. Many people close to the family believed the stress had broken him.

### 06_cap6
Then the world changed again. When the Second World War began, Ford, who had once been a loud pacifist, turned his factories into the arsenal of democracy.

At Willow Run, near Detroit, he built a plant almost a mile long, with one goal: to produce the B-twenty-four bomber the way he had once produced the Model T. Critics laughed. A bomber has over a million parts. It cannot be built like a car.

Early on, they were right. The plant struggled, and workers called it Willow Run, the place where they never run. But the machine slowly caught up. At its peak, a bomber rolled off that line every single hour.

Inside the company, though, the old man was fading. In nineteen thirty-seven, his security chief, Harry Bennett, had beaten union organizers outside the River Rouge plant in a brutal clash that the press called the Battle of the Overpass. Photographs shocked the country. Ford held out against the union for four more years, until a strike forced him to give in.

After Edsel died, the eighty-year-old founder returned to run the company himself. It was losing about ten million dollars a month. Only a family intervention, led by his wife Clara and his grandson Henry the Second, forced him to hand over control in nineteen forty-five.

The young grandson saved the company. The old man went home.

### 07_lesson
Henry Ford died on April seventh, nineteen forty-seven, at the age of eighty-three, during a power failure, in a house lit by candles. The empire he built had put the world on wheels.

His story gives two lessons. The first is that a great company does not just sell to customers. It builds the customers. When Ford paid workers enough to buy his cars, he made his own market.

The second is a warning. The same stubbornness that made him a giant, refusing to give up, refusing to listen, refusing to share control, almost destroyed everything he created.

Every great builder needs one thing he rarely wants: someone brave enough to tell him he is wrong.
