### 00_hook
Nineteen sixty-two. A twenty-four-year-old man in a plain business suit steps off a plane in Kobe, Japan, with a plan so audacious that it sounds like a lie. He tells the owners of a Japanese shoe company that he represents an American firm called Blue Ribbon Sports. There is only one problem. Blue Ribbon Sports does not exist.

He has no office, no employees, and no money. He has never imported a single pair of shoes. But he has a dream, and a sample of the best running shoes he has ever seen, and he is going to bet his whole future on a bluff.

That man is Phil Knight. The shoes will become Nike. And the company that begins as a lie told in a Japanese conference room will grow into one of the most powerful brands on the planet, worth more than one hundred billion dollars.

This is the story of how a shy runner from Oregon, who could not even say his own dream out loud, taught the world to just do it.

### 01_cap1
Philip Hampson Knight was born on the twenty-fourth of February, nineteen thirty-eight, in Portland, Oregon. His father, Bill Knight, was a lawyer who later became the publisher of a newspaper. His mother, Lota, was a homemaker. They were a respectable, conservative, middle-class family, and Phil was a quiet, polite, deeply shy boy who hated to stand out.

He was also small, and he was not a natural athlete. But he had one gift. He could run. He joined the track team at Cleveland High School, and he ran the mile. He was good, not great, but he loved the pain of it, the discipline, and the moment when the body wants to quit and the mind says no.

Running would shape his whole life. At the University of Oregon, he joined the track team under a young coach named Bill Bowerman. Bowerman was a legend in the making. He was a tough, demanding, endlessly curious man who believed that the right equipment could make an athlete faster, and he spent his free time experimenting with the shoes his runners wore.

Knight was a solid middle-distance runner, but never a star. Still, he learned something from Bowerman that no classroom could teach him. He learned that a small edge, repeated over and over, can change everything.

### 02_cap2
After graduating from Oregon in nineteen fifty-nine, Knight served a year in the Army Reserve. Then he enrolled at Stanford University to earn a business degree.

In a class on entrepreneurship, he was given an assignment. Write a paper about a new business idea. Knight thought about his own world, running. He knew that the best running shoes in America, made by the German companies Adidas and Puma, were expensive, and that the Japanese were starting to make cameras that threatened the German ones. What if Japanese running shoes could do the same to Adidas?

He wrote a paper titled, Can Japanese Sports Shoes Do to German Sports Shoes What Japanese Cameras Did to German Cameras? His professor liked it. Knight loved it. It was the first time he had been excited about a business, and he could not stop thinking about it.

After Stanford, his father gave him a small amount of money, and Knight did something bold. He took a trip around the world. In Tokyo, in nineteen sixty-two, he visited a company called Onitsuka, which made a brand of running shoes called Tiger. They were well made, cheap, and a little too good to believe.

He walked into the meeting, and when they asked the name of his company, he panicked. He blurted out a name that he had never used before. Blue Ribbon Sports. They asked him if he could be their American distributor, and he said yes.

### 03_cap3
Back in Oregon, Knight ordered a batch of samples. When the first twelve pairs of Tigers arrived, he sent a pair to his old coach, Bill Bowerman, with an offer to become a partner. Bowerman loved the shoes, and he put up half the money. In nineteen sixty-four, they formed Blue Ribbon Sports, with five hundred dollars from each of them.

Knight had a day job as an accountant, and he sold shoes on weekends. He drove up and down the West Coast, selling Tigers out of the trunk of his green Plymouth Valiant, at track meets and high schools. He stood on the sidelines and talked to runners, one by one. He knew that nobody understood a runner better than another runner.

The first year, he sold about thirteen hundred dollars of shoes. The next year, it was eight thousand. Then it began to double again and again. He opened the first small retail shop in Santa Monica, California, and hired a young salesman named Jeff Johnson, who wrote letters to Knight so full of ideas that he is sometimes called the first employee.

But there was a problem. Knight needed money, and the banks did not trust him. Each time sales grew, he needed to pay for the next order, and the cash never kept up. For years, the company teetered on the edge of bankruptcy, and Knight had to beg for loans from the First National Bank of Oregon, which was a source of constant stress.

### 04_cap4
By the end of the nineteen sixties, Blue Ribbon Sports was growing, but its fate was in someone else's hands. Onitsuka controlled the supply. And in nineteen sixty-nine and seventy, the relationship began to sour. Knight learned that the Japanese company was planning to cut him out and sell directly in America.

He had to build his own brand, and quickly. In nineteen seventy-one, Bowerman, who had been experimenting for years, made a breakthrough. At breakfast one morning, he poured rubber into his wife's waffle iron, and created a shoe sole with little rubber bumps that gripped the track. It was a rough, ugly prototype, but it worked. The waffle trainer was born.

They needed a name and a logo. Knight asked a graphic design student at Portland State University, Carolyn Davidson, to create something that suggested movement. She worked for hours, and charged him two dollars an hour. In the end, she delivered a swoosh, a simple curved checkmark. He paid her thirty-five dollars. He said he did not love it, but maybe it would grow on him.

The name came last. Jeff Johnson suggested Nike, after the Greek goddess of victory. Knight did not like it, but there was no time. Nike it was. In nineteen seventy-two, the first Nike shoes appeared at the Olympic Trials in Eugene, Oregon. And in nineteen seventy-one, the split with Onitsuka was official, and a lawsuit followed. The company fought it for years, and in the end, Nike won.

### 05_cap5
The nineteen seventies were the years of the running boom. Millions of Americans, inspired by the marathon, began to jog. Jogging became a fashion, a lifestyle, and a business. Nike was in the right place at the right time.

In nineteen seventy-four, the Waffle Trainer became a bestseller. In nineteen seventy-eight, Blue Ribbon Sports was officially renamed Nike, Incorporated. By nineteen seventy-nine, Nike had overtaken Adidas as the top-selling athletic shoe in America, with sales of about one hundred and fifty million dollars.

In nineteen eighty, the company went public. Knight, who had once begged banks for loans, was suddenly a billionaire, at least on paper. But the success also brought a new problem. Nike was a running company, and running was starting to slow down. In the middle of the nineteen eighties, a rival called Reebok exploded with aerobics shoes, and for the first time, Nike's sales fell.

Knight needed a new idea, and it came from a young basketball player. In nineteen eighty-four, he took a huge gamble on a rookie from North Carolina named Michael Jordan. Everyone else in the shoe business thought he was crazy. Jordan did not even want to sign with Nike. But Knight offered him a deal that no one had ever offered a rookie. A signature shoe, and a share of the profits.

### 06_cap6
The Air Jordan, released in nineteen eighty-five, was a phenomenon. It was black and red, banned by the NBA for violating the uniform rules, and Nike happily paid the fine for every game, because the publicity was worth millions. In its first year, the Air Jordan sold more than one hundred and twenty-six million dollars of shoes, far above the expectations.

In nineteen eighty-eight, Nike launched a new advertising campaign, with three words. Just do it. It was an invitation to everyone, not just athletes. It was short, simple, and fearless. Within ten years, Nike's share of the market grew from about eighteen percent to forty-three percent.

Under Knight's leadership, Nike turned athletes into icons. Michael Jordan, Tiger Woods, Serena Williams, Cristiano Ronaldo, and later LeBron James became not only sponsors but heroes. Knight understood something no one had grasped so deeply. People do not buy shoes. They buy stories.

But success had a dark side. In the nineteen nineties, Nike was accused of using sweatshops in Asia, with low wages, long hours, and poor conditions for workers. Protests spread across college campuses. Knight, famous for his calm, admitted publicly that the company had made mistakes, and he promised to change. Nike improved its factory standards, but the critics never fully forgot.

### 07_cap7
In two thousand and four, at the age of sixty-six, Knight stepped down as the CEO of Nike, though he stayed on as chairman. Over the next fifteen years, he handed the reins to new leaders, and slowly, in his own words, learned to let go.

In two thousand and sixteen, he published a memoir called Shoe Dog. It became a bestseller, and revealed a very different Phil Knight from the confident billionaire people imagined. It was a story of near bankruptcy, of panic, of mistakes, and of a man who thought he might fail every single day.

He also became one of the great philanthropists of his generation. Knight and his wife, Penny, gave more than two billion dollars to the University of Oregon, to Stanford, and to cancer research at Oregon Health and Science University. In two thousand and nineteen, at the age of eighty-one, he stepped down as chairman.

He had a deep sorrow, too. In two thousand and four, his son Matthew died in a scuba diving accident in El Salvador, at the age of thirty-four. Knight said later that Nike's mission was always bigger than a company. It was about believing in yourself, and in your children.

Today, Nike is worth more than one hundred billion dollars, and Phil Knight remains among the richest people in the world.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Phil Knight?

The first lesson is that you do not have to be the best to build something great. Knight was an average runner, a shy man, and a mediocre salesman. But he was stubborn. He kept going when others would have quit, and he found people who were better than him at everything.

The second lesson is that a great partner changes everything. Without Bill Bowerman, there would have been no waffle sole, no Nike. Knight knew his weakness and his strength, and he built a team around them.

The third lesson is to bet on people, not on products. Michael Jordan was a rookie nobody believed in. Knight bet on him, and the bet made Nike a global giant.

And the final lesson is that an idea is not enough. You must have the courage to act on it. Knight stood in a conference room in Japan with a made-up company, and said yes. Everything began with that moment.
