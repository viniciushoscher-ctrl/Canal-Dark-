### 00_hook
Nineteen forty-eight. In a small town in northern Spain, a thirteen-year-old boy walks into a clothing store, to buy some groceries on credit for his mother. The shopkeeper looks at him, and refuses. He tells the boy that the family has not paid its debts, and that he will not extend credit. The boy is humiliated. His father is a railway worker, earning a few pesetas a day, and his mother works as a maid.

On the way home, he makes a promise to himself. He will never again be poor. The next day, he asks his parents if he can leave school, and he finds a job. He is a delivery boy for a shirtmaker, and he is paid a small salary. He goes to work, and he never stops.

Seventy years later, that boy owns one of the largest clothing empires in the world. His stores sell a new fashion every two weeks, in more than ninety countries. For some months, he was the richest man on earth. And almost no one knows his face.

His name is Amancio Ortega. And he is the founder of Zara.

### 01_cap1
Amancio Ortega Gaona was born on the twenty-eighth of March, nineteen thirty-six, in Busdongo de Arbas, a tiny village in the province of Leon, in northern Spain. His father, Antonio, was a railway worker, and his mother, Josefa, was a housemaid. In July of that year, the Spanish Civil War began, and the country was torn apart.

The family moved around Spain to follow his father's job. When Amancio was a boy, they settled in the city of A Coruna, in Galicia, a rainy, poor coastal region. Money was always tight. Amancio had a short childhood, and by the age of fourteen, he was working.

His first job was in a shirt shop called Gala, in the center of the city, where he carried parcels and ran errands. He was a quick learner. Within a few years, he knew how clothes were made, and how they were sold. He worked in another shop, called La Maja, and learned how to cut fabric, how to price, and how to deal with customers.

He was a serious young man, and he did not drink or smoke. He read about business in the evenings, and he saved his wages. He was beginning to see how the clothing trade worked, and he noticed its flaw.

### 02_cap2
In nineteen sixty-three, at the age of twenty-seven, Ortega began to make his own garments. He and his wife, Rosalia Mera, a seamstress, started to produce women's bathrobes and nightgowns, in a small workshop. They sold them to local stores, and a few small shops. The business was called Confecciones Goa, from the initials of his name reversed, Ao.

The couple worked tirelessly. Rosalia designed, and Amancio managed the sales. The first years were hard, but the robes were cheap and well made. In a few years, they employed hundreds of workers, and the workshop became a small factory.

Then, in nineteen seventy-five, something happened that would change his business. A big customer in Germany cancelled a large order, at the last minute. Ortega was left with a mountain of unsold stock. He had no choice. He decided to open his own shop, to sell the goods himself.

In nineteen seventy-five, he opened the first Zara store in La Coruna. He wanted to call it Zorba, after the film, but the name was already used by a bar nearby. So he used the letters he had, and it became Zara.

### 03_cap3
The first Zara shop was modest, but it had a revolutionary idea. In traditional fashion, designers presented new collections twice a year, and shops ordered months in advance. Factories made the clothes in Asia, and shipped them for weeks. By the time they arrived, trends had often changed.

Ortega did the opposite. He brought design, production, and distribution under one roof, in Spain. Designers watched what customers bought, and sent new designs straight to the factory. In two or three weeks, a new item could be made, and reach the shops. Zara made small batches, and changed its stock twice a week. If something did not sell, it disappeared. If it did, it was made again.

The system was called fast fashion. It turned the industry upside down. A customer who saw something in Zara knew that it would not be there next month. They had to buy it now. And they came back, again and again, to see what was new.

Ortega also cut costs on advertising. He spent almost nothing on it. His shops, in the best locations on the busiest streets, were his advertising.

### 04_cap4
In nineteen eighty-five, Ortega formed a holding company, Inditex, Industria de Diseno Textil, to manage his growing empire. In nineteen eighty-eight, he opened the first Zara outside Spain, in Porto, Portugal. In nineteen eighty-nine, he crossed the Atlantic, with a store in New York. In nineteen ninety-one, Paris followed.

The expansion was fast. He added other brands, Pull and Bear, in nineteen ninety-one, Massimo Dutti in nineteen ninety-two, Bershka in nineteen ninety-eight, Stradivarius, and later Oysho and Zara Home. Each had its own target market, but all used the same system.

In two thousand and one, Inditex went public on the Madrid stock exchange. It raised a significant sum, and Ortega kept about sixty percent of the shares. Overnight, he became one of the richest men in Spain.

In the following years, Inditex kept on growing. By two thousand and eleven, it had more than five thousand stores. By two thousand and twenty, it had about six thousand, in over ninety countries, and sales above twenty-eight billion euros a year.

### 05_cap5
The secret was in the details. Ortega built a tightly controlled supply chain. Much of the production stayed close to home, in Spain, Portugal, and Morocco, so that Zara could react fast. Trucks and planes took the clothes from the central distribution center in Arteixo, near A Coruna, to the shops across the world, in a day or two.

Stores sent data every day to headquarters, about what customers tried on, and bought. Designers used it to decide what to make next. Ortega, who sat at a desk among his designers, in an open-plan office, believed that closeness to the customer was everything. He kept a simple rule. Information must flow quickly, from the shop floor to the factory.

His management style was unusual. He had no private office, and rarely spoke in public. He wore a blue blazer and a simple shirt. He refused interviews. For decades, Spanish newspapers had only one photograph of him, taken in the nineteen sixties. He ate lunch in the company canteen, and he walked to work.

In two thousand and eleven, at the age of seventy-five, Ortega formally stepped down as chairman, and handed the job to Pablo Isla. He remained the largest shareholder.

### 06_cap6
As Inditex grew, so did Ortega's fortune. In two thousand and twelve, Forbes ranked him the third richest person in the world. In two thousand and thirteen, he passed Warren Buffett, and in two thousand and fifteen, he was briefly the richest man on the planet, with a net worth of about eighty billion dollars. But he kept a modest lifestyle.

He also became an important philanthropist. His foundation gave hundreds of millions of euros to hospitals in Spain, and in two thousand and seventeen, he donated about three hundred million euros for cancer equipment to public hospitals, for the screening and treatment of cancer. In two thousand and twenty-two, he gave one billion euros to Spanish health services. Later donations followed.

The company also faced criticism. Activists and journalists accused some of its suppliers of poor working conditions, and low wages, in factories in places such as Brazil, Argentina, and Bangladesh. In two thousand and thirteen, the collapse of the Rana Plaza building in Bangladesh, which housed garment factories, made the world aware of the human cost of cheap clothing. Inditex said it was committed to improving conditions, and it signed safety agreements.

There were also debates about fast fashion, waste, and the environment.

### 07_cap7
Ortega's personal life was mostly private. His first wife, Rosalia Mera, with whom he started the business, was the cofounder of Zara. They had two children, Sandra and Marcos. Marcos, who was born with a disability, was cared for with great devotion. The couple divorced in nineteen ninety-six, and Rosalia became the richest woman in Spain, before her death in two thousand and thirteen. In two thousand and one, Ortega married Flora Perez, with whom he has a daughter, Marta, who is now seen as heir to the family empire.

Marta Ortega became the chair of Inditex in two thousand and twenty-two, after Pablo Isla left. Her father, in his late eighties, is still the biggest shareholder, with about fifty-nine percent of the company.

Zara has become the world's largest fashion retailer. Its model has been copied by dozens of competitors. In a world where fashion changes fast, Ortega's idea was to be faster.

He once said that the key to his success was to listen to the customer, and to be ready to change.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Amancio Ortega?

The first lesson is to turn humiliation into drive. A shopkeeper refused a boy credit. That boy built an empire that outsells any shopkeeper in history.

The second lesson is to control your system. By owning design, production, and distribution, Ortega could move faster than anyone.

The third lesson is to listen to data, and to the customer. Zara does not guess. It watches what sells, and responds.

And the final lesson is that quiet can be powerful. Ortega avoided publicity, wore plain clothes, and let his stores do the talking.
