### 00_hook
Two thousand eight. A rocket sits on a tiny island in the Pacific, called Omelek. It is the fourth attempt of a small company called SpaceX to reach orbit. The first three have failed. The company has run out of money, and the man who founded it has put in his last dollars. He has told his friends that if this launch fails, the company is finished.

At the same time, his electric car company, Tesla, is weeks away from bankruptcy. His marriage has ended. Newspapers call him a rich kid playing with rockets, and his friends are begging him to quit.

On September twenty-eighth, the rocket lifts off, and this time it reaches orbit. It becomes the first privately built liquid-fuel rocket to do so. That night, he cries with relief. His name is Elon Musk. And this is the story of how a man bet his entire fortune on two impossible dreams, nearly lost everything, and became the richest person on Earth.

### 01_cap1
Elon Reeve Musk was born on June twenty-eighth, nineteen seventy-one, in Pretoria, South Africa. His father, Errol, was an engineer. His mother, Maye, was a dietitian and model. His parents divorced when he was a child, and Elon grew up mostly with his father, a decision he would later regret.

He was a shy, bookish boy. He read for ten hours a day, devoured encyclopedias, and taught himself to code. At twelve, he sold the source code of a video game called Blastar to a magazine for five hundred dollars. At school he was bullied so badly that he spent a week in the hospital after being thrown down a flight of stairs.

At seventeen, to escape South Africa and its military draft, Musk moved to Canada, and later to the University of Pennsylvania. He studied physics and economics. After graduation, he started a PhD at Stanford, but left after just two days, to join the internet boom.

### 02_cap2
He was also, by his own account, relentless with himself. He worked a hundred hours a week, and he lived for years on a dollar a day for food, to prove that a person could survive on little while building a business. In nineteen ninety-five, Musk and his brother Kimbal started a company called Zip2, which provided online city guides to newspapers. They slept in the office, and showered at the YMCA. In nineteen ninety-nine, Compaq bought Zip2 for three hundred and seven million dollars. Musk, at twenty-eight, received about twenty-two million.

He did not retire. He put almost all of the money into a new venture, an online bank called X dot com. It merged with a competitor, and became PayPal. After a power struggle, Musk was removed as CEO. But when eBay bought PayPal in two thousand two for one point five billion dollars, he collected about a hundred and eighty million.

By thirty-one, Musk was a multi-millionaire. Most people would have enjoyed it. Instead, he decided to use the money on the two things that he cared about most: making humanity a multi-planetary species, and ending the world's dependence on fossil fuels.

### 03_cap3
In two thousand two, Musk founded SpaceX. He wanted to reduce the cost of rockets by a factor of ten. He traveled to Russia to buy a used missile, but the Russians laughed and insulted him, and demanded millions. On the flight home, he did the math in his head. He realized that he could build a rocket himself, for a fraction of the price.

Everyone told him he was crazy. Rocket science was the job of governments. The first rocket, the Falcon One, failed in two thousand six, and again in two thousand seven, and again in two thousand eight. By the third failure, SpaceX had almost no cash. Musk had spent about a hundred million dollars of his own money.

Meanwhile, in two thousand four, he invested in an electric car startup called Tesla. He became its chairman, and later its CEO. The Roadster, in two thousand eight, was a sports car with a battery that went two hundred miles. But development was slow, costs exploded, and the company was running out of cash.

### 04_cap4
December two thousand eight was the darkest month of his life. Tesla could not make payroll. SpaceX had one more rocket. Musk was going through a divorce. He was sleeping on friends' couches, and borrowing money from them to pay his personal bills.

Then, on September twenty-eighth, the fourth Falcon One reached orbit. A few months later, on Christmas Eve, NASA announced a contract for one point six billion dollars to resupply the International Space Station. SpaceX was saved. On the same day, Tesla closed a last-minute investment of just forty million dollars, hours before payroll would have bounced.

Musk later said that those days nearly broke him. He told an interviewer that he had been ready to give up, and that he had chosen to put every last dollar in, because anything less would have meant that neither company survived.

The next ten years were a different battle. In two thousand ten, Tesla went public. In two thousand twelve, the SpaceX Dragon capsule became the first private spacecraft to dock with the station. In two thousand fifteen, one of its rockets landed upright on a pad, after launching. Nobody had ever done it before.

### 05_cap5
Musk's rockets were now reusable. Where other rockets were thrown away after each flight, like a jumbo jet discarded after every trip, Falcon Nine boosters flew again and again. The cost of reaching orbit fell dramatically. In two thousand eighteen, he launched his own red Tesla Roadster into space, with a mannequin in a spacesuit at the wheel. The photograph went around the world.

Meanwhile, Tesla hit a wall known as production hell. The Model Three, a mass-market electric car, was so hard to build that Musk slept on the factory floor. Short sellers bet billions that Tesla would go bankrupt. But in two thousand twenty, the company delivered half a million cars, and turned an annual profit for the first time.

Its stock soared. In two thousand twenty, Tesla became the most valuable car company in the world. By the end of two thousand twenty-one, Musk was the richest person on Earth, worth over three hundred billion dollars.

### 06_cap6
He kept making bold bets. In two thousand sixteen, he cofounded Neuralink to connect brains to computers. He founded The Boring Company to dig tunnels. And in two thousand twenty-two, he bought the social network Twitter for forty-four billion dollars, then renamed it X.

Behind the achievements was a style of management that he called first principles. He asked engineers to ignore what had always been done, and to reduce a problem to its basic physics. A rocket, he said, is just metal, fuel, and labor, so why should it cost more than that?

His methods have been controversial. Critics say that he overpromises, treats employees harshly, and makes erratic statements online. Regulators have investigated him many times. His political activity has divided public opinion.

But even critics admit that he has done what most people said was impossible. SpaceX now launches more tonnage into orbit than the rest of the world combined. Starlink, its satellite network, provides internet to millions. And Tesla has pushed every other carmaker to go electric.

Musk now speaks about colonizing Mars. He has said that he wants to die on Mars, but not on impact.

### 07_lesson
Elon Musk's story holds two lessons. The first is to bet on the things you care about, and to keep betting even when the world says no. He invested nearly everything in two impossible companies, at the same time, and survived by only a few days.

The second is that extreme ambition requires extreme persistence. Musk failed again and again, and he kept going. Whether you admire him or not, the story shows that the line between a visionary and a fool is often success.
