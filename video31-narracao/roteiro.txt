### 00_hook
June, eighteen thirty-one. On a farm in the Shenandoah Valley of Virginia, a twenty-two-year-old man stands at the edge of a wheat field. Behind him is a strange wooden machine, with a spinning reel, a long blade, and a platform, and nobody in the world has ever seen anything like it. The neighbors have gathered to watch. Some are laughing. Others are shaking their heads.

For centuries, farmers have cut grain the same way. A man swings a blade by hand, under the burning sun, for days at a time. His own father spent twenty years trying to build a machine to do it, and failed.

The young man pulls the horse forward. The blade begins to move. And the wheat falls.

His name is Cyrus McCormick. He would fight rivals, lawyers, bankruptcy, and a fire that burned his factory to the ground. And in the end, he would change the way the entire world feeds itself. This is the story of the man who invented the harvest.

### 01_cap1
Cyrus Hall McCormick was born on February fifteenth, eighteen oh nine, at Walnut Grove, a farm in Rockbridge County, Virginia. He was the oldest of eight children, in a Scots-Irish Presbyterian family that had farmed that valley for two generations.

Walnut Grove had a blacksmith shop, a grist mill, a sawmill, and a small iron works. His father, Robert McCormick, owned about fifteen hundred acres and a number of enslaved workers, which was normal for a Virginia landowner at that time. Robert was also an inventor, with a workshop full of half finished ideas. Cyrus grew up surrounded by tools. He learned to forge iron and to fix almost anything that broke.

And every summer, he learned how brutal the harvest was. Wheat was cut with a sickle or a cradle, a long curved blade with wooden fingers. A strong man could cut perhaps two acres in a day, and only if the weather held. Grain that was not cut in time fell to the ground and rotted. A farmer could plant as much as he liked, but he could only plant what he could harvest.

That was the real limit on farming. Not land, not seed, not rain. It was the human arm.

His father knew it. Robert spent years building a horse drawn reaper. In eighteen sixteen he failed. In eighteen thirty, he tried one last time, and again the grain tangled, the blade clogged, and the horse dragged a pile of broken wood across the field. Robert gave up. But his son was watching.

### 02_cap2
Cyrus was twenty-one years old, and he decided to start over. Where his father had tried to copy the motion of the human arm, Cyrus thought like a mechanic. A reaper had to do four things at once. It had to divide the standing grain, cut the stalks, hold them against the blade, and catch them as they fell.

In the summer of eighteen thirty-one, in the family workshop, he built it. A single horse pulled the machine from behind. A wooden reel swept the grain toward a horizontal blade, which moved back and forth against fixed iron fingers. The cut grain dropped onto a platform, where a worker raked it into piles. It was rough, and it was loud. But it worked.

His first public test was on a neighbor's farm. The field was sloped and uneven, but the machine cut six acres of oats before sundown. A man with a cradle would have needed three days. The neighbors were amazed.

But a working machine is not a business. In eighteen thirty-three, a man in Baltimore named Obed Hussey announced a reaper of his own, and received a patent first. In June of eighteen thirty-four, Cyrus finally received his. From that day, two men claimed to have invented the same machine, and a rivalry began that would last twenty years. It taught Cyrus a lesson he would learn again and again. Inventing something was only the first step. Getting people to believe in it would be a much longer fight.

### 03_cap3
The next decade nearly broke him.

Farmers did not trust the new machine. It was heavy, expensive, and it broke down. A farmer who bought a reaper was risking his whole harvest on a design he had never seen work. Most chose the old way.

Cyrus and his father went into the iron business, and opened a furnace in the Virginia mountains. They borrowed heavily. Then in eighteen thirty-seven, a financial panic swept across the United States. Banks failed, and the price of iron fell through the floor. The family was left with enormous debts, and for a while it looked as if they would lose Walnut Grove itself.

During those years, his reaper sat in the shed. In eighteen forty, he sold exactly two machines. In eighteen forty-two, seven. The next year, twenty-nine. These were not the numbers of a rising empire. But every season he rebuilt the reaper, fixed the flaws one by one, and carried the lessons into the next machine.

And he began to understand something no other inventor around him understood. The problem was not the product. It was the selling. So Cyrus went to the farmers. He drove from county to county, and set up demonstrations in open fields, with crowds watching. He challenged Obed Hussey to public contests. He printed advertisements, which almost no manufacturer was doing then, and he printed letters from satisfied customers.

Slowly, the orders grew. But he was licensing other factories to build his machines, and the quality was uneven. Every broken reaper hurt his name. He realized that to build an empire, he had to build it himself, in the right place. And the right place was not Virginia.

### 04_cap4
In eighteen forty-seven, Cyrus McCormick looked at a map of America and made the biggest decision of his career. He would move to a small, muddy, fast growing town on the shore of Lake Michigan. The town was called Chicago.

At that time, Chicago had about sixteen thousand people. But Cyrus saw something that others missed. The wheat was moving west. The prairies of Illinois, Iowa, and Wisconsin were flat, wide, and endless, the perfect land for a horse drawn reaper. The lake gave him ships, and the new railroads were about to reach every farm in the Midwest. He chose the city not because it was big, but because of where it was going to be.

With a partner named William Ogden, the first mayor of Chicago, he built a large factory on the bank of the Chicago River. In its first year it produced about five hundred reapers. Within a few years, it was making thousands.

But the factory was not his greatest invention. His greatest invention was how he sold. First, a written guarantee: if the machine failed, the farmer got his money back. No manufacturer had ever done that. Second, credit: a farmer could pay part when he bought the machine and the rest after the harvest. Third, one fixed price, printed in the catalog, for everyone. And fourth, a network of local agents who demonstrated the reaper, trained farmers, and supplied spare parts.

Guarantees, installment credit, fixed prices, and service. Today they seem ordinary. In eighteen forty-eight, they were revolutionary. Historians would later say that McCormick invented not only a machine, but modern marketing.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen fifty-one, the world's first great international fair opened in London, inside the Crystal Palace, an enormous building of glass and iron. McCormick sent his reaper across the Atlantic.

The British were not impressed. The Times of London mocked the machine, calling it a cross between a wheelbarrow, a flying machine, and a circus chariot. The crowd walked past it.

Then came a test. English farmers and judges gathered at a wheat field in Essex, to compare the American machine with a British rival. The British machine jammed in the thick wet grain. McCormick's reaper kept cutting, cleanly, mile after mile. The same newspaper now wrote that the reaper alone was worth more than the whole cost of the exhibition, and the jury awarded McCormick the Council Medal, the highest prize of the fair.

Orders came in from England, France, Russia, and Germany. In eighteen fifty-five, at the Paris Exposition, he won the Grand Medal of Honor. Years later, the French Academy of Sciences elected him a member, saying he had done more for agriculture than any other living man.

But success had a price. Rival manufacturers copied his designs, and Cyrus, a man of stubborn pride, did not know how to ignore an insult. He would go to court. Again, and again, and again.

### 06_cap6
Between eighteen forty-eight and the middle of the eighteen fifties, McCormick fought one of the great patent wars in American history.

When his patent was about to expire, he asked the Patent Office to extend it. The request was refused. Rival makers copied his machine freely, and he spent a fortune in legal fees trying to hold the line.

The most famous case was in eighteen fifty-five, against a manufacturer from Illinois named John Manny. Manny's side hired a tall, awkward attorney from Springfield named Abraham Lincoln. Lincoln traveled to Cincinnati and prepared a careful argument. But when he arrived, he was pushed aside by a more famous, more polished team. A Pittsburgh lawyer named Edwin Stanton treated the country lawyer with open contempt, and Lincoln was never even asked to speak in court. He went home hurt. Years later, as president, he chose Stanton to be his Secretary of War, because Stanton was brilliant.

When the Civil War came, the reaper became more important than anyone had imagined. Millions of young men left the farms for the army, yet the North's farms kept producing, because machines were doing the work of the missing hands. Wheat from the Midwest fed the Union armies and was shipped to Europe for gold.

McCormick himself, however, was a Democrat with strong sympathies for the South, where he had grown up. In eighteen sixty-four he ran for Congress, and he lost. His opponents called him a traitor. For the first time, the great businessman learned that fortune did not buy popularity.

### 07_cap7
In eighteen fifty-eight, at the age of forty-nine, Cyrus married a young woman named Nettie Fowler. She was twenty-three, clever, patient, and deeply religious. She became his closest advisor.

Then, in October of eighteen seventy-one, came the Great Chicago Fire. For three days, flames swept the city. The McCormick factory, the engine of his fortune, was burned to the ground. Cyrus was sixty-two years old. Many men would have retired. Instead, with Nettie beside him, he told his family that he would rebuild, bigger than before. Within a year, the new factory was running, larger and more advanced than the old one. The fire, which should have destroyed him, became the start of a second empire.

He died on May thirteenth, eighteen eighty-four, at the age of seventy-five, in his home in Chicago. He left a fortune of around ten million dollars, and a company selling tens of thousands of machines a year.

The story did not end there. In eighteen eighty-six, a bitter labor dispute at the McCormick works led to the Haymarket affair, one of the most famous labor tragedies in American history. And in nineteen oh two, the company merged with its rivals, in a deal arranged by J.P. Morgan, to form International Harvester.

Today, the combine harvesters that roll across Kansas, Ukraine, and Brazil are the grandchildren of a wooden machine built in a Virginia workshop. One man with one modern machine can do the work of hundreds from the age of the cradle. That is why so few of us now work the land, and why so many of us can eat.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Cyrus McCormick?

The first lesson is that an invention is not a business. McCormick built a working reaper at twenty-two, but he did not become rich until he was nearly forty. The difference was selling. The guarantee, the credit, the agents, the demonstrations, the catalogs. He understood that you have to make a customer feel safe before he will take a risk.

The second is that failure can be a teacher. His father failed, and Cyrus studied why. The panic of eighteen thirty-seven crushed his finances. The fire of eighteen seventy-one crushed his factory. Each time, he rebuilt, and each time he was stronger.

The third is to go where the future is going. He left Virginia for a muddy town on a lake because he could see what Chicago would become. He did not choose the place that was best today. He chose the place that would be best in twenty years.

And the last is a warning. Cyrus was stubborn, proud, and always at war with someone. That stubbornness built his empire, but it also left behind a company that could not understand its own workers.

He started with one wooden machine, and a field of wheat that fell. He ended by changing how humanity eats. If you ever eat a slice of bread, remember the young man in Virginia who decided that the harvest did not have to be so hard.

Thanks for watching.
