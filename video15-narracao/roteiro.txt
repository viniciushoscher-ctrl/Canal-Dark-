### 00_hook
Nineteen sixty-two. In a small town in Arkansas called Rogers, a forty-four-year-old man opens a discount store with a plain sign. The store has bare concrete floors, cheap shelves, and cardboard boxes on the aisles. Inside, the prices are lower than anywhere else in the county. Customers laugh at how ugly the place is. Then they buy, and buy again.

The man behind the counter drives an old pickup truck, with a bird dog in the back seat. He wears a cap that says Wal-Mart. He cuts his own hair, and flies the cheapest airplane seats. And yet, within thirty years, he will become the richest man in America, and his little store will become the largest company in the world.

His name is Sam Walton. And this is how a small-town merchant used one idea, and a pickup truck, to build the biggest retailer in history.

### 01_cap1
Samuel Moore Walton was born on March twenty-ninth, nineteen eighteen, in Kingfisher, Oklahoma. His father, Thomas, was a farm loan officer who moved the family across the Midwest looking for work. During the Great Depression, the Waltons had little, and Sam learned to earn his own money from the age of seven.

He delivered newspapers, he sold magazine subscriptions, and he milked the family cow every morning before school. At school, in Shelbina, Missouri, he became the youngest Eagle Scout in the state's history, and the quarterback of the football team that won the state championship. He was elected student body president, too. He had a gift that no teacher could explain. He made people want to follow him.

In college, he waited tables to pay his bills, and he still ran for every student office. He studied economics at the University of Missouri, and graduated in nineteen forty. Then he took a job as a management trainee at J. C. Penney in Des Moines, Iowa, for seventy-five dollars a month.

### 02_cap2
When the Second World War began, Walton joined the army, and served as an intelligence officer. After the war, in nineteen forty-five, at twenty-seven, he used a loan from his father-in-law to buy a variety store in Newport, Arkansas. It was a Ben Franklin franchise, a small chain of five-and-dime shops.

He worked relentlessly. He studied every price. He discovered a simple principle. If he bought items cheaper, and sold them at a slightly lower price than his rivals, he made less per item but sold far more. The store's sales tripled in three years, and then it became the best Ben Franklin in the region.

Walton later called the next moment the worst of his life, and also the most useful. But in nineteen fifty, his landlord refused to renew the lease. He had read the contract carefully, and he realized that Walton had made the store so successful that he wanted it for his own son. Sam lost the store he had built, and he almost lost his confidence.

### 03_cap3
Walton moved his family to Bentonville, a tiny town in the Ozarks of Arkansas. He opened a new Walton's Five and Dime on the town square. This time he read every contract. In the following years, he opened more stores across small Arkansas towns. By nineteen sixty, he owned fifteen.

He flew a small airplane from town to town, looking down at the roads, and watching how people shopped. He visited his competitors, and he wrote notes on a pad. He also traveled to the Midwest, to see a new kind of store called the discount store, where goods were sold in bulk at very low prices.

Walton became convinced that the future of retail was in small towns, not in big cities. Big chains ignored towns of under five thousand people, because they thought there was no money. Sam disagreed. He believed that people in small towns also wanted low prices, and nobody was serving them.

He asked the big companies to join him, including Ben Franklin, but they refused. So he decided to do it alone.

### 04_cap4
On July second, nineteen sixty-two, Sam Walton opened the first Wal-Mart Discount City in Rogers, Arkansas. The store was plain, with cheap fixtures. But the prices were extraordinarily low. His slogan was simple: always low prices.

Walton's secret was his obsession with costs. He bought directly from manufacturers, cutting out the middlemen. He built distribution centers, so that every store could be resupplied quickly. He kept his stores in cheap buildings, and paid himself a small salary. Every dollar saved was passed on to customers.

Another trick was the size of the margin. Competitors marked up goods by thirty percent. Walton marked them up by twenty, and sold twice as many. Customers learned that Wal-Mart was cheaper without checking, and the habit became a brand.

He also built a unique culture. He called his employees associates, and he gave them a share in the profits. He walked into stores on Saturday mornings and led the staff in a cheer. Employees spelled out the name of the company, W-A-L-M-A-R-T, and shouted at the end, who is number one? The customer.

By nineteen seventy, Wal-Mart had thirty-eight stores and sales of forty-four million dollars. That year, he took the company public, and raised enough money to expand rapidly.

### 05_cap5
In the seventies and eighties, Wal-Mart grew faster than any retailer in history. Sam opened hundreds of stores across the South and Midwest. He used computers and satellites earlier than any competitor, to track inventory in real time. He built his own fleet of trucks.

Wall Street analysts doubted him for years. They said that a chain in tiny towns would never be profitable. By the mid nineteen eighties, Wal-Mart was earning more per square foot than almost any rival.

But he stayed the same. He drove an old Ford pickup truck, and ate breakfast at a local diner. He visited stores unannounced, and he talked to workers and customers. If he found a problem, he fixed it that day. He believed in what he called management by walking around.

In nineteen eighty-three, he started Sam's Club, a warehouse club for bulk shopping. In nineteen eighty-eight, he opened the first Supercenter, a store combining groceries and general merchandise. By the end of the decade, Wal-Mart was the third-largest retailer in the country.

In nineteen eighty-five, Forbes named Sam Walton the richest man in America, with a net worth of about two and a half billion dollars. He seemed embarrassed. He still shopped for his own clothes at his own store.

### 06_cap6
In nineteen ninety, Walton learned that he had a rare blood cancer, called hairy cell leukemia. He kept working. In nineteen ninety-two, President George H. W. Bush awarded him the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest civilian honor in the United States.

Sam Walton died on April fifth, nineteen ninety-two, at the age of seventy-four. He left behind nearly two thousand stores, and about three hundred and seventy thousand employees. His family inherited a fortune that would become the largest in America.

Even in his last months, Walton visited stores in a wheelchair, and told managers to keep prices low. His last book was called Made in America, and in it he insisted that he had never been smarter than anyone, just more stubborn.

His legacy is complicated. Critics say that Walmart crushed small-town shops, paid low wages, and relied on foreign factories. Supporters say that it saved families billions of dollars on groceries and clothes. Both are true.

Today, Walmart employs more than two million people, and earns over six hundred billion dollars a year, which makes it the largest company in the world by revenue. The heirs of Sam Walton remain among the richest families on Earth.

### 07_lesson
Sam Walton's story has two lessons. The first is that a good idea works best in the place that everyone else ignores. While rivals fought for big cities, Walton quietly won the small towns, and by the time they noticed, it was too late.

The second is to stay close to the customer. The richest man in America wore a cap, drove a pickup, and listened to cashiers. He used to say there is only one boss, the customer, and he can fire everybody in the company simply by spending his money somewhere else.
