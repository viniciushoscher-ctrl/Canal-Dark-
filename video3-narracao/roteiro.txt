### 00_hook
In 1848, a twelve-year-old boy stepped off a ship in America with nothing. Within a few years he was earning one dollar and twenty cents a week, breathing cotton dust in a factory basement.

Fifty-three years later, that same boy sold his company for four hundred and eighty million dollars, and the banker buying it said six words: "You are now the richest man in the world."

His name was Andrew Carnegie. His steel built the bridges, the railroads and the skyscrapers of a new country. And then, before he died, he gave away almost every dollar.

Was it generosity? Or was it the most brilliant cover-up in the history of American business?

To understand the man who gave it all away, you first have to see how he took it all. This is the story of Andrew Carnegie.
### 01_cap1
Andrew Carnegie was born on November twenty-fifth, eighteen thirty-five, in Dunfermline, Scotland. His father, William, was a handloom weaver, a proud craftsman who worked at a wooden loom in the family's one-room home.

Then the steam-powered factories arrived. A machine could do in an hour what William needed a day to finish, and the orders simply stopped. The family went from comfortable to desperate in a few years.

Young Andrew watched his father break. He never forgot it. Later he wrote that he wanted to be the one who owned the machines, not the one crushed by them.

In eighteen forty-eight, the family sold what little they had, borrowed money, and sailed to Pennsylvania. Andrew was twelve.

His first job was in a cotton mill near Pittsburgh. He changed bobbins for twelve hours a day, six days a week, for a dollar and twenty cents.

But the boy had something the other workers didn't. He wasn't planning to stay.
### 02_cap2
At thirteen, Carnegie got a better job: messenger boy at a Pittsburgh telegraph office, for two dollars and fifty cents a week.

While other boys wasted their free time, he memorized every street and every business in the city. He learned the names of the powerful men who received his messages, and he made sure they remembered his.

Then he taught himself to read the telegraph by sound, something almost nobody could do. Soon he was an operator.

One of the men who noticed was Thomas Scott, a top executive at the Pennsylvania Railroad, the biggest company in America. In eighteen fifty-three, Scott hired the eighteen-year-old Carnegie as his personal secretary.

It was the best education money couldn't buy. Carnegie sat beside the man who ran a giant, and learned how costs, schedules and people were managed on a scale nobody had ever seen.

And Scott would soon give him something worth far more than a salary: his first chance to become rich.
### 03_cap3
Scott let his young protégé in on investments. The first was shares in Adams Express, a delivery company. Carnegie didn't have the money, so his mother mortgaged the family home to help him buy in.

It paid off. The dividends arrived, and the boy who changed bobbins now had an income from capital.

Then came the Civil War. Scott became a senior official in Washington, and Carnegie went with him to run the military telegraph and the railroads that moved the Union army. He learned how a war machine runs on iron, rails and speed.

The war made iron the most valuable material in America. Carnegie put his money into iron works, a sleeping-car company, a bridge-building firm, and a small Pennsylvania oil farm that paid enormous dividends.

By the time he left the railroad in eighteen sixty-five, he was already a wealthy man in his twenties.

But iron was only the beginning. Across the ocean, he was about to see something that would change everything.
### 04_cap4
In eighteen seventy-two, Carnegie visited England and watched a new process called the Bessemer converter. A blast of air turned molten iron into steel in minutes, instead of days.

Steel was stronger than iron and far more durable. Everyone knew it would be important. Carnegie understood it would be everything.

Back in Pittsburgh, he did what most businessmen of the time were afraid to do: he bet it all. He sold his other interests and poured the money into a giant new mill on the Monongahela River.

He named it the Edgar Thomson Works, after the president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, his biggest future customer. It was a very deliberate piece of flattery.

The mill opened in eighteen seventy-five, just as the country was sinking into a depression. Carnegie didn't care. When competitors cut production, he kept building.

His rule was simple: cut costs, cut prices, and take the customers of everyone who couldn't keep up.
### 05_cap5
Carnegie's genius was control. He didn't just make steel. He bought the iron ore fields, the coal and coke supply, the ships and the railroads that carried them. Every step between the ground and the finished beam belonged to him.

No one else in America was doing it at this scale. When a supplier raised prices, Carnegie simply owned the supplier.

He obsessed over numbers. Every night, he wanted to know the exact cost of every ton. Managers who couldn't cut costs were replaced, and those who could became partners, bound to him by shares.

In eighteen eighty-nine he brought in Henry Clay Frick, the coke king, and made him chairman. Frick was a hard man who believed workers were an expense to be managed.

By nineteen hundred, Carnegie's mills were making more steel than all of Great Britain.

But efficiency has a price. Workers in the mills labored twelve-hour shifts, seven days a week, and the pay was falling as profits climbed.

Something was going to break.
### 06_cap6
In eighteen ninety-two, the contract with the union at Carnegie's Homestead plant was about to expire. Carnegie, who liked to call himself a friend of working men, left for his castle in Scotland. He left Frick in charge.

Frick wanted to break the union. He cut wages, built a fence topped with barbed wire around the plant, and locked the workers out.

Then he hired three hundred armed Pinkerton agents and sent them up the river on barges, at night, to take the mill by force.

The workers were waiting. On July sixth, a battle broke out on the riverbank. Guns, dynamite, burning oil on the water. By the end, men on both sides were dead.

The state militia arrived, the strike was crushed, and the union was effectively broken at Homestead.

Carnegie was thousands of miles away. He had always claimed to support workers' rights. Yet he never repudiated what was done in his name, and the steel kept flowing.

The public never forgot. The man who preached about uplifting the poor had just crushed them.
### 07_cap7
By the end of the century, Carnegie was in his sixties and tired of the fight. He wanted out.

At a dinner in eighteen ninety-nine, a young steel man casually asked what Carnegie's company might be worth. The idea spread, and it reached the most powerful banker in America: J.P. Morgan.

Morgan wanted to merge the steel industry into a single giant. Carnegie's company was the one piece he couldn't build without. Carnegie scribbled a number on a piece of paper: four hundred and eighty million dollars.

Morgan glanced at it and said, "I accept."

In nineteen oh-one, the deal created United States Steel, the first billion-dollar corporation in history. Carnegie's share was roughly two hundred and twenty-five million dollars.

As the story goes, Morgan shook his hand and said, "Congratulations, Mr. Carnegie. You are now the richest man in the world."

Carnegie had reached the very top.

And then, at sixty-six years old, he did something no one expected.
### 08_cap8
Years earlier, in eighteen eighty-nine, Carnegie had written an essay that shocked the rich. He called it "The Gospel of Wealth." Its central idea was brutal: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

He argued that a fortune was a trust. The wealthy had a duty to spend it, during their lifetime, on things that lifted society.

Now he meant it. He retired and went to work giving it away. He funded more than two thousand five hundred public libraries across the world, a concert hall in New York that still carries his name, universities, research institutes, and a palace in The Hague dedicated to peace.

By the time he died, in nineteen nineteen, he had given away roughly ninety percent of his fortune.

But the twist is what the money couldn't buy. Many of those libraries stood in mill towns where the men who filled his furnaces worked twelve-hour days.

He gave them books. Critics said he should have given them higher wages and shorter hours.

The question follows him to this day. Was the giving an act of conscience, or the price of a clean name?
### 09_lesson
Andrew Carnegie was two men at once. He was the boy who understood that whoever controls the process controls the profit, and he spent forty years building a machine that no competitor could match.

And he was the man who, once he had won, understood that a fortune means nothing if it dies with you.

His story holds a warning and a gift. The warning: efficiency without conscience crushes people, and a library can't undo a battle on a riverbank. The gift: wealth is only worth what you build with it.

The poor weaver's son changed a country twice. Once with steel, and once with generosity.

Which one will history remember? That, I'll leave to you.
