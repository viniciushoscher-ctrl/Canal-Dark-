### 00_hook
The eighteenth of June, eighteen fifteen. On a muddy field in Belgium, near a village called Waterloo, Napoleon Bonaparte faces the Duke of Wellington in the battle that will decide the fate of Europe. In London, the entire financial world is waiting. If Napoleon wins, British government bonds will collapse. If Wellington wins, they will soar.

A man stands in the Stock Exchange, leaning against a pillar, his face unreadable. His name is Nathan Rothschild. When he starts to sell, the market panics. Everyone believes he has secret news that Napoleon has won. They sell too, and the bonds crash.

But, says the legend, Nathan knew the truth. His couriers had brought him the news of Wellington's victory a day before the government heard. While the market collapsed, his agents were buying everything, quietly, at ruinous prices. When the truth arrived, he made a fortune.

Historians debate whether this famous story is true. But it captures something real. The family behind it, the Rothschilds, became the most powerful financial dynasty in history. This is the story of how five brothers from a Jewish ghetto built an empire that financed kings, wars, and railroads.

### 01_cap1
The story begins in Frankfurt, Germany, in the seventeen hundreds. Jews in the city were forced to live in a crowded, walled quarter called the Judengasse, the Jewish Lane. The gates were locked at night, and on Sundays and holidays. Jews could not own land, join most guilds, or hold public office. They could only trade in money, and in old goods.

Mayer Amschel Rothschild was born in this ghetto in seventeen forty-four. His family's house bore a sign of a red shield, in German, Rot Schild, which became the family's name. His father, a small merchant and moneylender, died when Mayer was eleven.

Mayer studied to become a rabbi, but then he was sent to work at a bank in Hanover. There he learned finance. When he returned to Frankfurt, he built a modest business, buying and selling old coins and medals, which he sold to collectors, among them an important client, Prince William of Hesse-Kassel, one of the richest rulers in Europe.

Mayer won the prince's trust, and that was the first step. He married Gutle Schnapper, and they had ten children, five sons and five daughters. And Mayer had a plan for the sons.

### 02_cap2
When the French armies swept over Germany, in the seventeen nineties, Prince William fled, and he entrusted his enormous fortune to Mayer Rothschild. According to legend, Mayer hid the money in wine barrels, and kept it safe from Napoleon's soldiers. In reality, he invested it, and made a profit, and was so honest that he gained a lasting reputation.

Mayer had realized that a family business could be stronger than any single bank. He sent his sons to the great capitals of Europe. Amschel stayed in Frankfurt. Salomon went to Vienna. Nathan went to London. Carl went to Naples. And James went to Paris.

Each of the five brothers ran his own house, but they acted as a single team. They shared information, shared risks, and sent letters and couriers constantly. They wrote to each other in a special code of Judeo-German, in Hebrew letters, to keep the secrets from outsiders.

Mayer Amschel died in eighteen twelve. But on his deathbed, he made his sons promise to stay together. His will required that the business be led by male family members, that they marry within the family, and that they never go to court against each other.

### 03_cap3
The most talented of the five was Nathan. Born in seventeen seventy-seven, he moved to Manchester at the age of twenty-one, to buy English cloth, and then to London in eighteen oh nine. He was a bold, blunt, and tireless man. He built a bank, Nathan Mayer Rothschild and Sons.

During the Napoleonic Wars, Nathan found a very profitable role. He helped the British government to send gold to Wellington's army in Spain and Portugal. It was a perilous business, which required secret routes through enemy territory. Nathan used his brothers, with their network across Europe, to move the gold. The risk was great, but so were the fees.

The Rothschild network of couriers and carrier pigeons became legendary. They were faster than the government's own messengers, and they carried news of battles and markets. In an age without telegraphs, speed was money.

After Waterloo, Nathan Rothschild was the most important financier in London. In the following years, he helped the British government to refinance its enormous war debts. And the family were now the main lenders to governments across Europe.

### 04_cap4
In the following decades, the Rothschilds became the bankers of kings and emperors. They financed the British government, and they helped fund the purchase of the Suez Canal, in eighteen seventy-five, when Prime Minister Benjamin Disraeli needed four million pounds, overnight, to buy the shares of the Egyptian ruler. Nathan's son Lionel provided the money in a few days.

They also played a major part in the railway age. In the eighteen thirties and forties, they financed railroads across France, Austria, and Italy, helping to build the transport network of Europe. James Rothschild, in Paris, became the richest banker in France, and the owner of the Chemin de Fer du Nord.

They were pioneers in bond markets. Rather than lend their own money, they sold bonds to many investors, spreading the risk. This allowed them to raise much larger sums for governments than a single bank could.

By the eighteen forties, the Rothschild family was the largest private bank in the world. At one point, their combined wealth was greater than the next largest banks put together. They were said to be worth more than all the other great banking families combined.

### 05_cap5
The family also won something their parents could not have imagined, respect and status. In eighteen twenty-two, the Austrian emperor made the five brothers barons. In eighteen forty-seven, Lionel de Rothschild was elected to the British Parliament, but because he was Jewish, he could not take the oath, which was in the name of a Christian faith. For eleven years, he was re-elected again and again and refused his seat. In eighteen fifty-eight, the law was changed, and he took his seat as the first practicing Jewish member of the House of Commons.

In eighteen eighty-five, Lionel's son Nathan became the first Jewish member of the House of Lords, as Baron Rothschild.

The family built vast country houses, in England and France, among them Waddesdon Manor, filled with art. They collected paintings, porcelain, and books. They built vineyards, and one of their wine estates, Chateau Lafite Rothschild, became among the most famous wines in the world.

They also gave huge amounts to charity, and funded hospitals, schools, and housing for the poor. Many in the family were committed to supporting the Jewish community, and Edmond de Rothschild supported the early Jewish settlements in Palestine.

### 06_cap6
But the Rothschilds also became the target of one of the ugliest myths in history. Because they were Jewish and rich, their name was used by anti-Semites to build conspiracy theories. For more than a century, writers, newspapers, and later the Nazis claimed that the Rothschilds secretly controlled governments and started wars. It was false. But it was a powerful lie, and it has never disappeared.

In reality, the family was cautious and often reluctant to fund wars. They preferred peace, which was better for trade. In eighteen forty-eight, when revolutions swept through Europe, and in nineteen fourteen, when war broke out, they were afraid, and they tried to prevent it.

In the twentieth century, the family lost much of its power. Two world wars and high taxes took a heavy toll. In nineteen thirty-eight, when Nazi Germany took over Austria, the Vienna branch of the family was seized, and Baron Louis Rothschild was imprisoned and forced to give up his fortune. Many in the family escaped. The old banks of Frankfurt, Vienna, and Naples were closed.

The family survived, and began again, as a smaller, but still significant, banking and investment business.

### 07_cap7
Today, the Rothschild name lives on in banks and investment firms, in London, Paris, and beyond. Rothschild and Company, a bank based in Paris, advises governments and corporations on mergers and restructuring. And the family's wine estates still produce some of the finest bottles in the world.

The family's fortune is impossible to measure, since it is spread among many descendants and branches. But in its prime, in the nineteenth century, it was probably the largest private fortune in the world, measured as a share of the world's economy.

The real legacy of the Rothschilds lies in how they changed finance. They built a truly international bank. They proved that information, trust, and family loyalty could be a form of capital. And they showed that a persecuted minority, with talent and discipline, could rise from a locked ghetto to the highest levels of power.

One story from Mayer Amschel's last days remains. When he died, he is said to have told his sons that if they wanted to stay rich, they must stay together.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from the Rothschilds?

The first lesson is that information is power. Their couriers and codes gave them news before anyone else. In every era, whoever knows first, wins.

The second lesson is to build a network you can trust. Five brothers, in five countries, working as one, beat banks that stood alone. Trust is a form of capital.

The third lesson is to spread your risk. By selling bonds to many investors, they lent to kings without risking everything.

And the final lesson is that unity outlasts brilliance. Mayer Amschel asked his sons to stay together. For a century, they did, and it made them the greatest financial family in the world.
