### 00_hook
May tenth, eighteen sixty-nine. In a barren desert valley in Utah, called Promontory Summit, a crowd of workers, soldiers, and officials gathers around a stretch of track. A man in a black suit steps forward and lifts a heavy hammer. In front of him is a spike made of solid gold. He swings, and he misses.

The telegraph operator taps out one word to the entire country anyway: done. Across America, bells ring, cannons fire, and crowds cheer. The first railroad across the continent is complete, and the man who held the hammer is the president of the company that built it.

He had been a failed lawyer, a grocer in a mining camp, and a governor who campaigned on racism. And yet he became one of the richest men in America, and founded a university in memory of his dead son. His name is Leland Stanford. And this is the story of how a grocer rode a railroad to a fortune, and a tragedy to immortality.

### 01_cap1
Amasa Leland Stanford was born on March ninth, eighteen twenty-four, near Albany, New York. His father ran an inn, and a farm, and was prosperous. Leland was one of eight children, a solid, serious young man, not a brilliant scholar but determined.

He studied law and was admitted to the bar in eighteen forty-eight. He moved to Wisconsin and opened a practice in Port Washington. The next year, a fire destroyed his law library, and his career stalled. When his brothers went to California for the Gold Rush, he stayed home with his new wife, Jane Lathrop, a charming woman from Albany.

Then he decided to follow. In eighteen fifty-two, Leland left Jane with her family, and sailed to California. He arrived in Sacramento with almost nothing. His brothers had opened a general store in a mining camp, and he joined them.

### 02_cap2
Stanford sold shovels, pickaxes, flour, and whiskey to miners. The gold seekers often paid in gold dust, and they were desperate for supplies. His store made a steady profit. In a few years, he moved to Sacramento and opened a larger grocery business, and he became a respected local merchant.

He also moved into politics, as a Republican. In eighteen fifty-nine, he ran for governor of California, and lost. In eighteen sixty-one, he ran again, and this time he won, in part because of a split among the Democrats. He took office as California's first Republican governor, just as the Civil War began.

His campaigns were controversial. He ran on a white-supremacist platform, and called Chinese immigrants the dregs of Asia. It was a stain on his record that would haunt his legacy, especially because his railroad would soon depend on the labor of thousands of Chinese workers.

The Chinese workers lived on rice, dried fish, and tea, and they boiled their water, which kept many of them healthier than their white colleagues. When they went on strike in eighteen sixty-seven, asking for equal pay, Crocker cut off their food supplies until they returned. The strike failed.

But in Sacramento, a different kind of group was gathering around him. Four local merchants, all in their thirties and forties, had met a young engineer with a big idea.

### 03_cap3
The engineer was Theodore Judah, who believed that a railroad could cross the Sierra Nevada mountains. Nobody had thought it possible. Judah had found a route, and he tried to raise money in San Francisco, but the bankers laughed at him. He found four Sacramento merchants who listened: Stanford, a hardware dealer named Collis Huntington, his partner Mark Hopkins, and a dry goods seller, Charles Crocker.

They became known as the Big Four. In eighteen sixty-one, they formed the Central Pacific Railroad. Stanford was elected president, because he was well known and politically connected. Huntington handled finances in Washington. Crocker managed construction. Hopkins kept the books.

In eighteen sixty-two, President Abraham Lincoln signed the Pacific Railway Act. It gave the Central Pacific land grants and government loans: sixteen thousand dollars for every mile of flat track, and up to forty-eight thousand for mountain miles. It was a gift worth millions.

### 04_cap4
Building the Central Pacific was one of the hardest engineering projects ever attempted. The line had to climb the Sierra Nevada, through granite and snow. Workers were hard to find, because miners were leaving for gold. So Crocker hired Chinese laborers, thousands of them, who proved to be skilled and tireless.

They hung in baskets from cliffs to drill holes for blasting powder. They tunneled through solid rock, sometimes progressing only a foot a day. In the winter of eighteen sixty-six and sixty-seven, avalanches buried camps. Hundreds died, perhaps more, and their deaths were barely recorded. They were paid less than white workers.

Meanwhile, the Big Four used the railroad to enrich themselves. They set up a construction company, the Credit and Finance Company, to which the railroad paid inflated fees. In effect, they paid themselves with the government's money. They also exaggerated the distance of the mountains, claiming that the foothills of the Sierra began at Sacramento, to receive the higher payments.

On May tenth, eighteen sixty-nine, the Central Pacific met the Union Pacific at Promontory Summit.

### 05_cap5
After the transcontinental railroad, the Big Four expanded. They acquired the Southern Pacific, and built lines across California, Arizona, Texas, and Louisiana. They controlled nearly all transportation in California. Farmers and merchants complained that the railroad set high rates, and ruined competitors. People called it the Octopus.

The company's power reached Washington. Huntington spent huge sums on lobbying, and bought votes in Congress. Newspapers published his letters, which revealed how much the railroad paid for favors. The scandal made the Big Four hated, but it did not stop them.

Stanford lived in a mansion on Nob Hill in San Francisco. He built a huge stock farm in Palo Alto, where he bred racehorses. A photographer named Eadweard Muybridge, hired by Stanford, used a row of cameras to prove that a galloping horse lifts all four feet off the ground. It was a milestone in photography, and the roots of motion pictures.

In eighteen ninety-three, Stanford served in the U.S. Senate, but his greatest passion was his only child, Leland Junior. A bright, sensitive boy, Leland Junior was destined to inherit everything.

### 06_cap6
In eighteen eighty-four, the family traveled to Europe. In Italy, Leland Junior caught typhoid fever. He died in Florence, just before his sixteenth birthday. Leland and Jane were devastated. Stanford fell into a deep despair.

One night, he said, he dreamed that his son spoke to him. The children of California would be his children now. He and Jane decided to use their fortune to found a university in memory of their son: Leland Stanford Junior University. They donated the Palo Alto farm, and about twenty million dollars. It opened in eighteen ninety-one.

Stanford died on June twenty-first, eighteen ninety-three. The railroad refused to pay his estate, which tied up the university in court. Jane saved it with her own money, living frugally. In the following decades, the university grew into one of the world's greatest, and the cradle of Silicon Valley.

### 07_lesson
Leland Stanford's story holds two lessons. The first is that great fortunes are often built on public power. His railroad profited from loans, land, and the sweat of workers who got little in return. It reminds us that who builds an empire is as important as who owns it.

The second is that grief can create a lasting purpose. Stanford turned his loss into an institution that has shaped the modern world, and in a university in Palo Alto, his son's name lives on.
