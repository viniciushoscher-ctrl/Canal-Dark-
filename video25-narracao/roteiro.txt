### 00_hook
May tenth, eighteen sixty-nine. In the desert of Utah, at a place called Promontory Summit, a crowd gathers around two steam locomotives that stand nose to nose. A man in a dark suit lifts a heavy hammer, and swings it at a spike made of gold. He misses. The crowd laughs, and a telegraph operator taps out the news anyway: done.

The first railroad across America is complete. And the man who swung the hammer had come west after a fire destroyed his law practice, with almost nothing.

His name is Leland Stanford. And this is the story of the railroad baron who built a university in memory of his son.

### 01_cap1
Amasa Leland Stanford was born on March ninth, eighteen twenty-four, in Watervliet, New York. His father was a farmer and a contractor, and the family was comfortable. Leland went to school, and studied law in Albany.

In eighteen fifty, he moved to Port Washington, Wisconsin, where he opened a law practice. It was a bad moment. In eighteen fifty-two, a fire destroyed his law library, and his savings. He lost almost everything.

The same year, he did what thousands of Americans were doing. He went west, to California, where the Gold Rush was under way. His brothers had already opened stores in the mining towns. Leland would sell supplies to the miners.

### 02_cap2
Stanford ran a general store in a camp called Michigan Bluff, and later in Sacramento. He learned that the people who sold shovels and food often made more money than the miners who dug for gold. In time, he became a respected merchant, and entered politics. In eighteen sixty-one, he was elected governor of California, a Republican who supported Abraham Lincoln.

That same year, an engineer named Theodore Judah came to Sacramento with a bold plan. He had found a path through the Sierra Nevada for a railroad. He needed investors, and four shopkeepers answered. They were Leland Stanford, Collis Huntington, Mark Hopkins, and Charles Crocker. History would call them the Big Four.

They formed the Central Pacific Railroad, and Stanford became its president.

### 03_cap3
The job was brutal. The Central Pacific had to climb the Sierra Nevada, blasting tunnels through granite, in deep snow. Most of the workers were Chinese immigrants, thousands of them, who did the most dangerous work for low pay. Many died in avalanches and accidents.

Meanwhile, the Union Pacific was building west from Nebraska, and the two companies raced to lay more track, because the government paid by the mile. In eighteen sixty-nine, the two lines met at Promontory Summit, in Utah.

The Big Four became rich, through government loans and land. Stanford was now one of the wealthiest men in California. He built a mansion in Sacramento, and later a larger one in San Francisco, on Nob Hill.

### 04_cap4
Stanford also loved horses. He bought a large farm in Palo Alto, and bred trotting horses. In eighteen seventy-two, he hired a photographer named Eadweard Muybridge to settle a bet, on whether a galloping horse has all four hooves off the ground at the same time.

Muybridge set up a line of cameras, and captured the answer in a series of photographs. A horse does, for a moment, fly. Those images are considered one of the first steps toward motion pictures.

In his later years, Stanford served again in public life. In eighteen eighty-five, he was elected United States senator for California, a seat he held until he died.

### 05_cap5
Stanford had one son, Leland Junior, born in eighteen sixty-eight. The boy was bright, and his parents adored him. In eighteen eighty-four, the family was traveling in Europe, and Leland Junior caught typhoid fever. He died in Florence, Italy, at fifteen years old.

Stanford and his wife, Jane, were heartbroken. After the funeral, he is said to have told his wife that the children of California would be their children. In eighteen eighty-five, they founded Leland Stanford Junior University, on the Palo Alto farm.

The university opened in eighteen ninety-one. It was coeducational, and it was open to anyone, which was unusual at the time. Stanford gave most of his wealth to it.

### 06_cap6
After his death on June twenty-first, eighteen ninety-three, the university nearly closed. The government sued the estate for the unpaid railroad loans, and Jane Stanford had to save it by cutting her own household costs to almost nothing. She kept it alive until the courts finished the case.

Stanford's reputation has always been mixed. The railroad he led made huge profits, and used workers who were treated unfairly. He was also criticized for his views about Chinese immigrants, even though the railroad could not have been built without them.

But the university, and the technology industry that grew around it, became his most lasting legacy. Silicon Valley owes much of its origin to Stanford.

### 07_lesson
Stanford's story shows two sides of ambition. He built an empire, and he took advantage of workers and government loans. And yet he also turned great tragedy into something that lasted.

If you want to leave a legacy, ask what you would build if you could not take the money with you. The railroad ended up being replaced. The university, and the idea behind it, kept growing.
