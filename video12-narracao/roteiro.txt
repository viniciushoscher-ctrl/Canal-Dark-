### 00_hook
Nineteen thirty-seven. A packed theater in Los Angeles goes dark, and a cartoon begins. A princess sings to a wishing well, and seven dwarfs march home from the mine. By the end, grown men in suits are wiping tears from their eyes. Hollywood had never seen anything like it.

Everyone in the industry had told the man behind it that it would be a disaster. They called it Disney's Folly. Nobody, they said, would sit through ninety minutes of cartoons. He bet his house, his studio, and everything he owned on being wrong about them.

His name is Walt Disney. And before he built the most valuable entertainment empire on Earth, he went bankrupt, lost his first big character to a thief, and slept in a studio with mice for company. This is how a man with no money built a kingdom out of a mouse.

### 01_cap1
Walter Elias Disney was born on December fifth, nineteen-oh-one, in Chicago. His father, Elias, was a strict and restless man who moved the family from one failing farm and business to another. Young Walt delivered newspapers before dawn, in the snow, and was often beaten when his work was not good enough.

But he had one escape. He drew. In the middle of Missouri, he sketched the farm animals, and neighbors paid him a few pennies for the pictures. At sixteen he lied about his age to join the Red Cross as an ambulance driver in the First World War. He covered the vehicle with cartoons, and he arrived in France just after the fighting stopped.

When he returned to Kansas City, he took a job at an advertising studio, where he learned the new art of animation. At twenty-one, with a borrowed camera and a friend named Ub Iwerks, he started his own tiny company. They called it Laugh-O-Gram.

### 02_cap2
Laugh-O-Gram made short fairy-tale cartoons, and for a time, it worked. But Walt was a poor businessman. He spent every dollar he earned on new ideas. In eighteen months he was bankrupt. He could not pay his rent, and he ate cold beans from a can. He slept in his office, where, he later said, the mice kept him company. One mouse in particular he fed crumbs from his lunch.

In nineteen twenty-three, with forty dollars in his pocket, he boarded a train to Hollywood. He joined his brother Roy, who had always believed in him, and together they opened a small studio in the back of an uncle's garage. It was called the Disney Brothers Cartoon Studio.

The early years were brutally hard. Walt drew at night, then drove around Los Angeles to find theaters that would show his work. Roy kept the books and borrowed from relatives, and the two brothers rarely took a salary. Walt sometimes skipped meals so the animators could be paid on Friday. That loyalty to his small crew would define him for the rest of his life.

Their first success was a series about a girl named Alice. Then came Oswald the Lucky Rabbit, a character that looked set to make them rich. And that is when the worst thing happened.

### 03_cap3
In nineteen twenty-eight, Walt traveled to New York to negotiate a better deal for Oswald. Instead, the distributor, a man named Charles Mintz, told him something shocking. Mintz owned the character, not Walt. He had also quietly hired away most of Walt's animators. Disney could accept a lower fee, or he could lose everything.

Walt refused. On the long train ride back to California, he was ruined and furious. But somewhere between the stations, he remembered the little mouse from his old office in Kansas City.

He sketched a new character, round-eared and cheerful, with a high voice. His wife, Lillian, thought that Mortimer was a silly name. She suggested Mickey. And the secret ingredient was that this time, Walt and Roy would own everything, and never trust a distributor with it again.

Ub Iwerks drew the first cartoons, almost alone, working in a locked room to keep the secret from Mintz.

### 04_cap4
The first two Mickey cartoons found no buyer. Then Walt made a bold decision. Talking pictures had just arrived in theaters, so he made a cartoon with sound. On November eighteenth, nineteen twenty-eight, Steamboat Willie premiered in New York. Audiences laughed and cheered. A mouse whistled, and played a cow's teeth like a xylophone, in perfect time with the music.

Walt did the voice of Mickey himself, a high, squeaky falsetto he kept for nearly twenty years. He also learned that perfection mattered more than speed. He made his animators redo scenes again and again, and he demanded that every gesture of the mouse show a clear emotion, not just a joke.

Suddenly, Mickey Mouse was everywhere. Within a year there were Mickey dolls, books, watches, and lunchboxes. Disney understood something that no other filmmaker did. A cartoon character could earn money from far more than the film itself.

By the early thirties, he added color with Technicolor, and signed an exclusive deal for the process. He won an Academy Award for a cartoon called Flowers and Trees. Then he made the biggest decision of his life. He would make a full-length animated feature film, something no one had ever done.

### 05_cap5
The film was Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs. Production lasted three years, and it cost almost one and a half million dollars, in the middle of the Great Depression. Every time the budget grew, Walt borrowed more. Roy begged him to stop, and the bank threatened to cut him off. Walt mortgaged his own home to finish it.

The press called it Disney's Folly. Critics were sure that audiences would be bored, or that the bright colors would hurt their eyes. At the premiere in December nineteen thirty-seven, Hollywood's biggest stars were in the room, and when it ended, the entire audience stood and cheered.

Snow White earned more than eight million dollars in its first run, which made it the most successful film in history at that time. Walt used the profits to build a new studio in Burbank. He followed with Pinocchio, Fantasia, Dumbo, and Bambi, though none matched the first.

Then the war came, and Disney's overseas markets collapsed. Debts grew. A strike by his animators in nineteen forty-one broke Walt's heart, because he saw his artists as a family. For a time, the studio nearly collapsed.

### 06_cap6
After the war, Walt looked for a new idea. He remembered taking his daughters to amusement parks, where he sat on a bench eating peanuts, while the girls rode the carousel. He wanted a place where parents and children could play together.

Everyone thought it was crazy. Bankers refused to lend him money. So Walt turned to television, which Hollywood considered a threat. In exchange for financing, ABC aired a weekly show called Disneyland, which advertised the park to millions of homes.

On July seventeenth, nineteen fifty-five, Disneyland opened in Anaheim, California. The opening day was a disaster. Counterfeit tickets brought crowds twice the size expected, the asphalt was still wet, and there was not enough drinking water. Reporters called it Black Sunday.

What made Disneyland different was a rule Walt repeated to his designers. Every detail must tell a story. Workers were called cast members, the streets were swept constantly, and the park was built like a movie set, so that guests walked inside a film. Competitors had built messy, cheap fairs. Walt built a world, and people paid to be part of it.

But the guests kept coming. Within seven weeks the park welcomed a million visitors. Walt planned something even bigger, a second park in Florida, with room for an entire city of the future. He did not live to see it open. He died of lung cancer on December fifteenth, nineteen sixty-six.

### 07_lesson
Walt Disney's story holds two lessons. The first is to own what you create. After losing Oswald, he never let anyone else control his characters again, and that single decision created the largest entertainment company in the world.

There is also a quieter lesson in the way he treated failure. He lost a studio, a character, and nearly his home, and each time, he used the loss as fuel. The mouse itself was born on a train ride after the worst day of his career.

The second is that people will call your biggest idea a folly, right until it succeeds. Snow White, Disneyland, and Mickey Mouse were all called impossible. Walt kept saying the same thing: it is kind of fun to do the impossible.
