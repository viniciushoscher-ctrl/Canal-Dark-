### 00_hook
Nineteen forty-three. A fifteen-year-old boy kneels beside the bed of his dying father in a cramped apartment in Hong Kong. The father, a school headmaster, has tuberculosis, and he cannot afford a doctor. The family has fled a war. They have no money, no home, and no future.

When the old man dies, the boy does something that most children never have to do. He quits school, the thing his father valued most, and takes a job to feed his mother and his younger brother and sister. He works from dawn to midnight, sixteen hours a day, carrying a heavy bag of goods through the streets.

Today, that boy owns ports on six continents, mobile phone networks in dozens of countries, supermarkets, power plants, and a huge portion of the land in the city where he once begged for work.

He is called Superman in Hong Kong, and his name is known across Asia. This is the story of Li Ka-shing, who turned plastic flowers into a global empire.

### 01_cap1
Li Ka-shing was born on the twenty-ninth of July, nineteen twenty-eight, in the city of Chaozhou, in the province of Guangdong, in southern China. His family came from a line of scholars. His father, Li Yunjing, was a primary school principal, and his mother, Zhuang Bijing, was devoted to her children. In a country where most people were poor farmers, they belonged to the educated class.

But China in the nineteen thirties was a country in chaos. Warlords fought over provinces. Famine spread. And in nineteen thirty-seven, Japan invaded. When the soldiers reached the region of Chaozhou, in nineteen forty, the Li family was forced to flee. With little more than the clothes they wore, they traveled south to the British colony of Hong Kong.

Hong Kong was crowded with refugees, and the family had to rely on relatives. They lived in a small, crowded room. Ka-shing, who was twelve, was an excellent student, and he continued his school, learning English by reading a dictionary. He said later that he would take old dictionaries from the street market, memorize the words, and then sell the book back to the vendor.

In nineteen forty-one, Japan invaded Hong Kong, and life became harder still. Then in nineteen forty-three, his father died.

### 02_cap2
At fifteen, Li Ka-shing became the head of his family. He left school and went to work for a plastics trading company, selling plastic belts and other products. At first, he did the dirtiest jobs, carrying goods, cleaning, and running errands. But he was clever, and he worked harder than anyone else.

He later described his approach. He would wake up early, sleep late, and use every moment to learn. He studied his customers. He read about the plastic industry in the evenings. He learned English at night. And he saved half of everything he earned.

His boss noticed the hard worker. By the age of seventeen, Li was the best salesman in the company. At eighteen, he was the production manager. At twenty, he was the general manager of a plastics factory, the boss of workers who were much older than he was.

It was a rapid rise. But Li was not satisfied. In nineteen fifty, at the age of twenty-two, he decided to start his own company. He had a small amount of savings, and a loan from relatives. He rented a tiny factory space in Hong Kong, in a rundown industrial building, and bought a few old machines. He called it Cheung Kong, which means the Yangtze River, the great river of China. It was a promise that his small stream would one day become a river.

### 03_cap3
The first years of Cheung Kong were difficult. It made plastic toys and containers, but it was a crowded market, and profits were small. The turning point came in nineteen fifty-seven.

Li was reading a plastics magazine when he saw an article about an Italian company that was making artificial flowers from a new plastic material. He realized that this was a great idea. Hong Kong had many cheap workers, and plastic flowers could be made in large amounts. The cost was low, and artificial flowers were becoming fashionable in Europe and the United States.

He decided to go to Italy himself to see how the flowers were made. He toured the Italian workshops, and quietly learned the techniques. Then he returned to Hong Kong, bought new molds, and started to produce plastic flowers of his own.

They were a hit. By the early nineteen sixties, Li Ka-shing was known as the Plastic Flower King. His flowers were exported to America and Europe, and he became a millionaire before he was thirty-five.

But he had learned a lesson in business. He noticed that the plastic flower market could vanish as quickly as it had arrived. He wanted something more permanent. He looked around Hong Kong, and he saw it was growing, and that its land was limited. He turned his attention to real estate.

### 04_cap4
In the nineteen fifties and sixties, Li began to buy small pieces of land and build apartment blocks. At first he did it carefully, in small steps. He reinvested the profits from his factory, and he borrowed from banks.

Then came the great test. In nineteen sixty-seven, Hong Kong was shaken by riots. Communist supporters clashed with the British authorities, and thousands of people lost faith in the city. Bombs exploded in the streets. Property owners panicked. Many families sold their land and fled abroad, for whatever price they could get.

Li took the opposite view. He believed that Hong Kong would survive, and that the riots would pass. While others sold, he bought. He bought land, apartments, and buildings at rock bottom prices.

He was right. Within a few years, the unrest ended, and Hong Kong's economy exploded. Property prices rose many times over. His company, Cheung Kong, began to develop big building projects. In nineteen seventy-two, it was listed on the Hong Kong stock exchange. Li Ka-shing had become one of the biggest developers in the city.

He gave a simple rule for these years. When everyone is afraid, buy. When everyone is greedy, be careful. It was a lesson he would repeat for the rest of his life.

### 05_cap5
By the end of the nineteen seventies, Li wanted to expand beyond real estate. And he found a target. Hutchison Whampoa was an old British trading company, one of the great trading houses of Hong Kong, with docks, shops, and cement works. It was in trouble, and its main shareholder, the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, was looking for a way out.

In nineteen seventy-nine, Li made a bold move. He agreed to buy a controlling share of Hutchison Whampoa for about six hundred and thirty-nine million Hong Kong dollars. It was the first time that a Chinese businessman had taken over one of the great British trading houses of Hong Kong. It was a symbolic moment, the end of the colonial era in business.

Li did not stop there. In the nineteen eighties, he bought Hongkong Electric, the company that supplied the power for the island. He expanded into container ports, building terminals in Hong Kong and then around the world. He bought a stake in the Canadian oil firm Husky Oil. He built a retail empire with the pharmacy chain Watsons.

By the nineteen nineties, he was the richest man in Asia. He was a master dealmaker, quiet and patient, always willing to wait. He once said that a good businessman should be able to make a deal that is good for both sides, so that everyone wants to work with him again.

### 06_cap6
In the nineteen nineties, he made the biggest bet of his career. He saw that mobile phones would change the world, and he invested billions in telecom networks. In nineteen ninety-four, his company launched Orange, a mobile network in Britain. It was a success. A few years later, in nineteen ninety-nine, Li sold Orange to the German company Mannesmann for about fourteen billion dollars. It was one of the best business deals in history.

When Hong Kong returned to Chinese rule in nineteen ninety-seven, many people feared that the businessmen would flee. Li stayed. He maintained good relations with both Beijing and the West, and he was one of the few tycoons who could talk to everyone. His critics said he was too close to the powerful. His supporters said he was a pragmatist who understood how to survive.

In the two thousands, he launched a new network, called Three, which brought mobile broadband to Europe and Australia. He kept investing in ports, retail, and infrastructure, in more than fifty countries. His rule was to build slowly, but never to be left without cash.

At the same time, he became one of the most generous givers in Asia. He established the Li Ka Shing Foundation, and promised to give away one third of his fortune. He funded hospitals, universities, and a medical school, and the Shantou University in his home province.

### 07_cap7
In two thousand and fifteen, at the age of eighty-six, he reorganized his empire into two main companies, one for his property and infrastructure, one for his ports, telecom, and retail. It was a way to make his legacy simpler, and to prepare his sons for the future.

In two thousand and eighteen, at the age of eighty-nine, he announced his retirement. His elder son, Victor Li, took over as chairman. At the farewell, Li received a long standing ovation from thousands of employees and shareholders.

He was still active in his foundation, and he remained one of the richest people in the world. But he never forgot where he started. He said that he still thought of the boy who carried a heavy bag through the streets of Hong Kong. He believed that his hardships had made him what he was.

In an interview, he said that when he was young, he thought about how to make money. But as he grew older, he found that what mattered most was the people who trusted him. A reputation, he said, is something you can build in a day, but only if you have spent forty years working for it.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Li Ka-shing?

The first lesson is to turn hardship into fuel. He lost his father at fifteen, and he worked in the streets. He did not complain. He used his suffering to make himself stronger, and he never wasted a day.

The second lesson is to keep learning. He studied English from a dictionary, read plastics magazines at night, and traveled to Italy to learn a new technique. He believed that knowledge was the only advantage nobody could take away.

The third lesson is to be brave when others are afraid. In nineteen sixty-seven, when everyone was running from Hong Kong, he bought. Fear is often the best time to invest.

And the last lesson is that trust is worth more than money. He built partnerships on fair terms, and in the long run, everyone wanted to do business with him.
