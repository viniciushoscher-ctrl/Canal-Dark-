### 00_hook
April nineteen twelve. A fifty-four-year-old candy maker named Milton Hershey has just made a decision that will save his life. He and his wife, Kitty, had booked passage home from Europe on the most famous ship in the world. Then a business matter called him back to Pennsylvania, and he cancelled. The ship was the Titanic.

Hershey did not know it yet, but he had already survived something far more dangerous than the Atlantic. By the age of twenty-nine he had gone broke, and then he had gone broke again. He had burned through his own money, his mother's money, and the money of relatives who never forgave him. In the town where he grew up, people shook their heads and said that Milton Hershey would never amount to anything.

Instead, he would build the largest chocolate factory on earth. He would build an entire town around it, with streets named after cocoa and chocolate, with a zoo, a park, a school, and a trolley line. He would become one of the richest men in America.

And then, at the height of his success, he would give nearly all of it away, to orphan boys he would never meet.

This is the story of Milton Hershey, the man who failed three times before he found the one idea that was sweeter than all the others.

### 01_cap1
Milton Snavely Hershey was born on the thirteenth of September, eighteen fifty-seven, on a farm in Derry Township, in central Pennsylvania. His family came from German-speaking farmers who had settled the region a century earlier, and at home the language of the kitchen table was Pennsylvania Dutch.

His mother, Fanny, was a devout Mennonite. She was disciplined, practical, and fiercely determined that her only surviving child would grow up to be useful. Her family, the Snavelys, were careful people who saved their money and paid their debts.

His father, Henry, was the opposite. Henry Hershey was a charming dreamer who could never stay in one place or one plan. He tried farming, then selling books, then inventing a patent cough remedy, then a new kind of horse feed, then speculating on oil and on silver. He moved the family again and again, always sure that the big payoff was one more scheme away. Every time, the scheme collapsed, and Fanny's relatives had to rescue them.

Young Milton learned two lessons from this household, though he did not know it yet. From his father, he learned how to dream big. From his mother, he learned how to work.

His schooling was short and messy. The family moved so often that he attended a string of different schools, and by the time he was about thirteen he had barely finished the fourth grade. His reading and spelling stayed weak for the rest of his life.

Henry decided that the boy needed a trade. He found him a job as an apprentice to a German-language newspaper printer in the town of Gap. Milton hated it. The story goes that one day his cap fell into the printing press, and he was so distracted by his own daydreams that he never noticed. The printer fired him, or perhaps he quit. Either way, the career in newspapers was over.

Fanny stepped in. She asked her family for help, and in eighteen seventy-two, fourteen-year-old Milton was sent to Lancaster, Pennsylvania, to learn the business of a confectioner named Joseph Royer, who ran an ice cream parlor and candy shop.

For four years he stirred copper kettles, pulled taffy, boiled sugar, and learned a hundred small secrets about how candy is made. Royer was a patient teacher, and Milton was an eager student. By the time his apprenticeship ended, he knew something that would change his life. He had found the one thing that made him forget to daydream.

He loved making candy.

### 02_cap2
In eighteen seventy-six, the United States celebrated its one hundredth birthday with a great fair in Philadelphia, called the Centennial Exposition. Millions of visitors came to see steam engines, telephones, and machines from around the world. The city was bursting with people, and every one of them, Milton Hershey figured, would want something sweet.

He was nineteen years old. He had no savings. But he had a mother who believed in him, and an aunt and uncle on the Snavely side who agreed to lend him a few hundred dollars. With that borrowed money, he rented a small shop in Philadelphia and opened his own candy business.

It was a brave move for a teenager. It was also a hard one. Hershey worked from before dawn until late at night. He made the candy himself, sold it himself, and delivered it himself, sometimes pushing a cart through the streets. His mother left the farm to help him. Even his father came to Philadelphia to lend a hand, though Henry's help was usually more talk than work.

For a while, it looked as though the gamble might pay off. His candies sold, and the shop stayed open. But Hershey had a problem he could not see. He made good candy, but he did not yet understand business. He bought ingredients at the wrong prices, he gave credit too freely, and he never earned enough to pay down what he owed. Sugar was expensive. Customers were slow to pay. The debts grew faster than the sales.

After six years, in eighteen eighty-two, the shop collapsed. The creditors came. His family's money was gone, and not only his own. His mother's relatives had lent him thousands of dollars, and they now had nothing to show for it. Some of them told Milton, to his face, that he was a bad risk and should never be trusted with money again.

The failure was more than financial. It broke something in the family. His aunt and uncle refused to speak to him for years. His father, who had also borrowed against the business, was crushed. At twenty-five, Milton Hershey was not only bankrupt. He was ashamed.

He did the only thing he could think of. He packed a bag, left the East, and went west.

### 03_cap3
The wilderness years of Milton Hershey are the part of the story that most people never hear. They are also the part that made the man.

In eighteen eighty-three he arrived in Denver, Colorado, a rough mining boom town full of men with money and nothing to spend it on. He found a job with a local confectioner, and there he learned the single most important thing of his early career. The candy maker made caramels, but he did not make them the way everyone else did. Instead of using paraffin wax and sugar, he used fresh milk. The result was a caramel that was richer, creamier, and better tasting than anything Hershey had ever made.

It was a small secret, and it would eventually make him rich. But not yet.

Hershey left Denver and tried his luck in Chicago, then in New Orleans, working in confectionery shops, hunting for a partner or a loan. Nothing stuck. Finally, in eighteen eighty-five, he went to New York City and opened his own candy shop on the Upper East Side, where he made fine candies and sold them from a handcart.

It was his second attempt. And just as in Philadelphia, it failed.

By eighteen eighty-six, Milton Hershey was twenty-nine years old. He had failed twice. He was out of money, and in no condition to ask his family for more. His uncle and aunt wanted nothing to do with him. A cousin had lent him money, and he could not pay it back. At one point, with no cash and no prospects, he took a train back to Pennsylvania almost like a beaten man, expecting that the only road left was a life of small jobs.

His father, who had never held on to a dollar, was waiting. His mother, who had never given up on her boy, was waiting too.

Here is what most people miss. Hershey was broke, but he was not empty-handed. He had spent ten years working in candy kitchens. He had learned from three different cities and two different ruined businesses. He had the milk caramel recipe from Denver. And he had something that cannot be lent or borrowed: an absolute refusal to quit.

Everything he had learned was about to come together.

### 04_cap4
He went back to Lancaster, the town where he had apprenticed as a boy. It was the place where somebody still knew his name. In eighteen eighty-six, at the age of twenty-nine, Milton Hershey started again, and he did it with one product: caramels made with fresh milk.

He called his new business the Lancaster Caramel Company. He had almost no money. He worked with a few helpers in a small, rented space, and he cooked batch after batch, tinkering with his recipe until the texture was perfect. He chewed caramels all day. He tested them against every competitor. He wanted the soft, creamy, long-lasting caramel that people would remember.

Money was the problem. Without it, he could not buy sugar, rent machines, or hire workers. The banks in Lancaster would not touch him. They knew his history. Two failed businesses were written on his reputation like a stain.

Then luck arrived, but luck that he had earned. An English candy importer named William Lebkicher tasted his caramels. He loved them. He placed an order for a large shipment to be sent to England, and he gave Hershey a note promising to pay for it.

That note changed everything. Hershey carried it to a Lancaster bank. A promise from a respected English importer was something the bank could trust. They gave him the loan he needed, and for the first time in his life he could buy enough ingredients to meet an order that big.

The shipment sailed. The money came back. More orders arrived. Within a few years the Lancaster Caramel Company was one of the most successful candy makers in America, with factories running, hundreds of workers on the payroll, and customers across the country and overseas.

Hershey, once the failure of the family, was now a millionaire. The relatives who had once refused to speak to him now wanted to shake his hand. He paid back every creditor. He did not gloat. He did not take revenge. He simply kept working, because he had learned that money could vanish as fast as it came.

He also had a restless mind. The caramel business was a triumph, but it was not enough. Hershey was beginning to wonder what came next.

The answer was waiting in Chicago.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen ninety-three, Chicago hosted the World's Columbian Exposition, a vast fair that drew twenty-seven million visitors. Milton Hershey went to see it. Among the endless exhibits of machines and marvels, one display stopped him cold.

It was a set of German chocolate-making machinery, rollers and grinders and mixers built to turn raw cocoa beans into smooth chocolate. At the time, chocolate was a luxury in America. It was expensive, it was bitter, and it was mostly sold to the rich. Hershey stood in front of those machines and saw a different future.

He bought the whole thing. He had it shipped to Lancaster, and he set up a small chocolate operation inside his caramel factory. At first, he used the chocolate only as a coating for his caramels. But he could not stop thinking about it.

The big idea came into focus. Chocolate, he believed, should not be a treat for the wealthy. It should be a treat for everyone. A man or a child should be able to walk into any store, put down a nickel, and walk out with a bar of good chocolate.

To do that, he had to solve a hard problem. Milk chocolate was the kind people actually wanted to eat, but it was nearly impossible to make. Swiss chemists had cracked it, but the Swiss kept their recipe secret. Hershey experimented for years, mixing and failing and mixing again. He used fresh milk from nearby farms, and he tested hundreds of variations until he found a formula that worked at a large scale and a small price.

In eighteen ninety-eight, he married Catherine Sweeney, whom everyone called Kitty. She was a warm, quick-witted young woman from Jamestown, New York, and she became his closest companion for the rest of her life.

Then, in nineteen hundred, Hershey did something that stunned his colleagues. He sold the Lancaster Caramel Company, the business that had made him rich, to a competitor for one million dollars. He kept only the chocolate side. When people asked why, he gave them a reply that has become famous. Caramels, he said, are a fad. Chocolate is a permanent thing.

He was forty-two years old, and he was betting everything on a candy that most Americans could barely afford.

Later that year, the first Hershey milk chocolate bar went on sale. It cost five cents. It was a hit.

### 06_cap6
Now Hershey had a problem that every successful man dreams of. He needed more of everything: more milk, more space, more workers. And he had a bold idea about where to put it.

He did not want to build in a crowded city. Cities, he thought, were dirty, noisy, and expensive. Workers lived in cramped tenements and paid high rents. They were unhappy and they quit. He remembered the farm country where he grew up, with its clean air and its dairy herds. Fresh milk was the heart of his chocolate, and dairy farms were all around Derry Township.

So in nineteen hundred and three, he broke ground on a huge factory in the cornfields of his birthplace, not far from the house where he had been born. People thought he had lost his mind. A chocolate factory in the middle of a farm? How would he find workers? Where would they live?

Hershey's answer was simple: he would build them a town.

He laid out streets and named them Chocolate Avenue and Cocoa Avenue. He built houses for workers that were better than anything a factory hand could normally afford, with gardens, with indoor plumbing, and with room to breathe. He paid for a trolley line, so that people could ride to work. He built a bank, a department store, and churches. He paid for a public school. He opened a park, which still exists today as Hersheypark, where workers could take their families for picnics and rides. He built a zoo, a swimming pool, a ballroom, and a community center.

The town opened officially in nineteen oh six, and he named it Hershey.

Back at the factory, the product line grew. In nineteen oh seven, he introduced a small, flat-bottomed piece of milk chocolate, wrapped in silver foil with a little paper flag. He called it the Kiss. Nobody agrees on exactly why. But it caught on, and today the company makes tens of millions of them every single day.

By around nineteen eleven, sales passed five million dollars a year. The nickel bar was still a nickel. Hershey had done what no one thought possible. He had made milk chocolate cheap enough for the average American, and he had built a model town to prove that business could treat people well.

Milton Hershey, once the town failure, was now a legend.

### 07_cap7
But in the middle of the glory, there was a hole in his life. Milton and Kitty Hershey wanted children, and they never had any.

Kitty's health, which had never been strong, began to decline. She developed a painful illness that doctors in the early nineteen hundreds could not treat. For years, Hershey took her to specialists in New York and Europe, searching for a cure. He spent a fortune on doctors, and none of it helped. Her legs weakened, and eventually she could barely walk.

Out of that sadness came the project that would define his life.

In nineteen oh nine, Milton and Kitty Hershey signed a deed of trust to open a school for orphan boys, on the farmland around their home. They called it the Hershey Industrial School. The idea was simple. Boys who had lost their fathers, and who had no one to pay for their schooling, would get a home, a bed, food, an education, and a trade, all free. They would live in houses with caring house parents, and they would grow up with a chance.

Hershey did not do this for show. He visited the boys. He walked the grounds. He sat with them at their tables and he talked to them about their lives. The boys who studied there remembered that he treated them like sons.

In nineteen fifteen, Kitty died. She was only in her early forties. Milton Hershey was shattered. For a time, people said, he could not bear to go on, and he threw himself into work to survive the silence. The marriage that had given him strength was over, and there would be no children to carry his name.

Three years later, in nineteen eighteen, he made the decision that would stun the country. Quietly, without any ceremony, Hershey transferred nearly his entire fortune to the school: the controlling shares of the Hershey Chocolate Company, worth about sixty million dollars at the time. He kept almost nothing for himself.

He explained it simply. He had no children. So, he said, the orphan boys would be his heirs.

It was one of the greatest acts of giving in American history, and it came from a man who had once been so broke that his own family wouldn't speak to him.

At the same time, his business kept growing beyond the borders of Pennsylvania. Sugar was the key ingredient in chocolate, and the price swung wildly. To protect himself, Hershey bought huge tracts of land in Cuba, built sugar mills, an electric railway, and another model village for the workers, which was also called Hershey. The same man who failed at selling candy from a handcart now ran sugar plantations across the sea.

### 08_cap8
In nineteen twenty-nine, the American stock market crashed. Banks failed. Factories closed. Millions of men were thrown out of work, and in the winter of nineteen thirty, long lines of the hungry stretched down city streets.

Milton Hershey, now in his seventies, made a choice. He would not lay off his workers. And, he decided, he would do something even bolder. He would use the crisis to build.

While the rest of the country shrank, Hershey put thousands of local men to work. They built a grand hotel on a hill overlooking the town. They built a community center, a sports arena, a theatre, a stadium, and a new office for the company. Many historians believe it was the largest building program in the region during the Depression, and it kept Hershey, Pennsylvania, alive while other towns slowly starved.

He liked to say that the best cure for hard times was giving people honest work. And so, for years, the chocolate town kept humming. Even during the worst of the Depression, the Hershey Company stayed profitable.

Not everything was peaceful. In April nineteen thirty-seven, workers staged a sit-down strike at the factory, demanding better pay and the right to a union. It turned ugly when farmers and loyal employees stormed the plant, beating strikers with clubs. The reputation of the kind chocolate king took a hit that never fully healed. Hershey, by then nearly eighty, was heartbroken that the people he thought of as his family had turned against each other.

Then came the war. In the late nineteen thirties, the United States Army came to Hershey with a strange request. They wanted a chocolate bar that could survive the tropics. It had to weigh four ounces, hold six hundred calories, and resist melting in extreme heat. One officer said it should taste only a little better than a boiled potato, so soldiers would not eat it unless they truly needed it.

Hershey's chemists built it. Called the Ration D bar, it went around the world in the pockets of American soldiers. During the war, the company produced billions of the bars, and Hershey's factories earned an Army-Navy award for their work.

Milton Hershey lived just long enough to see the war end. He died on the thirteenth of October, nineteen forty-five, at the age of eighty-eight, in the hospital he had helped build in his own town. He was buried next to Kitty.

His company is still among the largest chocolate makers on earth. His town still stands. And the school he and Kitty founded, now called the Milton Hershey School, is today one of the best-funded boarding schools in the world, still educating thousands of children for free, owned by the same trust that holds the Hershey Company.

### 09_lesson
So what can we learn from the life of Milton Hershey?

The first lesson is that failure is not a verdict. It is a classroom. Hershey went bankrupt in Philadelphia, and he went bankrupt in New York. If he had listened to the voices around him, he would have quit before he turned thirty. Instead, he took what he learned, the milk caramel from Denver, the discipline from Lancaster, the patience of ten years in candy kitchens, and he used it to start again.

The second lesson is that the best products are made for ordinary people. Hershey could have sold expensive chocolates to the rich. Instead, he made a nickel bar, and he sold more of them than anyone thought possible. He did not chase luxury. He chased the many.

The third lesson is that the people who work for you are your greatest asset. Hershey built homes, parks, and schools for his workers. He believed that a happy town would build better chocolate. And for decades, he was right.

The final lesson is about what a fortune is for. Milton Hershey died with almost no money in his name. He had given it all to orphan boys who began as strangers and became, by the thousands, his legacy. He proved that a man can build an empire and still be remembered not for what he kept, but for what he gave.

He failed at twenty-five. He failed at twenty-nine. And then he changed the way the world eats chocolate.
