### 00_hook
April nineteen twelve. A fifty-four-year-old candy maker named Milton Hershey has just made a decision that will save his life. He and his wife, Kitty, had booked passage home from Europe on the most famous ship in the world. Then a business matter called him back to Pennsylvania, and he cancelled. The ship was the Titanic.

Hershey did not know it yet, but he had already survived something far more dangerous than the Atlantic. By the age of twenty-nine he had gone broke, and then he had gone broke again. He had burned through his own money, his mother's money, and the money of relatives who never forgave him. In the town where he grew up, people shook their heads and said that Milton Hershey would never amount to anything.

Instead, he would build the largest chocolate factory on earth. He would build an entire town around it, with streets named after cocoa and chocolate, with a zoo, a park, a school, and a trolley line. He would become one of the richest men in America.

And then, at the height of his success, he would give nearly all of it away, to orphan boys he would never meet.

This is the story of Milton Hershey, the man who failed three times before he found the one idea that was sweeter than all the others.

### 01_cap1
Milton Snavely Hershey was born on the thirteenth of September, eighteen fifty-seven, on a farm in Derry Township, in central Pennsylvania. His family came from German-speaking farmers, and at home the language of the kitchen table was Pennsylvania Dutch.

His mother, Fanny, was a devout Mennonite, disciplined and practical, and fiercely determined that her only surviving child would grow up to be useful. His father, Henry, was the opposite. Henry Hershey was a charming dreamer who could never stay in one place or one plan. He tried farming, then selling books, then a patent cough remedy, then a new kind of horse feed, then speculating on oil and silver. Every time the scheme collapsed, and Fanny's relatives had to rescue them.

Young Milton learned two lessons from this household. From his father, he learned how to dream big. From his mother, he learned how to work.

The family moved so often that by the time he was about thirteen he had barely finished the fourth grade. His father found him a job as an apprentice to a German-language newspaper printer. Milton hated it. The story goes that his cap fell into the printing press, and he was so lost in daydreams that he never noticed.

So in eighteen seventy-two, fourteen-year-old Milton was sent to Lancaster, Pennsylvania, to learn the business of a confectioner named Joseph Royer. For four years he stirred copper kettles, pulled taffy, and boiled sugar. By the end, he had found the one thing that made him forget to daydream. He loved making candy.

### 02_cap2
In eighteen seventy-six, the United States celebrated its one hundredth birthday with the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia. Millions of visitors came, and every one of them, Milton Hershey figured, would want something sweet.

He was nineteen years old, with no savings. But an aunt and uncle agreed to lend him a few hundred dollars, and with that borrowed money he opened his own candy shop in Philadelphia. He worked from before dawn until late at night, making the candy himself, selling it himself, and sometimes pushing a cart through the streets. His mother left the farm to help him.

But Hershey had a problem he could not see. He made good candy, but he did not yet understand business. He bought ingredients at the wrong prices, gave credit too freely, and never earned enough to pay down what he owed. The debts grew faster than the sales.

After six years, in eighteen eighty-two, the shop collapsed. His mother's relatives had lent him thousands of dollars, and they now had nothing to show for it. His aunt and uncle refused to speak to him for years. At twenty-five, Milton Hershey was not only bankrupt. He was ashamed.

He packed a bag, left the East, and went west.

### 03_cap3
The wilderness years of Milton Hershey are the part of the story that most people never hear. They are also the part that made the man.

In eighteen eighty-three he arrived in Denver, Colorado, a rough mining boom town. He found a job with a local confectioner, and there he learned the most important thing of his early career. The candy maker did not make caramels with paraffin wax and sugar, like everyone else. He used fresh milk. The result was richer, creamier, and better than anything Hershey had ever made. It was a small secret, and it would eventually make him rich. But not yet.

Hershey tried Chicago, then New Orleans, hunting for a partner or a loan. Nothing stuck. Finally, in eighteen eighty-five, he opened his own candy shop in New York City, and sold fine candies from a handcart. It was his second attempt. And just as in Philadelphia, it failed.

By eighteen eighty-six, Milton Hershey was twenty-nine years old. He had failed twice. He was out of money, and he took a train back to Pennsylvania almost like a beaten man.

But he was not empty-handed. He had spent ten years in candy kitchens. He had the milk caramel recipe from Denver. And he had something that cannot be lent or borrowed: an absolute refusal to quit.

### 04_cap4
He went back to Lancaster, the town where he had apprenticed as a boy. In eighteen eighty-six, at the age of twenty-nine, Milton Hershey started again, with one product: caramels made with fresh milk. He called his new business the Lancaster Caramel Company.

Money was the problem. The banks in Lancaster would not touch him. They knew his history, and two failed businesses were written on his reputation like a stain.

Then came luck, but luck that he had earned. An English candy importer tasted his caramels, loved them, and placed an order for a large shipment to England, with a note promising to pay for it. Hershey carried that note to a Lancaster bank. A promise from a respected importer was something the bank could trust, and they gave him the loan he needed.

The shipment sailed. The money came back. More orders arrived. Within a few years the Lancaster Caramel Company was one of the most successful candy makers in America, with hundreds of workers and customers overseas.

Hershey, once the failure of the family, was now a millionaire. He paid back every creditor. He did not gloat, and he did not take revenge. He kept working, because he had learned that money could vanish as fast as it came.

But the caramel business was not enough. The answer to what came next was waiting in Chicago.

### 05_cap5
In eighteen ninety-three, Chicago hosted the World's Columbian Exposition, a vast fair that drew twenty-seven million visitors. Among the endless exhibits, one display stopped Milton Hershey cold. It was a set of German chocolate-making machinery, built to turn raw cocoa beans into smooth chocolate.

At the time, chocolate was a luxury in America, expensive, bitter, and mostly sold to the rich. Hershey stood in front of those machines and saw a different future. He bought the whole thing and had it shipped to Lancaster.

The big idea came into focus. Chocolate should not be a treat for the wealthy. It should be a treat for everyone. A man or a child should be able to walk into any store, put down a nickel, and walk out with a bar of good chocolate.

To do that, he had to solve a hard problem. Milk chocolate was what people wanted, but it was nearly impossible to make, and the Swiss kept their recipe secret. Hershey experimented for years, with fresh milk from nearby farms, until he found a formula that worked at a large scale and a small price.

In eighteen ninety-eight, he married Catherine Sweeney, whom everyone called Kitty. Then, in nineteen hundred, he did something that stunned his colleagues. He sold the Lancaster Caramel Company, the business that had made him rich, for one million dollars, and kept only the chocolate. Caramels, he said, are a fad. Chocolate is a permanent thing.

Later that year, the first Hershey milk chocolate bar went on sale. It cost five cents. It was a hit.

### 06_cap6
Now Hershey needed more of everything: more milk, more space, more workers. And he had a bold idea about where to put it. He did not want to build in a crowded city, where workers lived in cramped tenements and quit. So in nineteen hundred and three, he broke ground on a huge factory in the cornfields of his birthplace. People thought he had lost his mind.

Hershey's answer was simple: he would build the workers a town.

He named the streets Chocolate Avenue and Cocoa Avenue. He built houses with gardens and indoor plumbing, better than any factory hand could normally afford. He paid for a trolley line, a bank, a department store, and a public school. He opened a park, which still exists today as Hersheypark, and built a zoo, a swimming pool, and a community center. The town opened officially in nineteen oh six, and he named it Hershey.

In nineteen oh seven, he introduced a small, flat-bottomed piece of milk chocolate, wrapped in silver foil with a little paper flag. He called it the Kiss. Today the company makes tens of millions of them every single day.

By around nineteen eleven, sales passed five million dollars a year, and the nickel bar was still a nickel. He had made milk chocolate cheap enough for the average American, and he had built a model town to prove that business could treat people well.

### 07_cap7
But in the middle of the glory, there was a hole in his life. Milton and Kitty Hershey wanted children, and they never had any.

Kitty's health began to decline. She developed a painful illness that doctors in those years could not treat. For years, Hershey took her to specialists in New York and Europe, and spent a fortune on doctors, and none of it helped.

Out of that sadness came the project that would define his life. In nineteen oh nine, Milton and Kitty signed a deed of trust to open a school for orphan boys on the farmland around their home, the Hershey Industrial School. Boys who had lost their fathers would get a home, food, an education, and a trade, all free. Hershey visited the boys, sat with them at their tables, and treated them like sons.

In nineteen fifteen, Kitty died, in her early forties. Milton Hershey was shattered, and he threw himself into work to survive the silence.

Three years later, in nineteen eighteen, he made the decision that would stun the country. Quietly, without any ceremony, Hershey transferred nearly his entire fortune to the school: the controlling shares of the Hershey Chocolate Company, worth about sixty million dollars. He had no children, he said, so the orphan boys would be his heirs.

It was one of the greatest acts of giving in American history, and it came from a man who had once been so broke that his own family would not speak to him.

### 08_cap8
In nineteen twenty-nine, the American stock market crashed. Banks failed, factories closed, and millions of men were thrown out of work.

Milton Hershey, now in his seventies, made a choice. He would not lay off his workers, and he would use the crisis to build. While the rest of the country shrank, Hershey put thousands of local men to work. They built a grand hotel on a hill, a community center, a sports arena, a theatre, and a stadium. It kept Hershey, Pennsylvania, alive while other towns slowly starved.

Not everything was peaceful. In April nineteen thirty-seven, workers staged a sit-down strike at the factory. It turned ugly when farmers and loyal employees stormed the plant and beat the strikers. The reputation of the kind chocolate king never fully healed.

Then came the war. The United States Army asked for a chocolate bar that could survive the tropics. It had to weigh four ounces, hold six hundred calories, and resist melting. One officer said it should taste only a little better than a boiled potato, so soldiers would not eat it unless they truly needed it. Hershey's chemists built it. Called the Ration D bar, it went around the world in the pockets of American soldiers, and during the war the company produced billions of them.

Milton Hershey died on the thirteenth of October, nineteen forty-five, at the age of eighty-eight, in the hospital he had helped build in his own town. He was buried next to Kitty.

His company is still among the largest chocolate makers on earth. And the school he and Kitty founded, now the Milton Hershey School, is one of the best-funded boarding schools in the world, still educating thousands of children for free.

### 09_lesson
So what can we learn from the life of Milton Hershey?

The first lesson is that failure is not a verdict. It is a classroom. Hershey went bankrupt in Philadelphia, and he went bankrupt in New York. If he had listened to the voices around him, he would have quit before he turned thirty. Instead, he took what he learned, the milk caramel from Denver, the discipline from Lancaster, the patience of ten years in candy kitchens, and he used it to start again.

The second lesson is that the best products are made for ordinary people. Hershey could have sold expensive chocolates to the rich. Instead, he made a nickel bar, and he sold more of them than anyone thought possible. He did not chase luxury. He chased the many.

The third lesson is that the people who work for you are your greatest asset. Hershey built homes, parks, and schools for his workers. He believed that a happy town would build better chocolate. And for decades, he was right.

The final lesson is about what a fortune is for. Milton Hershey died with almost no money in his name. He had given it all to orphan boys who began as strangers and became, by the thousands, his legacy. He proved that a man can build an empire and still be remembered not for what he kept, but for what he gave.

He failed at twenty-five. He failed at twenty-nine. And then he changed the way the world eats chocolate.
