### 00_hook
July seventeenth, nineteen forty-six. A burning airplane tears through a Beverly Hills neighborhood, clips three houses, and slams into the ground in a ball of fire. Neighbors run toward the wreck, expecting to pull out a body.

Instead, they find a thin, tall man crawling out of the flames, burned across his chest, with his clothes on fire. He is one of the richest men in America, and he has just crashed his own experimental plane.

His name is Howard Hughes. And the crash was only the beginning of a story that would end in a sealed hotel room, with a man who owned half of Las Vegas and could not touch a doorknob.

### 01_cap1
Howard Hughes was born on Christmas Eve, nineteen oh-five, in Houston, Texas. His father, Howard Senior, had invented a drill bit that could cut through hard rock, and it changed the oil business. The Hughes Tool Company rented the bits instead of selling them, and the money poured in.

Howard was a quiet boy who loved machines. By twelve, he had built a motorized bicycle from spare parts. When he was sixteen, his mother died. Two years later, his father died too.

The drill bit was a small miracle of engineering. Before it, drilling through hard rock could take weeks and destroy many cheap bits. The new bit, with its cones of steel teeth, bit through rock far faster. Oil companies around the world wanted it, and they were willing to pay rent on it forever.

At eighteen, he was an orphan with a giant company. A court allowed him to take control at nineteen. Relatives expected him to sell it and enjoy his money. Hughes did something else. He kept the tool company, which would fund every dream he ever had, and he moved to Hollywood.

### 02_cap2
Hughes wanted to make movies, and he wanted them bigger than anyone else. His first film, Hell's Angels, was a war epic about pilots in the First World War. He bought dozens of real planes, hired hundreds of pilots, and spent years filming. The budget exploded to nearly four million dollars, an astonishing sum in nineteen thirty.

During filming, a pilot died in a crash, and Hughes himself nearly died when he flew a plane for a scene and crashed. He did not stop. When sound movies arrived, he scrapped most of the film and shot it again.

The movie was a hit. It also launched Jean Harlow, one of Hollywood's biggest stars. Hughes then produced Scarface, a violent gangster film that was banned in several states. Hughes also dated some of the most famous actresses in the world, including Katharine Hepburn and Ava Gardner. Newspapers followed him everywhere, and he learned how to use the press as a weapon, and also how to hide from it.

He became a Hollywood legend before he was thirty, and he still was not satisfied.

What he really loved was flying.

### 03_cap3
In nineteen thirty-two, Hughes started an aircraft company. He wanted to build the fastest plane in the world. He sat in the design office, drawing and redrawing, and obsessing over every rivet. He insisted that the rivets be flush with the skin so the airplane would cut through the air with almost no drag.

In nineteen thirty-five, he flew the Hughes H-1 racer, and he set a new world speed record of three hundred and fifty-two miles per hour. Two years later, he flew coast to coast in just over seven hours.

Then, in nineteen thirty-eight, he did something that made him a national hero. He flew around the world in three days, nineteen hours, and seventeen minutes, beating the old record by more than half. New York gave him a ticker tape parade.

The pilot who loved speed was also an engineer. He tested every design himself. Colleagues said he knew every wire in his planes, and that he could spot a flaw in a blueprint from across the room.

He was thirty-two years old, handsome, rich, and famous. Nothing seemed impossible.

### 04_cap4
During the Second World War, the United States government hired Hughes to build two enormous flying machines. One was a photo-reconnaissance plane. The other was a gigantic wooden flying boat designed to carry soldiers across the Atlantic and avoid German submarines. The press called it the Spruce Goose.

The project was a disaster. It was late, expensive, and almost impossible to build. By nineteen forty-seven, the Senate was investigating Hughes for wasting millions in war contracts. In front of cameras, Hughes fought back, shouting at senators, and the hearings became a spectacle.

Then, on November second, nineteen forty-seven, he did the impossible. He climbed into the giant boat, with reporters on board, and flew it for about a mile, a few feet above the water of Long Beach harbor. The Spruce Goose, which everyone had mocked, had flown.

He said that if it failed, he would leave the country. It did not fail, and yet he never flew it again.

In nineteen forty-six, Hughes was testing the XF-eleven, a fast reconnaissance plane for the Army. Minutes into the flight, a propeller malfunctioned, and the plane lost control. He tried to reach a golf course, failed, and crashed into the houses.

Doctors said he would not live. He had crushed ribs, a collapsed lung, and severe burns. Hughes survived, but he spent months in bed, and he asked for a special hospital bed with controls he could operate himself. Engineers built it, and it became the model for modern hospital beds.

### 05_cap5
After the crash in nineteen forty-six, Hughes was in terrible pain, and he began taking strong painkillers. His mind, which had always been intense, started to change. He became obsessed with germs. He demanded that his aides wear white gloves, open doors with tissues, and follow written instructions of hundreds of pages to open a can of fruit.

At the same time, he was building another empire. In nineteen forty-eight, he bought control of the movie studio RKO. In nineteen sixty-six, he sold his shares in the airline TWA for about five hundred and fifty million dollars, one of the largest personal payouts in history.

Then he arrived in Las Vegas, took the top floor of a hotel, and refused to leave. When the owners tried to throw him out, he simply bought the hotel. Then he kept buying. Casinos, land, even a local television station.

His company, Hughes Aircraft, became a pillar of the American military, building missiles, radar, and satellites. In nineteen seventy-four, the Central Intelligence Agency used his name as cover to build a giant ship called the Glomar Explorer, which secretly tried to raise a sunken Soviet submarine from the bottom of the Pacific.

At his peak, Hughes was one of the richest men in the world, and he lived like a prisoner.

### 06_cap6
In his last years, Hughes lived in blacked-out hotel rooms, in Las Vegas, Nassau, Vancouver, and Acapulco. He rarely bathed or cut his hair. He wrote long memos about his phobias, and he watched the same movies over and over.

Late in life, aides described a man who spent days naked in a darkened room, living on chocolate bars and milk, and watching movies on a projector. His fingernails grew long. He was still signing deals, buying hotels, and running a business empire by telephone, through a small circle of loyal men.

He died on April fifth, nineteen seventy-six, on a plane taking him to a hospital in Houston. He weighed about ninety pounds. The doctors had to use fingerprints to identify him.

He had no wife, no children, and no clear will. A handwritten document called the Mormon will appeared, leaving a fortune to unlikely heirs, but a court declared it a forgery. After years of fights, his estate was divided among distant cousins.

Years later, a Hollywood director made a film about him, and a new generation learned his name. But the real story is stranger than the movies, because Howard Hughes was not just a rich eccentric. He was also one of the great builders of the twentieth century.

The fortune, worth well over a billion dollars, had been built on a drill bit. But the man who inherited it had spent his life trying to outrun something he could never escape.

### 07_lesson
Howard Hughes lived a spectacular life. He broke records, produced hit movies, and built machines that dazzled the world. And yet, he died alone, afraid of an invisible enemy.

His story has two lessons. First, genius without limits can become a trap. Hughes could buy anything, so no one could ever tell him no.

Second, a fortune is not freedom. At the end, the richest man in the world was a man who could not walk through a door.
