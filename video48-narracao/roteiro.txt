### 00_hook
The sixteenth of September, nineteen ninety-two. In London, the British government is trying to hold the value of the pound. The Bank of England has raised interest rates twice in a single day, to fifteen percent, and has spent billions of pounds in a desperate attempt to keep the currency from falling. By evening, it gives up. The pound crashes out of the European exchange rate system. The day will be known as Black Wednesday.

Somewhere in New York, a sixty-two-year-old Hungarian-born investor smiles. He has been betting against the pound for weeks, with a position of more than ten billion dollars, the largest of its kind in history. In a single day, he makes about one billion dollars.

The newspapers call him the man who broke the Bank of England. His name is George Soros.

But the story of how he got there starts long before, in a city under Nazi occupation, where a fourteen-year-old boy learned to survive with forged papers and a false name. This is the story of George Soros, the survivor who became the most famous and most controversial speculator in the world.

### 01_cap1
George Soros was born Gyorgy Schwartz on the twelfth of August, nineteen thirty, in Budapest, Hungary, into a prosperous Jewish family. His father, Tivadar, was a lawyer, a free spirit, and a passionate speaker of Esperanto, the universal language. In the family, they changed their name to Soros in nineteen thirty-six, to avoid growing anti-Semitism.

In March nineteen forty-four, Nazi Germany occupied Hungary. Within weeks, hundreds of thousands of Hungarian Jews were being deported to death camps. Tivadar Soros, who had survived as a prisoner in Russia in the First World War, knew exactly what to do. He bought false identity papers for his family, and scattered them across Budapest, each with a new name.

George, then thirteen, was sent to live with a Hungarian official in the Ministry of Agriculture, posing as his godson. He later recalled that he saw the danger, but did not feel fear. He saw an adventure. Once, he accompanied the official to inspect a Jewish family's property for confiscation.

The Soros family survived, nearly all of them, when thousands around them did not. George took from those months a lesson that he would carry for his whole life. In a time of chaos, those who adapt and think clearly can survive, while those who cling to old certainties perish.

### 02_cap2
After the war, Hungary fell under Soviet control. In nineteen forty-seven, at the age of seventeen, George left for London. He arrived alone, with almost no money, and for years he lived in poverty. He worked as a railway porter, a waiter, and even a house painter. He studied at the London School of Economics, where he met a professor who changed his life, the philosopher Karl Popper.

Popper taught that no one possesses the final truth, and that open societies, which accept their own errors, are better than closed ones. Soros absorbed the idea, and it would later shape both his investing and his philanthropy.

In nineteen fifty-six, he moved to New York, and found work on Wall Street as an arbitrage trader. He worked at several firms, and learned how to read markets. In nineteen sixty-nine, he started a fund, and in nineteen seventy he partnered with a young trader named Jim Rogers, to launch a new fund. It was later renamed the Quantum Fund.

The fund was a spectacular success. From nineteen seventy to nineteen eighty, it rose by an average of about thirty percent a year. By the end of the nineteen eighties, Soros was among the most closely watched investors in the world.

### 03_cap3
Soros had a unique theory of markets, which he called reflexivity. Traditional economists believed that markets reflect reality. Soros believed that markets also change reality. If investors believe a currency will fall, they sell it, and that selling makes the currency fall. Beliefs and facts feed each other, in a loop, until the loop breaks.

He used the idea as a guide. He looked for moments when the market's prevailing belief was wrong, and when the loop was about to snap. Then he made a big bet.

His style was unusual. He was not a calm, cool financier. His son said that he made decisions with his body. When he had a back spasm, he knew something was wrong with his position, and he sold. He called it his first line of defense against being wrong.

He also had a rule. When he saw a great opportunity, he did not bet a little. He bet big. A friend and colleague, Stanley Druckenmiller, who ran the fund for him for years, said that Soros taught him the most important lesson of his career. It is not whether you are right or wrong that counts, but how much money you make when you are right.

### 04_cap4
By nineteen ninety-two, Britain had a problem. The country had joined the European Exchange Rate Mechanism, which tied the pound to the German mark. But Germany had high interest rates, after its reunification, and Britain was in recession. It could not afford to keep rates high, and the pound was overvalued.

Soros and Druckenmiller saw the trap. They calculated that the British government could not defend the pound forever. In the weeks before September, they built a massive short position, selling pounds they did not own, with a total of about ten billion dollars. On Black Wednesday, the Bank of England tried to defend the pound, and failed.

In a single day, Britain lost billions from its reserves, the currency was devalued, and it left the Exchange Rate Mechanism for good. For the country, it was a humiliation. Yet in the longer term, many economists argued that the devaluation helped the British economy to recover.

For Soros, it was a triumph. Quantum made about one billion dollars. He earned the nickname the man who broke the Bank of England. His own personal gain that year was about six hundred and fifty million dollars. He had become a legend.

### 05_cap5
Soros did not stop there. In the nineteen nineties, he made bets against currencies across the world. In nineteen ninety-seven, during the Asian financial crisis, Malaysia's prime minister, Mahathir Mohamad, accused him of causing the collapse of his country's currency, and called him a moron. Soros denied that he was responsible, saying that the crisis was caused by the weaknesses of the countries themselves.

He also lost money. In nineteen ninety-eight, he lost about two billion dollars on bets in Russia, during its own financial collapse. And in two thousand, during the dot-com bubble, his fund took heavy losses, and he stepped back from daily management.

But he had already begun to turn his attention to a different goal. In nineteen seventy-nine, he had started to give money to support black students in apartheid South Africa, and to Eastern European dissidents. In nineteen eighty-four, he founded the first Open Society foundation in Hungary, to support free thinking, and to challenge the communist regime. He sent photocopiers to universities, so that students could make copies of banned materials.

When the Berlin Wall fell, his network of foundations spread across Eastern Europe. He funded scholarships, independent media, and civil society groups.

### 06_cap6
In the next decades, Soros became one of the biggest donors in the world. He founded the Central European University in Budapest. He created the Open Society Foundations, which gave money to causes in more than one hundred countries. By two thousand and twenty, he had given more than thirty-two billion dollars, and in two thousand and seventeen, he transferred about eighteen billion dollars to the foundation, making it one of the largest philanthropies in the world.

But he also became a lightning rod. He spent millions to support the Democratic Party and opponents of George W. Bush in the two thousand and four American election. His critics, on the political right, painted him as a puppet master, and some of the attacks were laced with ugly anti-Semitic conspiracy theories.

In Hungary, the country of his birth, the government of Viktor Orban launched a campaign against him in two thousand and seventeen, with billboards. In two thousand and eighteen, the Central European University was forced to move most of its programs to Vienna.

Soros answered the attacks calmly. He said that he did not seek to be loved, but wanted to help societies stay open. Supporters called him a champion of freedom. Opponents called him a threat. It was hard to find a person who had no opinion.

### 07_cap7
He married three times, and has five children. In two thousand and twenty-three, at the age of ninety-two, he passed control of his Open Society Foundations and a large part of his empire to his son Alexander, who has taken a more political role.

He has a fortune of about seven billion dollars today, much smaller than it would be if he had not given away so much. In total, he has donated over thirty-two billion dollars. Few financiers in history have given away so much.

Some people see him as a hero who used his wealth to fight authoritarian regimes. Others see him as a speculator who destroyed currencies and enriched himself while others suffered. Probably, as he himself might say, both views contain some of the truth, and neither contains all of it.

Whatever the verdict, no one disputes that he changed finance. He proved that one man, with a sharp idea and the courage to bet big, could move the market of an entire nation.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from George Soros?

The first lesson is to survive first. Soros learned as a boy, under the Nazis, that staying alive is the first priority. In markets, too, the first rule is to avoid ruin, so that you can play again tomorrow.

The second lesson is to know that you can be wrong. Soros believed that all human understanding is imperfect. He changed his mind quickly when facts changed, and he cut losses without ego.

The third lesson is to bet big when you are right. It is not enough to have a good idea. You must be ready to size your bet when the opportunity is clear.

And the final lesson is that wealth is a tool. Soros built a fortune, and then spent it on the causes he believed in. Whether you agree with him or not, he used his money to try to shape the world.
