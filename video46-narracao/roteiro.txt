### 00_hook
Nineteen fifty-nine. A young chemical engineer, newly graduated with a doctorate, is working in a small plastics lab at General Electric in Pittsfield, Massachusetts. He is twenty-three years old, loud, impatient, and proud. One day, he runs an experiment, and the pilot plant explodes. The roof of the building is blown off.

He is called into his boss's office. He expects to be fired. Instead, his boss tells him to come back and try again, and the young man never forgets that moment. Many years later, he would say that it taught him the most important lesson of his career. Great leaders do not punish failure. They punish timidity.

That young man is Jack Welch. He would rise through the ranks of General Electric, and at the age of forty-five, he would become the youngest chairman in the history of the company. Over the next twenty years, he would increase its value by more than thirty times, and become known as the greatest manager of the century.

He would also earn a very different nickname, Neutron Jack. This is the story of a man who saved a giant by cutting it to pieces.

### 01_cap1
John Francis Welch Junior was born on the nineteenth of November, nineteen thirty-five, in Peabody, Massachusetts, a working-class town north of Boston. He was the only child of Irish Catholic parents, John and Grace Welch. His father was a railroad conductor, a gentle, quiet man. His mother was the force of the family.

Grace Welch was the person who shaped Jack. She was tough, funny, and relentless. She told him that he could do anything he put his mind to, and she demanded that he be honest, work hard, and never blame others. He later said that she taught him almost everything he knew about leadership.

Young Jack had a severe stutter. He talked so fast that his words tripped over each other. His mother told him the reason was simple. His brain was working faster than his tongue could keep up. He turned the weakness into a strength, and he never stopped talking.

He was a good student, and a passionate athlete. He played hockey and golf, and he was the captain of his high school hockey team. He had a short temper, and when he lost, he threw his stick. His mother, instead of punishing him, once walked into the locker room and told him that if he did not know how to lose, he would never know how to win.

### 02_cap2
Welch studied chemical engineering at the University of Massachusetts, the first in his family to go to college. Then he earned a master's degree and a doctorate at the University of Illinois. In nineteen sixty, he joined General Electric as an engineer in its plastics division in Pittsfield.

It was a modest start. He was paid ten thousand five hundred dollars a year. After a year, when he learned that everyone in his group received the same raise, he was so angry that he decided to resign. The company was too slow, too bureaucratic, and too comfortable. He had a job offer from another firm, and he was packing his things.

But a senior manager named Reuben Gutoff, who was impressed by the young man's energy, drove to his house, and persuaded him to stay. Gutoff promised that things would change.

They did. Welch became a rising star in the plastics business. He led the development of a new plastic called Noryl, which became a big success. He worked furiously, and he kept getting promoted. In nineteen sixty-eight, at the age of thirty-two, he became the youngest general manager in the history of the company. By nineteen seventy-two, he was a vice president. By nineteen seventy-nine, he was a vice chairman. He was clearly in the race to become the next chief executive.

### 03_cap3
In the nineteen seventies, General Electric was one of the biggest companies in the world. It made everything, from light bulbs and refrigerators, to jet engines and nuclear power plants, and it employed more than four hundred thousand people. But it was slow and weighed down by layers of management. Profits were flat. Japanese competitors were attacking.

In nineteen eighty, the chairman, Reg Jones, began a famous search for his successor. He ran a long contest among a handful of candidates, and the story was covered in the press like a presidential election. On the first of April, nineteen eighty-one, at the age of forty-five, Jack Welch was named the eighth chairman of General Electric.

The old guard was shocked. Welch was young, brash, and unlike any of his predecessors. Many people predicted he would fail.

On his first day, he set out a simple rule. He said that GE would be number one or number two in every business it was in, or it would fix, sell, or close the business. Every division had to be first or second in its market. There would be no exceptions.

It was a tough rule. And he meant it.

### 04_cap4
In the first years of the eighties, Welch did something that shocked America. He cut more than a hundred thousand jobs. He sold or closed about two hundred businesses, including entire divisions that had been part of GE for generations, such as small appliances and the mining business.

The press gave him a new name, Neutron Jack, after the neutron bomb, which kills people but leaves the buildings standing. It was not meant as a compliment. Workers were furious. Unions were outraged. Town councils feared for their communities.

Welch did not apologize. He said that he was not cruel. He said that the only real job security came from a company that was winning, and that pretending otherwise was dishonest. He argued that it was better to be tough and fair now, rather than be forced to close entire plants later.

At the same time, he made a big bet on the future. He sold the old, but he bought the new. In nineteen eighty-six, he bought RCA, which owned the television network NBC, for six and a half billion dollars, one of the largest non-oil takeovers up to that time. He also built GE Capital, the financial arm of the company, into a monster.

### 05_cap5
Welch knew that cutting was not enough. He needed to change the culture. In nineteen eighty-nine, he launched a program called Work-Out, in which employees were invited to town hall meetings to challenge their managers and propose ways to remove pointless bureaucracy. For the first time, a factory worker could tell a vice president that a rule made no sense.

He also became famous for the way he evaluated people. Each year, every manager was ranked. The top twenty percent were rewarded and promoted, the middle seventy percent were developed, and the bottom ten percent were removed. It was called the vitality curve, and the press called it rank and yank. It was brutal, and it was copied by hundreds of companies.

In the nineteen nineties, Welch pushed another idea, which he called boundarylessness. He wanted ideas to move across the company without being blocked by departments or titles. And later, he launched Six Sigma, a quality program borrowed from Motorola, which aimed to reduce errors to almost zero.

The results were extraordinary. When Welch took charge in nineteen eighty-one, GE was worth about fourteen billion dollars. By the time he retired in two thousand and one, it was worth about four hundred billion dollars. At one point, in the year two thousand, it was the most valuable company in the world.

### 06_cap6
Welch was a celebrity. He appeared on magazine covers. He gave speeches to thousands. Executives across the world read his books and studied his methods. In two thousand, Fortune magazine named him Manager of the Century.

But there were cracks. Critics said that GE's profits depended too much on GE Capital, which was a financial company in disguise. They said that the company seemed to deliver earnings that were suspiciously smooth, quarter after quarter, and that this relied on accounting tricks. Welch denied it.

There was also the matter of his personal life. In nineteen eighty-eight, he divorced his first wife, Carolyn, after twenty-eight years, and in nineteen eighty-nine he married Jane Beasley. In two thousand and two, a bitter divorce with his second wife revealed that his retirement package included a luxurious apartment in New York, private jets, and tickets to sporting events, all paid by GE. The company's shareholders were outraged, and Welch agreed to pay for these himself.

In September two thousand and one, he retired, and handed the company to Jeff Immelt. Four days later, the world changed on September eleventh, and GE's fortunes began to fall.

### 07_cap7
After his retirement, Welch wrote two books. The first, Jack: Straight from the Gut, was a bestselling memoir. The second, Winning, became a manual for managers worldwide. He also taught, and he started the Jack Welch Management Institute, which offered an online master's degree in business.

But his reputation suffered as time passed. GE's stock collapsed after two thousand and eight, when the financial crisis exposed the risks at GE Capital. By two thousand and eighteen, GE was removed from the Dow Jones Industrial Average, which it had joined in nineteen oh seven. Many critics blamed the culture of short-term earnings that Welch had built.

Welch himself defended his record until the end. He argued that GE's later mistakes were the fault of those who came after him.

He died on the first of March, two thousand and twenty, at the age of eighty-four, in New York City. Obituaries called him one of the most influential, and most controversial, business leaders in American history. Nobody doubted that he had changed the way companies are run.

### 08_lesson
So what can we learn from Jack Welch?

The first lesson is to face reality. Welch insisted that leaders must see the world as it is, not as they wish it to be. Most companies fail because they ignore the truth for too long.

The second lesson is to be number one or number two, or fix the business. Focus is a form of power. A company that tries to do everything often does nothing well.

The third lesson is that candor beats comfort. Welch hated politeness that hides problems. He wanted honest feedback, and he gave it.

And the final lesson is a warning. Welch's methods built a giant, but also a culture obsessed with quarterly numbers. A company must be strong not only today, but for the next hundred years.
