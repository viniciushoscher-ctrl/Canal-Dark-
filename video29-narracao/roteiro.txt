### 00_hook
Eighteen ninety-four. In a town outside Chicago, a model company town, with brick houses, a library, a park, and a theater, thousands of workers walk off the job. Their wages have been cut by a quarter. But the rents they pay to their own boss have not been reduced by a dollar. Children are hungry. A delegation goes to talk to the boss, and he refuses to even listen.

The strike spreads across the country. Railroads stop. President Grover Cleveland sends federal troops to Chicago, and thirty people die in the streets. A national union leader named Eugene Debs is sent to prison.

The boss is a soft-spoken, elegant man who thought he was a benefactor. He built the most beautiful factory town in America. He invented the luxury train car, and made his fortune. His name is George Pullman. And this is the story of how the man who built a perfect town started a national crisis, and how his fear of his own workers followed him to the grave.

### 01_cap1
George Mortimer Pullman was born on March third, eighteen thirty-one, in Brocton, New York. His father was a carpenter and house mover, who specialized in lifting buildings off their foundations. At fourteen, George left school to work in a country store. Two years later, he joined his brother as an apprentice cabinetmaker.

When the Erie Canal was widened in eighteen fifty-four, the state needed contractors to raise buildings along the route. Young George, then twenty-three, won contracts to lift warehouses, hotels, and houses without disturbing the people inside. He was good at it, and made a name for himself.

In eighteen fifty-five, he moved to Chicago, a muddy city that was sinking into its swamp. The city decided to raise its streets by several feet, to install sewers. Pullman became a star. He directed hundreds of men, using jackscrews to lift whole buildings, including the Tremont Hotel, a six-story brick structure with guests still inside.

### 02_cap2
But Pullman's mind was on another idea. As a young man, he had traveled on an overnight train from Buffalo to Westfield, New York. The seats were hard, the air was foul, and he could not sleep. He decided that travelers deserved better.

In eighteen fifty-eight, he persuaded a railroad to let him convert two old coaches into sleeping cars. He added hinged upper berths and folding seats that turned into beds. They were an instant hit, and passengers loved them. But the first cars were crude.

In eighteen sixty-four, he built the Pioneer, a car that was wider, taller, and more luxurious than any other. It cost twenty thousand dollars, almost ten times what a normal car cost. It had plush carpets, polished wood, and fine upholstery. Railroads hesitated.

Then came a tragic stroke of luck. In April eighteen sixty-five, President Abraham Lincoln was assassinated, and his funeral train traveled to Illinois. The Pioneer was added to the train because it was the finest car in the country. Pullman's car became famous overnight.

### 03_cap3
He was a master of detail. Every curtain, every mirror, and every piece of silver on a Pullman car followed his designs. He rode his own trains, inspected the linen, and sent back dishes that did not meet his standards. The cars became rolling hotels, and ordinary travelers talked about them like palaces.

Pullman created the Pullman Palace Car Company in eighteen sixty-seven. Unlike other makers, he did not sell his cars to railroads. He leased them, with his own conductors and staff. Passengers paid extra for the comfort, and Pullman earned the revenue. He put his name on the cars, and a Pullman journey became a mark of status.

His next stroke was the dining car, then the parlor car, and the vestibuled car, which allowed passengers to walk between cars. He insisted on perfect service. He hired former slaves and Black men from the South as porters. They were reliable, polite, and paid very little. They worked twenty hours a day, and relied on tips. For decades, it was the largest employer of Black men in America.

By the eighteen seventies, Pullman cars were on nearly every major railroad. His company controlled the sleeping car business, and earned millions. He lived in a mansion in Chicago, and entertained presidents.

### 04_cap4
In eighteen eighty, Pullman made his most ambitious move. He decided to build a perfect town for his workers, thirteen miles south of Chicago, on the shore of Lake Calumet. He hired architect Solon Beman and landscape designer Nathan Barrett. The town, named Pullman, was spotless. It had brick row houses with gas and running water, tree-lined streets, a hotel, a market, a library, a church, and a theater.

Pullman said that clean, beautiful surroundings would make better workers. And better workers would make better cars. He believed that people were improved by a good environment. There were no taverns, because he did not want his workers to drink.

But there was a catch. The company owned everything. The rent was higher than in nearby towns, and the water and gas cost more. Workers had to live in Pullman, if they wanted to work for him. Rents were deducted directly from wages. The town was admired as a model of enlightened capitalism. But workers resented living under a landlord's eye.

### 05_cap5
The town drew visitors from around the world, and newspapers praised Pullman as an enlightened employer. A British visitor called it the most perfect town in the world. Pullman believed that gratitude would keep his workers loyal.

In eighteen ninety-three, a financial panic struck, and orders collapsed. Pullman cut wages by an average of twenty-five percent, and laid off thousands. But he refused to lower rents. Many workers took home almost nothing after paying the company. One worker wrote that he was paid so little, he could not afford to eat.

A committee of workers asked to talk to Pullman. He refused, and fired some of them. On May eleventh, eighteen ninety-four, about four thousand workers went on strike. They joined the American Railway Union, led by Eugene Debs.

Debs organized a boycott. Railroad workers refused to handle Pullman cars. Within days, rail traffic across the country was paralyzed. The railroads asked the federal government for help. Attorney General Richard Olney obtained a court order, and President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago. Riots erupted. Dozens died. Debs was jailed for six months.

### 06_cap6
The strike was crushed, but the public was outraged by Pullman's behavior. A federal commission found that the company's paternalism was to blame, and that he should have negotiated. In eighteen ninety-eight, the Illinois Supreme Court ordered the company to sell off the town.

Pullman grew isolated. He received threats, and withdrew from public life. He died on October nineteenth, eighteen ninety-seven, of a heart attack, at age sixty-six. He feared that radicals would steal his body from the grave. So his family buried him in a steel-lined vault, covered with tons of concrete and railroad ties.

Pullman's company survived for decades. The Pullman porters later formed the first Black-led labor union, the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, which became a key force in the civil rights movement. The town of Pullman is now a national monument.

### 07_lesson
George Pullman's story holds two lessons. The first is that good intentions are not enough. Pullman believed he was a benefactor, but he never asked his workers what they wanted. A paternal boss who controls housing, wages, and rent holds too much power.

The second is that a service can be a status symbol. Pullman did not sell a bed; he sold luxury, and a way to travel. When your customers feel they are buying prestige, they will pay far more.
