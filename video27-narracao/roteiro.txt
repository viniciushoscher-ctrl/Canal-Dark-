### 00_hook
Eighteen sixty-eight. In a New York courtroom, a rough old man with a Bible in his coat pocket is questioned by lawyers. He is a pious Methodist who prays every morning. He founded a theological seminary. He gives money to churches. And he is also the most cunning stock manipulator on Wall Street, a man known as the Speculator, who made his fortune by cheating other speculators.

The lawyers ask him about a battle for control of the Erie Railroad, a fight against the richest man in America, Cornelius Vanderbilt. In that battle, he and two partners, Jay Gould and Jim Fisk, secretly printed new shares of stock to flood the market, and drove Vanderbilt to the brink of ruin. The old man smiles. He knows that he is on the right side of the law. Or at least, the side nobody can prove.

His name is Daniel Drew. He started as a cattle drover with a trick for making his cattle look heavier. He ended up one of the richest men in New York, and died penniless. This is the story of the man who invented stock watering.

### 01_cap1
Daniel Drew was born on July twenty-ninth, seventeen ninety-seven, in Carmel, New York, in the countryside north of the city. His father was a poor farmer, who died when Daniel was a boy. The family lost its farm, and Daniel went to work early. He never learned to read or write well, and for the rest of his life, he signed his name with difficulty.

In the War of Eighteen Twelve, he served briefly in the militia, and never saw real combat. When he returned, he found work as a cattle drover. In those days, farmers in upstate New York drove their cattle on foot to the markets of New York City, and he took over the business.

It was a hard life, on the road for weeks. But Drew had an eye for a deal. He bought cattle cheap in the countryside and sold them at a profit in the city. He had one more talent, and it would later make him famous.

### 02_cap2
Drew developed a trick. Cattle were sold by weight. Before arriving in the city, he would feed his cattle salt, so that they were thirsty. Then he let them drink huge amounts of water just before weighing. A cow could drink dozens of pounds of water. When it was weighed, the buyer paid for water as if it was meat.

This was called watering stock. Later, on Wall Street, Drew used the phrase for something else: issuing new shares of stock without any real assets behind them, to dilute the value. It was the same idea, applied to paper.

By the eighteen thirties, Drew was a successful cattleman. He bought a tavern in New York City, the Bull's Head, a gathering place for cattle drovers. He also opened a steamboat line on the Hudson River, in competition with Cornelius Vanderbilt.

### 03_cap3
The steamboat war was fierce. Drew and Vanderbilt slashed fares, and they fought for passengers. At one point, tickets from New York to Albany were cut to a quarter, and then to nothing. But Vanderbilt was too strong. In the end, the two men made a truce. Drew sold his boats to a larger company, and moved into banking and stocks.

He opened a brokerage firm on Wall Street called Drew, Robinson and Company, in eighteen forty-six. He bought into the Erie Railroad, and in eighteen fifty-two, he became a director and its treasurer. Drew used his position to manipulate the stock. He lent the railroad money, and took shares as collateral. Then he sold the shares short, and pushed the price down.

People called him the Great Bear of Wall Street. He had no morals about money. He would spread false rumors, buy and sell on inside information, and take secret positions. He is credited with one of the greatest insider schemes of the age.

### 04_cap4
At the same time, Drew was deeply religious. He attended Methodist camp meetings. He prayed publicly. He funded a Methodist church in lower Manhattan, and in eighteen sixty-seven, he gave a large donation to found the Drew Theological Seminary, in Madison, New Jersey, which later became Drew University.

Critics saw him as a hypocrite. Wall Street joked about him, saying that if you were to get a seat at his table, it would cost you everything you owned. He would pray with one hand, and pick your pocket with the other.

He was also superstitious. He disliked risk in his personal life, and he kept a small pocket Bible, which he read in the carriage on the way to the stock exchange. He would then walk into the exchange and sell a stock short with a clear conscience.

Drew himself was proud of his cunning. He liked to tell the story of how he walked into any game with a preacher's solemn face. He said that speculation was a business of nerve, and that he never liked to lose.

In the eighteen sixties, the Erie Railroad became his playground. And then Cornelius Vanderbilt, who had built the New York Central, decided to take it over.

### 05_cap5
The Erie War began in eighteen sixty-eight. Vanderbilt, then seventy-four, tried to corner Erie shares to take control of the railroad. Drew, Fisk, and Gould were determined to stop him. So they did something dramatic. They issued fifty thousand new shares by converting bonds, secretly, and dumped them on the market. Vanderbilt kept buying, thinking he was buying a controlling share, but he was only buying paper.

A New York judge ordered them to stop. Drew and his partners fled to New Jersey, with six million dollars in cash packed in suitcases, and set up a headquarters in a Jersey City hotel. They were protected by armed guards and cannons. They even tried to bribe members of the New York legislature to legalize their actions.

Vanderbilt eventually realized he had been outplayed. He settled, and the Erie Ring kept the money. For Drew, it was a great victory, but also the beginning of a long decline, when his partners Gould and Fisk outsmarted him as well.

### 06_cap6
Drew had made one fatal mistake. He had trusted his younger partners. Jay Gould was a quiet, brilliant manipulator, thirty years younger. Jim Fisk was a showman with a flair for publicity. They learned everything from the old man, and then took it from him.

In eighteen sixty-nine, Gould and Fisk turned on Drew. In another battle for Erie, they squeezed him, and he lost heavily. In the panic of eighteen seventy-three, Drew lost most of what remained. His brokerage firm collapsed, and in eighteen seventy-six, he declared bankruptcy. He had owed more than a million dollars.

The old man spent his last years in a small house, in the care of his son. He lived on a small allowance. He prayed, and he told friends that everything he had lost would be returned to him in heaven. He died on September eighteenth, eighteen seventy-nine, at age eighty-two. He was almost penniless.

Drew's story entered folklore. His cattle trick gave Wall Street a term, watered stock, which remains in use. And his pious manner was a model for later tycoons, who combined spirituality with ruthless business.

### 07_lesson
Daniel Drew's story holds two lessons. The first is that tricks work until someone plays a better one. Drew fooled buyers and rivals for decades, but he was fooled in the end by younger, more ruthless partners.

The second is that a fortune built on deception does not last. He made millions by taking advantage of others, and he died with nothing. His name survives in a university, a gift from the best years of his life, which is perhaps the best investment he ever made.
